    24	
    25	The field's first belief was that recommending people is recommending items. Score how much the viewer likes the candidate, then rank.
    26	
    27	The first evidence against that belief was diagnostic, not algorithmic.    155	## The arc in one sentence
   156	
   157	The field moved from *score the viewer*, to *score both directions*, to *clear the market under capacity*, to *change the product rules too*, to *stop measuring matches and start measuring retention*, and finally in 2026 to a real dating field experiment that improved congestion-adjusted matching while total dates fell and conversation did not move — and the survey that assembled this arc learned, three separate times, that a retrieval notebook is a witness rather than an oracle.
   158	
   159	## Standing direction
   160	
   161	1. Add a reciprocal term that conditions on the other side's remaining reply capacity. Tinder reports having A/B tested this shape as P(Match) against P(Like).
   162	2. Put an explicit allocation layer above the score. Start with LiJAR-style soft boost and penalty behind a relevance floor, because it is the safest deployed pattern. Treat exposure-constrained deferred acceptance as the dating-native target design.
   163	3. Keep like caps, batch size, initiation rules, and scarce signals inside the same design space as the model.
   164	4. Replace swipe-rate north stars with match distribution, wasted-like rate, conversations, and two-sided retention. Report effects separately for sender-constrained, receiver-constrained, and balanced segments.
   165	5. Do not test any of the above on a user-split A/B. Use geographic, cluster, or two-sided randomization.

    35	## Design Patterns
    36	
    37	1. **Reciprocal scoring.** Score a recommendation by the probability that both parties like and respond to each other: Tu et al., “Online Dating Recommendations: Matching Markets and Learning Preferences,” SocialRecSys 2014, improved success rates by 46.84% for male suitors versus one-sided recommendation.
    38	
    39	2. **Forecast-based capacity redistribution.** Forecast incoming demand and softly penalize oversubscribed profiles while boosting underserved ones behind a relevance floor: Borisyuk et al., “LiJAR: A System for Job Application Redistribution towards Efficient Career Marketplace,” KDD 2017, shifted applications +6.5% toward underserved jobs, −8.7% away from overserved jobs, and increased entropy 12%.
    40	
    41	3. **Expected-load caps.** Constrain expected likes or dates—not merely the number of matches—using a serving-time allocation mechanism: Sekiya et al., “Integrating Predictive Models into Two-Sided Recommendations: A Matching-Theoretic Approach,” arXiv 2026, improved simulated effective dates from 0.0584 to 0.0623 over the current recommender and validated the effect with a geographic field experiment.
    42	
    43	4. **Market-clearing redistribution.** Use equilibrium prices or unmatched probabilities to reduce visibility for congested users and increase it for underexposed users: Tomita et al., “Fast and Examination-agnostic Reciprocal Recommendation in Matching Markets,” RecSys 2023, reduced match Gini from 0.387 to 0.102 on dating data.
    44	
    45	5. **Retention-optimized allocation.** Optimize the joint retention gain of the viewer and recipient, rather than raw match probability or equal exposure: Kishimoto et al., “Beyond Match Maximization and Fairness: Retention-Optimized Two-Sided Matching,” ICLR 2026, achieved higher retention using approximately 70% of Max Match’s volume.
    46	
    47	6. **Curated, capacity-constrained menus.** Select each user’s displayed assortment while accounting for both-side backlogs and collision risk: Ashlagi et al., “Assortment Planning for Two-Sided Sequential Matching Markets,” Operations Research 2022, achieved at least one-third of the LP upper bound in simulations while explicitly bounding supplier overexposure.
    48	
    49	7. **Interference-aware experimentation.** Randomize by regions, clusters, time blocks, or both sides because a treatment changes the shared pool available to controls: Holtz et al., “Reducing Interference Bias in Online Marketplace Experiments Using Cluster Randomization: Evidence from a Pricing Meta-experiment on Airbnb,” Management Science 2025, measured 19.76% bias from individual-level randomization.
    50	
    51	These adjacent-marketplace results require caution: music, Airbnb, jobs, listings, and creator-content systems generally have unlimited or renewable supply-side capacity. Their mechanisms transfer only after replacing “item availability” with a person’s scarce reply bandwidth.
    52	
    53	## Most Fundamental Methods
    54	
    55	1. **Reciprocal recommendation:** Pizzato et al., “Recommending People to People: The Nature of Reciprocal Recommenders with a Case Study in Online Dating,” User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction 2013, introduced harmonic-mean bilateral compatibility scoring.
    56	
    57	2. **Stable matching:** Gale and Shapley, “College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage,” American Mathematical Monthly 1962, introduced deferred acceptance, the foundational matching mechanism repeatedly adapted to reciprocal recommendation.
    58	
    59	3. **Transferable-utility matching:** Choo and Siow, “Who Marries Whom and Why,” Journal of Political Economy 2006, introduced the equilibrium framework underlying congestion pricing and market-clearing recommenders.
    60	
    61	4. **Exposure fairness:** Singh and Joachims, “Fairness of Exposure in Rankings,” KDD 2018, established optimization over exposure allocations rather than relevance alone.
    62	
    63	5. **Interference-aware experimentation:** Johari et al., “Experimental Design in Two-Sided Platforms: An Analysis of Bias,” Management Science 2022, formalized why single-sided experiments mismeasure marketplace interventions and introduced two-sided randomization estimators.

    65	## Recommendations
    66	
    67	1. **Build first: a reciprocal, expected-load capacity reranker.** Keep the existing candidate generator and add a serving-layer score combining bilateral match probability with receiver-side expected incoming likes/dates, remaining reply bandwidth, and recent backlog. Start with LiJAR-style soft boost/penalty and ECDA-style expected-load caps. This is the most practical combination of production evidence and dating-native validity. ECDA is especially compelling because it improved effective dates in a real dating platform; LiJAR supplies the safer incremental deployment pattern.
    68	
    69	2. **Optimize and measure whole-market health.** Make the primary objective a retention-weighted marketplace outcome: two-sided retention and conversations, subject to maintaining total matches. Track total matches, conversations per match, share of users with at least one match, received-like/match Gini, wasted likes, reply latency, and churn by desirability cohort. Do not rely on CTR, swipe-right rate, or single-viewer CVR.
    70	
    71	   Evaluate with geographic or graph-cluster randomization, switchbacks, or two-sided randomization—not ordinary user-level A/B tests. Report treatment effects separately for sender-constrained, receiver-constrained, and balanced segments. Use a short-term wasted-like metric only as a validated surrogate for long-term retention; Lyft’s region-split/surrogacy framework is a useful template.
    72	
    73	3. **Avoid optimizing raw reciprocal relevance alone.** ReSeq, TinVec, Smart Photos, and similar unilateral or unconstrained reciprocal rankers may improve local match probability while concentrating exposure on already-desirable users. Also avoid hard global caps as the first intervention: congestion theory supports limits, but dating-native evidence warns that blunt caps can reduce match volume. Prefer soft, personalized throttling with relevance floors and explicit rollback guardrails.

    75	## Anti-Patterns
    76	
    77	- **Popularity/CTR optimization without receiver capacity:** reinforces rich-get-richer exposure and wastes likes.
    78	- **Unconstrained reciprocal scoring:** mutual-interest prediction improves pair quality but can still funnel demand toward the same “superstar” users.
    79	- **Uniform exposure equality:** may destroy total utility; Lorenz-dominance work shows equality can drive everyone’s utility toward zero.
    80	- **Hard, platform-wide like caps:** can reduce useful search and match volume; use targeted, adaptive limits instead.
    81	- **Static offline fairness metrics as the objective:** exposure parity is not equivalent to retention, conversations, or market health.
    82	- **Individual-level A/B testing:** creates interference because treated users consume the same recipients’ reply capacity as controls.

    28	
    29	- Calibrate view→like→like-back→conversation separately by side and report reciprocal coverage, following Yang et al., *Revisiting Reciprocal Recommender Systems: Metrics, Formulation, and Method*, KDD 2024.

     8	
     9	1. **Reciprocal scoring, not viewer-only relevance.** Fuse P(like | viewer, candidate) with P(like-back | candidate, viewer, capacity).      6	## Summary
     7	Extended (journal) version of a RecSys'24 paper on fairness in reciprocal recommender systems (RRS) for two-sided matching platforms like online dating. The authors observe that maximizing expected matches (Social Welfare, SW) concentrates recommendation exposure on popular users who cannot reciprocate everyone's interest, producing unfair "opportunity" distributions. They adapt envy-freeness from fair-division theory to define "double envy-freeness" (both sides simultaneously envy-free over their recommendation opportunities) for RRS, and propose maximizing Nash Social Welfare (NSW, the product of expected-match utilities) via an alternating Frank-Wolfe algorithm as a scalable, near-envy-free alternative to SW maximization. A Sinkhorn-based fast approximate algorithm adds scalability, plus a tunable α-SW method interpolating between SW and NSW. On synthetic data and two real dating datasets (a Japanese online dating platform, and the public Speed Dating Experiment dataset), NSW achieves near-zero envy while keeping competitive match counts, at some cost in total matches relative to pure SW maximization.
     8	
     9	## Method
    10	Two-sided matching market: n agents on the left (N), m agents on the right (M). Each pair (a_i, b_j) has estimated one-directional preference probabilities p̂1(i,j) and p̂2(j,i). Recommendations are doubly stochastic matrices A_i (agent a_i's ranking distribution over M) and B_j (agent b_j's ranking distribution over N). Using a position-based examination model e(k) (probability of examining the k-th ranked candidate), the probability a_i applies to b_j is p̂1(i,j)·Σ_k e(k)A_i(j,k); a match occurs only if **both** sides apply — a genuine mutual-application reciprocal mechanism, distinct from prior work that used one-sided relevance or assumed symmetric preferences. Utility U_i(A,B) = expected number of matches for agent i; Social Welfare SW(A,B) = Σ utilities (total expected matches). Fairness: an agent envies another same-side agent if it would prefer that agent's "opportunity" (the recommendation rows it receives from the other side) to its own; a policy is "double envy-free" if no agent envies another on either side. Key algorithms: (1) **SW method** — alternating maximization of total expected matches via Frank-Wolfe (non-convex, converges to a local optimum); (2) **NSW method** — alternating maximization of the Nash Social Welfare (product of utilities) via Frank-Wolfe on log NSW, proven (Theorem 5.2, building on Saito & Joachims 2022) to be approximately doubly envy-free when both sides are NSW-best; (3) **α-SW** — a tunable interpolation W^α_i(A,B) = (1/α)ΣU_i(A,B)^α that recovers SW at α=1 and converges to NSW as α→0; (4) a **Sinkhorn-algorithm** fast approximate solver (entropy-regularized optimal transport) replacing the LP subproblems, giving large scalability gains (GPU-parallelizable; ~200s vs >1,000s for LP at n=50–75, and tractable up to n=300 where LP becomes infeasible).

     6	## Summary
     7	Two-sided matching platforms (online dating, recruiting) that optimize for total match count concentrate matches on a small set of already-popular users while leaving many others under-matched and prone to churn; naive fairness-of-exposure objectives don't reliably fix this either, since exposure equalized in proportion to merit doesn't necessarily track what actually keeps a given user on the platform. The paper formally reframes the objective as maximizing *user retention* directly (not match count, not an axiomatic fairness criterion), and introduces MRet (Matching for Retention), a dynamic learning-to-rank algorithm that scores each candidate recommendation by the sum of the retention-probability gain to the receiving user and the retention-probability gain to the recommended user, using a real per-user retention curve learned from profile + interaction history. Because exactly maximizing this joint-gain objective is NP-hard, MRet uses a Jensen's-inequality lower bound plus a concavity-based linear bound to reduce the problem to a simple per-item score computable via argsort in O(N log N). On both synthetic data and real interaction data from a large Japanese online dating platform, MRet achieves higher user retention than max-match, uniform, and fairness-of-exposure (FairCo) baselines, using substantially fewer total matches than a match-maximizing policy.
     8	
     9	## Method
    10	MRet models a per-user retention function f(x, m) — the probability a user x stays on the platform given cumulative matches m — learned via regression on user context and interaction history, under the assumption that f is concave in m (diminishing retention return to additional matches; empirically supported by the platform's real login-continuation-vs-match-count curve). At each arrival of a user x, the ideal ranking σ* maximizes the *joint* retention-probability gain: the receiver x's own gain from the new matches, plus the sum, over the K recommended candidates y, of each candidate's own retention gain from being shown to x (weighted by rank-position visibility α_k). This exact objective (Eq. 9) is NP-hard to optimize directly. MRet's trick: (1) a Jensen-inequality lower bound decomposes the receiver-side concave gain into a sum over positions; (2) a concavity-based linear bound converts each candidate's non-linear retention gain into a term linear in the position weight α_k. Combining both yields a per-candidate additive Score(y) (Eq. 13) such that ranking candidates by Score(y) via a simple argsort provably maximizes a lower bound on the true joint-retention objective (proved via the rearrangement inequality). This reduces an NP-hard combinatorial ranking problem to individual score computation, at the same computational cost as standard learning-to-rank.

    20	**Synthetic experiments:** As the time horizon τ increases, Max Match accumulates by far the most total matches, Uniform the fewest, yet Uniform and Max Match achieve almost the same user retention rate — demonstrating that raw match volume does not track retention.      8	## Summary
     9	On two-sided dating/matching platforms, a naive recommender that ranks receivers purely by predicted "dating rate" concentrates recommendations on a small subset of highly responsive, popular receivers, generating severe congestion that overstates true efficiency. The paper models recommendation as a many-to-many matching problem and designs *integrators* that map predicted login, like, and reciprocation ("relike") probabilities into a recommendation matrix under proposer-side cognitive-capacity constraints. It introduces *effective dates*, a congestion-discounted outcome metric, and proposes *Exposure-Constrained Deferred Acceptance* (ECDA), which caps receiver-side exposure by expected number of likes or dates rather than by headcount. Using production-grade predictive models and real interaction data from CoupLink (a Japanese dating platform with 1.5M+ cumulative users), calibrated simulations show ECDA increases effective dates and receiver-side dating probability despite reducing total (raw) dates. A large-scale geographic field experiment (difference-in-differences across Kanto vs. Kansai-Tokai) confirms the effect operates in a live platform, improving early-stage matching equity/efficiency without degrading downstream messaging engagement once extreme-tail congestion is accounted for.

    11	## Method
    12	- **Setting:** proposers `i ∈ [I]` (predominantly male on CoupLink) and receivers `j ∈ [J]` (predominantly female); a recommendation matrix `M = (M_ij) ∈ [0,1]` gives the probability receiver `j` is recommended to proposer `i`. Each proposer has a *cognitive capacity* `c_i` capping how many receivers he reviews (`Σ_j M_ij ≤ c_i`), treated as a hard constraint on all candidate integrators.
    13	- **Date-formation funnel** (Figure 1): recommendation (prob. `M_ij`) → proposer logs in (prob. `λ_i`) → proposer sends a like (prob. `α_ij`, the *like rate*) → receiver logs in (prob. `λ_j`) → receiver sends a *relike* (prob. `β_ij`, the *relike rate*) → a *date* is formed. The overall probability of a date is `δ_ij M_ij`, where `δ_ij := λ_i α_ij λ_j β_ij` is the *dating rate*.
    14	- **Performance metrics:** (1) *Average Dates* = `(1/I) Σ δ_ij M_ij`; (2) *Average Effective Dates* — discounts a proposer-receiver pair's contribution by the receiver's overall congestion `μ_j = Σ_k δ_kj M_kj` via the factor `(1 - e^{-μ_j})/μ_j`, derived under the assumption that a receiver who forms multiple dates picks exactly one uniformly at random to pursue each day (used only as a transparent congestion discount, not a behavioral claim); (3) *Dating Probability* (per proposer/receiver) — the extensive-margin probability of forming at least one date.
    15	- **Rank-order lists (ROLs):** either "sorting by like rates" (myopic — ranks candidates only by the proposer's/receiver's own action probability) or "sorting by dating rates" (accounts for both sides' full funnel, including activity/responsiveness).
    16	- **Integrators compared:**
    17	  - **One-sided** — greedily recommends each proposer his top-`c_i` ROL entries with no receiver-side control (structurally close to CoupLink's actual production recommender).
    18	  - **DA (Deferred Acceptance)** — imposes a headcount capacity `q_j` on how many proposers a receiver can be recommend-matched with; standard stable-matching DA. **Theorem 1:** when ROLs are sorted by dating rate, DA is provably equivalent to a fast greedy algorithm (sort all pairs by `δ_ij` descending, match while both sides' capacities allow), making it tractable at platform scale.
    19	  - **ECDA (Exposure-Constrained DA)** — same DA logic, but receiver capacity `q_j` is defined on *expected exposure* rather than headcount: either expected likes received (`Σ_i M_ij λ_i α_ij ≤ q_j`, "like exposure") or expected dates formed (`Σ_i M_ij δ_ij ≤ q_j`, "date exposure"). Requires extending DA to allow fractional proposals/acceptances. **Theorem 2:** also reduces to a fast greedy algorithm when ROLs are dating-rate sorted.

    40	## Limitations
    41	- The paper treats predicted login/like/relike rates as ground-truth primitives for the simulation analysis; prediction error itself is explicitly set aside as a separate, well-studied ML problem, not addressed here.
    42	- The "effective dates" congestion discount relies on an explicit simplifying assumption (a congested receiver picks one date per day uniformly at random) that the authors state is used "solely to obtain a transparent congestion discount," not as a claim about real relationship-formation behavior.
    43	- The fast-greedy equivalence that makes DA/ECDA tractable at scale (Theorems 1–2) holds only when ROLs are sorted by dating rate; like-rate-sorted DA/ECDA are not computationally practical at the ~8,000-proposer empirical-market scale.
    44	- Field-experiment identification rests on only two geographic macro-units (Kanto vs. Kansai-Tokai), so conventional large-cluster asymptotic standard errors are unavailable; the authors explicitly instruct readers to treat the DiD significance stars as suggestive rather than definitive.
    45	- Full-sample realized post-engagement (messaging) outcomes move in the opposite direction from the upstream (predicted/early) gains; this tension is only resolved by excluding the top 0.1% of receiver-days, and the authors state that further improvements in *substantive* match quality (sustained messaging) "may require separate tuning or design changes beyond the ranking algorithm" — i.e., ECDA does not, by itself, solve downstream engagement quality.
    46	- Only the receiver side is exposure-constrained in this paper's implementation; the proposer side (predominantly male on CoupLink) is capacity-constrained only via cognitive capacity `c_i`, not via a symmetric reciprocal-exposure mechanism.

     8	## Summary
     9	Standard completely-randomized A/B tests assume no interference (SUTVA), which is routinely violated in marketplaces where two populations of agents (e.g., buyers and sellers, viewers and content creators) interact strategically and treatment of one unit can spill over onto others. The paper gives the first formal finite-sample analysis of **Multiple Randomization Designs (MRDs)** — experiments where two (or more) populations are independently randomized and a pair/tuple is only "treated" if all its constituent units are — and in particular of **Simple MRDs (SMRDs)**, the leading two-population case. Under a tractable "local interference" assumption (a pair's outcome depends only on its own treatment plus the treated-fraction of its row and column, not the full assignment matrix), they derive unbiased plug-in estimators, exact finite-sample variances, conservative variance estimators, and a design-based finite-population central limit theorem for four causal estimands: average treatment effect (ATE), buyer-side spillover, seller-side spillover, and a "direct effect" purged of spillovers. Simulations of a strategic two-sided marketplace (Nash-equilibrium content creators and advertisers) show that a standard single-sided A/B test can recover the **wrong sign** of a platform intervention's profit effect, while the SMRD-based ATE estimator recovers the correct sign, and that the conservative SMRD test detects a true spillover effect with 99.5% power in their simulated setting.

    11	## Method
    12	- **Setting:** I buyers, J sellers (generalized to any two interacting populations, e.g., creators/advertisers, riders/drivers). Outcome `Y_ij(w)` for pair (i,j) is a potential outcome indexed by the full I×J binary treatment-assignment matrix **w**.
    13	- **Local interference (Assumption 2.4):** `y_ij(w) = y_ij(w')` whenever (a) the pair's own assignment `w_ij` is unchanged, (b) the fraction of treated sellers for buyer i is unchanged, and (c) the fraction of treated buyers for seller j is unchanged. This is weaker than full SUTVA (no interference at all) but far more tractable than unrestricted interference; it is motivated by a worked Nash-equilibrium example (Example 2.5) where strategic content creators and advertisers respond to their own average treatment exposure, producing exactly this structure.
    14	- **Simple Multiple Randomization Design (SMRD, Definition 3.4):** independently select `I_T` of I buyers and `J_T` of J sellers into "eligible" groups; pair (i,j) is treated only if *both* i and j are eligible (`W_ij = T` iff `min(w^B_i, w^S_j) = 1`). This induces **four assignment types** per pair: `cc` (neither eligible), `ib` (buyer-only eligible), `is` (seller-only eligible), `tr` (both eligible/treated) — all four types are mutually comparable pre-treatment (randomization), but not necessarily comparable post-treatment, which is precisely what lets SMRDs detect and measure spillovers (a two-arm single-population experiment cannot).
    15	- **Estimands:** linear combinations of the four type-level population averages `ȳ_γ`, e.g. `τ_ATE = ȳ_tr − ȳ_cc`, buyer-spillover `τ^B_spill = ȳ_ib − ȳ_cc`, seller-spillover `τ^S_spill = ȳ_is − ȳ_cc`, direct effect `τ_direct` (a `[1,-1,-1,1]` contrast that cancels spillovers).
    16	- **Estimators:** the plug-in type-average `Ŷ_γ` (mean outcome within each observed assignment-type bucket) is proven unbiased for `ȳ_γ` (Lemma 4.1/Theorem 4.2) under local interference; linear combinations of these estimate any of the estimands above unbiasedly.
    17	- **Variance:** exact finite-sample covariances of the four type estimators are derived (Theorem 4.3), decomposed into buyer-level, seller-level, and buyer×seller interaction variance components; a conservative (Young's-inequality-based) variance estimator is given (Theorem 4.4/Eq. 15) since the true covariance across types is not point-identifiable without stronger assumptions.
    18	- **Inference:** a design-based finite-population Central Limit Theorem (Theorem 4.8) extending Li & Ding (2017) / Shi & Ding (2022) from single- to multi-population design-based inference, giving a Berry-Esseen-type bound on the Studentized statistic's deviation from normality; enables standard hypothesis tests (e.g., testing for zero spillover) using the conservative variance estimator.

    25	- **Unbiasedness/variance verification (Figures 1–2):** across 10,000 Monte Carlo re-randomizations, the type estimator `Ŷ_cc` is centered exactly on the true population value, and its variance estimator `Σ̂_cc` is centered on the true variance, matching the paper's theorems; the spillover estimator `τ̂^B_spill` is approximately Gaussian as predicted by the CLT, and the conservative variance estimator's distribution is centered above (conservative relative to) the true variance.

    27	- **Headline comparison vs. standard single-randomization (Figure 4):** across 10,000 re-randomizations of each design type, the standard single-sided difference-in-means estimator `τ̂_SRD` is centered clearly on the **negative** side of zero (roughly the −6 to 0 range in the reported distribution, mean noticeably below zero), while the SMRD-based `τ̂_ATE` estimator is centered clearly on the **positive** side (roughly 0 to 9 range) — i.e., in this simulated strategic-complementarity marketplace, the standard single-randomization design usually estimates the **wrong sign** of the platform intervention's effect on total profit, while the SMRD design usually recovers the correct sign.
    28	- **QQ-plot check (Figure 3):** the Studentized spillover statistic tracks the theoretical Gaussian quantiles closely, empirically supporting the CLT (Theorem 4.8).
    29	- No point estimates of real-platform metrics (e.g., dollar revenue, match rate) are reported anywhere in the paper — all quantitative results are simulation-based verification of statistical properties, not applied business results.

    31	## Limitations
    32	- **Sparsity (Remark 4.9, self-flagged):** the CLT's error bound requires the effective "signal density" `C_β̄` to be small relative to `{I⁻¹+J⁻¹}⁻¹`; with sparse potential outcomes (many zero entries, common in pairwise buyer-seller interaction data), the theorem's guarantees can require the population to be very large relative to sparsity — the authors explicitly call generalizing to heavy-tailed/sparse settings "an important direction for future work."
    33	- The local interference assumption is **not testable** within an SMRD itself — the paper proves that "differences between expected outcomes for the comparison groups can always be rationalized in a way that is consistent with local interference" under the SMRD's four assignment types; testing local interference requires a richer design (illustrated in Section 6/eq. 17 extensions).
    34	- **No unbiased covariance estimator** exists across assignment types without further assumptions on potential outcomes — only a conservative upper bound is available (an intrinsic limitation of design-based inference here, analogous to the classical Neyman variance-bound issue for single-population RCTs).
    35	- All empirical validation is **synthetic simulation**, not real platform data; the paper is explicitly a statistical-theory contribution with simulation-based validation of correctness rather than a field experiment or applied deployment study.
    36	- The core SMRD design only handles two populations and a specific (multiplicative "AND"-style) treatment-assignment rule; more general MRDs (three+ populations, non-binary/scored eligibility, clustered variants) are sketched as extensions in Section 6 but not analyzed with the same rigor.

    60	## Project Relevance
    61	Addresses **Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics and experimentation under interference)** squarely and by name — this is a methods paper for exactly the "interference-aware A/B testing" problem the project's framing calls out. If the dating app wants to test a change to exposure allocation, like limits, or reciprocal scoring, a standard single-sided randomized test risks exactly the failure mode this paper demonstrates empirically: getting the *sign* of the platform-level effect wrong because of cross-side strategic spillovers (e.g., a change that looks good in a viewer-only A/B test could actually be reducing total matches once provider-side reply-capacity effects are accounted for). The four-assignment-type SMRD design (independently randomize both the "sender" and "receiver" side) is a directly implementable recipe for measuring both direct effects and cross-side spillovers of a dating-app intervention, which is exactly what's needed to validate changes to capacity-aware allocation or market-design levers without contaminated estimates.

    63	**Disanalogy to flag:** none regarding capacity per se — this is a domain-agnostic statistical framework, not tied to any particular notion of scarce reply capacity; it applies equally to unlimited-capacity marketplaces (e.g., generic buyer-seller settings) and scarce-capacity ones like dating. The relevant caveat is method-fit rather than domain-fit: the local interference assumption (outcome depends only on own treatment + row/column treated-fraction) is a simplifying approximation that may not fully capture richer strategic dynamics in a reciprocal dating market (e.g., a user's behavior might depend on *which specific* other users were treated, not just the treated fraction), and this is not testable within the basic SMRD design itself.

Act 8 is not really another turning point. It is an audit appendix that restates corrections and unresolved disputes. That is useful, but it should be framed as an epilogue rather than an act in the field’s causal development.

The major missed turning point is the move into dating-native field experimentation before 2026. The directly read ECDA paper cites Chen, Hsieh and Lin’s 2023 *Reducing Recommendation Inequality via Two-Sided Matching: A Field Experiment of Online Dating*. Meanwhile, Act 8 says Rios–Saban–Zheng may be the largest dating field result found but leaves it outside the research arc. These belong at the end of Act 2 or in a short act before the experimentation section:

> Offline market-clearing and fairness proposals were followed by attempts to change allocation on live dating platforms; 2026 ECDA then added explicit expected-load constraints and downstream messaging measurement.

That transition matters more to the central thesis than the Mashayekhi survey-year correction or several of Act 8’s tool-history details. It determines whether 2026 was truly the first field validation or instead the point where an existing field-experiment line became more capacity-explicit and measured a longer outcome chain. The Chen paper—and Rios if its identity can be resolved—must be read directly before making the “first/only field experiment” claim.
