## Summary
This is a design/vision paper from CyberAgent describing an in-progress project at **Tapple**, a Japanese online dating platform with over 7 million registered users, to replace the platform's conventional reciprocal-recommender fusion approach with a **matching-theory-based recommender system (MTRS)** grounded in the Choo & Siow (2006) transferable-utility (TU) matching model. The core motivation is exactly the "superstar" problem: standard reciprocal recommenders fuse one-sided preference scores (e.g., via harmonic/arithmetic/geometric mean) but ignore the fact that highly desirable users receive an overwhelming volume of likes relative to their capacity to respond, degrading overall user experience. Their proposed reciprocal score is computed from an equilibrium matching model that jointly incorporates preferences *and* capacities, and they discuss the computational scaling problem (their exact formulation is O(|X||Y|) at Tapple's user scale) and approximation strategies (locality-sensitive hashing, approximate nearest-neighbor search) to make it tractable. The paper reports no offline or online evaluation results — it describes the method and implementation plan, plus a discussion of open future directions (bandit algorithms, fairness via matching-with-constraints, and the difficulty of running clean online experiments in an interference-heavy two-sided market).

## Method
Standard reciprocal recommender systems (RRS) predict unilateral preference scores p_x,y (man x's preference for woman y) and p_y,x via matrix factorization on historical implicit feedback ("likes"/"thanks"), then fuse them into a reciprocal score via an aggregation function φ(p_x,y, p_y,x) — common choices in the literature being harmonic mean (Pizzato et al. 2010), arithmetic mean, geometric mean, or learned weighted combinations — and rank candidates by this score, **without accounting for either side's finite capacity to respond**. The paper's MTRS instead adopts the **Choo & Siow (2006) transferable-utility (TU) matching model**: it treats matching as a decentralized equilibrium market where an implicit "transfer" τ_x,y between matched pairs adjusts until both sides' probabilistic demand for each other coincides. Under a standard-Gumbel-distributed idiosyncratic-preference-error assumption (as in Choo & Siow 2006), the equilibrium reciprocal score has the closed form: **μ_x,y = μ_y,x = exp((p_x,y + p_y,x)/2) · √(μ_x,0 · μ_y,0)**, where μ_x,0 and μ_y,0 are the equilibrium probabilities that x (resp. y) remains unmatched, subject to the constraint that each user's match probabilities across all candidates plus staying unmatched sum to 1. Critically, μ_x,0/μ_y,0 act as an **implicit, learned capacity/saturation discount**: as a "superstar" user's aggregate demand rises, their equilibrium probability of staying available to any one specific candidate falls, automatically discounting how strongly they are recommended to any single person — this is the mechanism that redistributes exposure away from over-liked users without a hand-tuned cap. The equilibrium (μ_x,0, μ_y,0 for all users) is computed via the **Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure (IPFP)** (Decker et al. 2013; Galichon & Salanié 2021), a closed-form iterative update. Because full computation is O(|X||Y|(d+T)) (d = MF embedding dimension, T = IPFP iterations) — prohibitive at Tapple's million-user scale — the authors propose approximating the per-step summations using **locality-sensitive hashing** (Gionis et al. 1999) and **approximate nearest-neighbor search** (Wang et al. 2015), targeting O(|X|+|Y|) per iteration.

## Limitations
The authors explicitly flag: (1) **scalability is the critical unresolved challenge** — the exact TU-matching computation is O(|X||Y|(d+T)), "prohibitively large" at Tapple's scale, and even their proposed LSH/ANN approximation is presented as an in-progress mitigation, not a solved/validated one; (2) maintaining the full pairwise reciprocal-score matrix is also flagged as space-consuming given feedback sparsity (N ≪ |X|+|Y|); (3) **online experimentation is fundamentally hard** in this setting because standard A/B testing assumes SUTVA (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption), which is violated when users interact with each other through the platform — they note structural estimation may be needed instead, and list this as future work rather than a solved problem; (4) fairness and bandit-based exploration/exploitation are both flagged as important but unaddressed future directions, not part of the current system.
