## Summary
Standard network-effects theory predicts that bigger platforms (more candidates to choose from) should always win — yet eHarmony restricts users to a limited number of candidate profiles and still charges roughly 25% more than Match.com, which offers unrestricted browsing. The paper resolves this puzzle by deriving indirect network effects from matching-market micro-foundations rather than assuming them to be linear and positive: showing a user more candidates creates a positive "choice effect" (more likely to see someone attractive) but also a negative "same-side competition effect" (the candidates you like also have more options, so are less likely to pick you back). Because these two forces trade off differently depending on an agent's private outside-option value (utility from staying alone), users with low outside options are hurt more by competition than helped by choice, so they self-select into restricted-choice platforms — letting such platforms coexist profitably, and charge a premium, alongside unrestricted-choice competitors.

## Method
A stylized one-period game-theoretic model of a symmetric two-sided (heterosexual) dating market. Each agent has a private outside-option value 𝔞 ~ G(𝔞) on [0,1] (utility from remaining unmatched) and meets N candidates from the other side; how much agent i likes each candidate is drawn independently and subjectively as Λ ~ G(Λ) on [0,1] (purely "horizontal" preferences — no shared vertical quality scale). Each agent makes at most one offer, to the most-liked candidate above their own 𝔞; a match forms only if offers are mutually reciprocated. Key mechanical result (Lemma 1): the probability of being rejected by a random candidate is Pr(rej|N) = N/(N+1), strictly increasing in N — this is the "competition effect." The expected value of a successful match is non-decreasing in N — the "choice effect" (Corollary 1). Proposition 1 shows these forces combine into a non-monotonic relationship: for every agent type 𝔞 there exists a finite optimal choice-set size N̄(𝔞) beyond which additional candidates *reduce* expected utility, and N̄(𝔞) is non-decreasing in 𝔞 (agents with a high outside option benefit from ever-larger choice sets; low-𝔞 agents are hurt by "too many candidates"). Section 4 embeds this into a market for platforms: a monopolist platform offering N < Ω candidates (versus a free, unrestricted outside market offering Ω) can profitably charge a positive fee to a self-selected subset of low-𝔞 agents (Proposition 2); with two competing platforms M1 < M2, there exists an equilibrium where both coexist, with the smaller-choice platform M1 charging a strictly higher fee than M2 (Proposition 3) — self-selection further lowers the effective rejection probability on the restricted platform beyond the mechanical N/(N+1) formula, reinforcing its profitability. Robustness checks in the appendix: a two-tentative-offer extension (relaxing the single-offer assumption) preserves the qualitative result; a Monte Carlo simulation introduces correlation h between an agent's outside option 𝔞 and how much others like them (Λ), and shows the curvilinear (rise-then-fall) utility-vs-N relationship persists for all tested h ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}, vanishing only in the limiting case h=1 (fully vertical/objective-quality preferences).

## Datasets and Baselines
Purely theoretical — no empirical dataset or algorithmic baseline. The paper's sole real-world touchstone is a stylized motivating fact (not independently measured in this paper): eHarmony, which restricts users to a limited candidate pool, charges customers roughly 25% more on average than Match.com, which offers unlimited browsing. The paper's implicit "baseline" is the standard linear-positive-network-effects assumption used in prior platform-competition literature (Rochet & Tirole 2003; Katz & Shapiro 1985, 1994), which this paper's micro-founded model is built to depart from and improve on.

## Results
No empirical numeric results; the paper's "results" are closed-form propositions and a numerical simulation:
- Pr(rejection | N candidates) = N/(N+1) (exact, closed form).
- Total expected payoff EU(𝔞|N) is unimodal in N for every agent type; the optimal N̄(𝔞) is weakly increasing in 𝔞 (Proposition 1; illustrated with a step-function figure over 𝔞).
- Willingness-to-pay WTP(𝔞) = EU(𝔞|N) − EU(𝔞|Ω) is positive and strictly decreasing for 𝔞 below a cutoff 𝔞̄, and negative above it (Lemma 2) — this cutoff characterizes the self-selection into a restricted-choice platform.
- In the duopoly equilibrium (Proposition 3), there exist thresholds 0 < 𝔞̂₁ < 𝔞̂₂ < 1 sorting agents into the small-choice platform, the large-choice platform, and the unmatched outside option, respectively, with f₁ > f₂ (the smaller platform charges the higher fee).
- Simulation with correlated 𝔞/Λ (h up to 0.8, N tested up to 150): the qualitative "limits to positive network effects" curve holds at every tested correlation level short of h=1; e.g., at h=0.4 an agent with 𝔞=0.4 prefers a platform offering 2 candidates over 3, while an agent with 𝔞=0.6 prefers 4 over any other number.

## Limitations
Self-flagged by the authors: the model requires same-side competition to exist on *both* sides of the market for the mechanism to operate — the authors explicitly note it would not apply to markets lacking same-side competition (their own counter-example: video-game platforms, where one gamer buying a copy doesn't reduce another's ability to buy the same game); the main model restricts each agent to a single offer (relaxed via a tentative-offer extension in Appendix A.1, where the qualitative results are shown to hold); the main model assumes fully independent, purely "horizontal" (subjective) preferences with no correlation between an agent's outside option and how desirable others find them — relaxed only via numerical simulation (no closed-form solution exists once 𝔞 and Λ are correlated, and the correlated-preference effect vanishes entirely at h=1, perfect vertical sorting); the model assumes the platform changes access symmetrically for both sides at once, excluding scenarios where access is restricted on one side but expanded on the other; the paper explicitly does not rely on any assumption of behavioral choice-overload aversion, noting its economic mechanism is sufficient on its own (though it does not rule out behavioral effects compounding it).

## Heavily Cited Prior Works
1. Katz, M.L. & Shapiro, C. (1985, 1994) — foundational linear/positive network-effects theory; the baseline assumption this paper relaxes.
2. Rochet, J.-C. & Tirole, J. (2003) — "Platform Competition in Two-Sided Markets," the standard linear-positive-network-effects platform model contrasted throughout.
3. Damiano, E. & Li, H. (2007, 2008) — "Price Discrimination and Efficient Matching" / "Competing Matchmaking," the paper's closest related work on platforms using price/access restrictions to sort agents into pools.
4. Ellison, G. & Fudenberg, D. (2003); Ellison, Fudenberg & Mobius (2004) — prior results on coexistence of differently-sized auction platforms, contrasted on modeling assumptions (agents choosing platforms before vs. after knowing their own type).
5. Becker, G.S. (1973) — "A Theory of Marriage: Part I," the seminal vertical/assortative-matching theory this paper's fully horizontal-preference model deliberately departs from.
6. Brandenburger, A.M. & Nalebuff, B.J. (1996) — "Co-opetition," cited for the idea that candidates simultaneously act as complements (opposite side) and substitutes (same side), which is the intuition behind the choice/competition-effect decomposition.
7. Iyengar, S. et al. (2006); Schwartz, B. & Ward, A. (2004) — behavioral-economics "choice overload" literature, explicitly distinguished from this paper's purely economic (non-behavioral) mechanism for why more choice can hurt agents.

## Project Relevance
Addresses **Layer 3 (market-design levers)** most directly: this is a rigorous theoretical justification for "like limits" / restricted candidate pools as a viable, even premium-priced, product-design lever — directly relevant to deciding whether and for whom to cap exposure. It does not address **Layer 1 (reciprocal scoring)** — how much an agent likes a candidate (Λ) is exogenous and unmodeled, not estimated from data — nor **Layer 2 (capacity-aware exposure allocation)**, since the model restricts choice *symmetrically and platform-wide* (the same N for everyone on a given platform tier), not per-user based on individual capacity or current match load; nor **Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics/interference)**, which is entirely out of scope. **Disanalogy flag:** the mechanism operates at the "which platform tier does this user choose" level, not the individual per-user allocation level the project's core problem requires (redistributing surplus likes away from a small set of highly desirable users toward under-matched ones) — this paper explains why *uniform* choice restriction can be a viable business model, not how to *target* restriction or redistribution at the individual level.
