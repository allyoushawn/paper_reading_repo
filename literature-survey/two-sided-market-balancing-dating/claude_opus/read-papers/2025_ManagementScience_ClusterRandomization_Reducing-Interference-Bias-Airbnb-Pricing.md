# Reducing Interference Bias in Online Marketplace Experiments Using Cluster Randomization: Evidence from a Pricing Meta-experiment on Airbnb

- **notebook source_id:** `6a17afaa`
- **extraction method:** direct PDF read (NotebookLM unavailable)

## Summary
Online marketplaces violate the SUTVA "no interference" assumption underlying standard A/B tests because buyers and sellers compete for the same consideration sets, so individual-level randomized total average treatment effect (TATE) estimates can be biased ("interference bias"). The authors run an in vivo meta-experiment ("experiment over experiments," Saveski-style) on Airbnb testing a platform-fee increase, comparing an individual-level-randomized arm against a cluster-randomized arm (clusters built from listing co-occurrence in guest search sessions). They find the individual-level TATE (−0.345 bookings/listing) is significantly more negative than the cluster-randomized TATE (−0.277 bookings/listing), and estimate that at least 19.76% (±9.06%) of the individual-level TATE is attributable to interference bias and is eliminated by cluster randomization. They also prove a new proposition, extending Eckles et al. (2017), showing cluster randomization is guaranteed to reduce TATE bias even when direct and indirect treatment effects have opposite signs — the realistic marketplace case, unlike prior network-interference theory which assumed same-signed ("self-reinforcing") spillovers.

## Method
Interference is modeled via a linear "interference matrix" B: E[Y_i(Z)] = α_i + Σ_j B_ij Z_j, where Z_j is seller j's treatment assignment. Proposition 1 (extending Eckles et al. 2017's Theorem 2.1): for any interference matrix B whose diagonal entries share a sign and whose off-diagonal entries share a sign, cluster randomization's TATE bias is always ≤ individual-level randomization's TATE bias — even when direct (within-cluster) and indirect (cross-cluster/spillover) effects have opposite signs, which the authors argue is the realistic marketplace case (raising one host's price shifts demand to competing listings: a negative spillover from a positive direct effect). Clusters were built from a 16-dimensional "demand embedding" per listing (word2vec/skip-gram over sequences of listings co-viewed in the same guest search session, following Grbovic & Cheng 2018), then hierarchically partitioned (recursive bisection, Kang et al. 2016) into clusters capped at 1,000 listings. 75% of clusters were cluster-randomized (treatment/control assigned to the whole cluster); 25% were individual-level (Bernoulli) randomized as an internal comparison arm. A joint model, Y_i = α + (β+νM_i)T_i + ξM_i + Σγ_l·1(B_i=l) + δX_i + ε_i (M_i = arm indicator), estimates ν as the bias difference between arms; Ω = ν̂/(ν̂+β̂) is the estimated fraction of the naive TATE due to interference bias.

## Datasets and Baselines
Real production A/B test on Airbnb: 2,602,782 listings, run March 16–21, 2019, testing a guest-facing platform-fee increase (treatment) vs status quo (control). 647,377 listings in the individual-level-randomized meta-control arm (25% of clusters); 1,955,405 listings in the cluster-randomized meta-treatment arm (75% of clusters; 2,981 treated / 2,979 control clusters). The comparator is the individual-level-randomized TATE itself, treated as the "naive," bias-inflated benchmark against which the cluster-randomized estimate is compared; pretreatment balance was checked and confirmed (no significant differences, p up to 0.90).

## Results
Individual-level randomized TATE on bookings: −0.345 (SE 0.013, p<0.01). Cluster-randomized TATE: −0.277 (SE 0.012, p<0.01). The joint model estimates the "true" TATE at −0.277 bookings/listing, with −0.068 bookings/listing (19.76% ± 9.06%) of the naive individual-level estimate attributable to interference bias and eliminated by cluster randomization. Heterogeneity (suggestive, not statistically significant differences between subgroups): interference bias is 12.05% (±11.55%) in supply-constrained geographies vs 28.65% (±14.91%) in demand-constrained geographies; 14.98% (±11.69%) in low-quality-cluster geographies vs 25.92% (±15.14%) in high-quality-cluster geographies. Only 20.1% of listing hosts searched at all in their own geography in the pre-experiment month (median 8 searches among those who did), and only 12% viewed a competitor's listing page — evidence that interference is driven by shared buyer consideration sets ("sellers co-occur ... and compete"), not sellers observing and reacting to each other.

## Limitations
Authors' own stated limitations: Proposition 1's guarantee applies only to interventions that uniformly increase or decrease demand for treated sellers, not to a mixture of both; cluster randomization brings "substantial reductions in statistical power" versus individual-level randomization; there is "not a one-size-fits-all solution" — cluster randomization has different tradeoffs from two-sided randomization (Johari et al. 2022, loses power under supply-demand imbalance) and switchback experimentation (Bojinov et al. 2022, vulnerable to temporal "carryover" spillovers in a booking market where guests browse over days/weeks); the supply/demand-constrainedness and cluster-quality heterogeneity results are explicitly flagged as suggestive, not confirmed; cluster construction has no single optimal method — the authors used an ad hoc cluster-size threshold (1,000) chosen specifically for this meta-experiment.

## Heavily Cited Prior Works
- Eckles, Karrer, Ugander (2017) — theory that cluster randomization reduces (not necessarily eliminates) interference bias under same-signed spillovers; this paper's Proposition 1 directly extends their Theorem 2.1 to opposite-signed effects.
- Ugander, Karrer, Backstrom, Kleinberg (2013) — proposed graph cluster randomization (GCR) as a network-interference experiment design.
- Saveski, Pouget-Abadie, Saint-Jacques, Duan, Ghosh, Xu, Airoldi (2017) — the "meta-experiment" ("experiment over experiments") design this paper's own meta-experiment directly follows, originally run on LinkedIn.
- Blake & Coey (2014); Fradkin (2015) — earlier evidence that naive online-marketplace experimentation can overstate TATE by up to 100%, motivating this line of work.
- Johari, Li, Liskovich, Weintraub (2022) — "two-sided randomization," a competing marketplace-interference-bias solution discussed as the paper's main alternative/complement.
- Bojinov, Simchi-Levi, Zhao (2022) — switchback experimentation, the other major competing design.
- Grbovic & Cheng (2018) — the listing-embedding methodology this paper adapts to build its demand embeddings for clustering.

## Project Relevance
Addresses Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics and experimentation under interference) directly and is one of the strongest sources in this survey for the "interference-aware A/B testing" sub-problem: it gives a concrete, production-scale empirical estimate (≈20% bias) of how badly naive individual-level randomization can mis-measure a two-sided-marketplace intervention, plus a provable guarantee for when cluster randomization helps. **Disanalogy flag:** Airbnb listings are a supply side with effectively unlimited "reply capacity" — a host can book as many guests as calendar availability allows, and the interference mechanism here is buyers competing over shared search consideration sets, not receivers running out of ability to reciprocate. A dating app's reply-capacity scarcity is a *human* constraint (attention/time to respond to likes) and arguably a stronger source of interference than the demand-side browsing competition modeled here — so the cluster-randomization *technique* is portable, but this paper's own interference model (buyer-side consideration-set overlap) would need to be re-derived around receiver capacity exhaustion for a dating context rather than reused as-is.
