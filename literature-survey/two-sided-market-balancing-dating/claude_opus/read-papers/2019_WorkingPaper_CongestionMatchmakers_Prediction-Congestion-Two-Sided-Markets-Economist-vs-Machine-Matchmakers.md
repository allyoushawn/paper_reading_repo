# Prediction and Congestion in Two-Sided Markets: Economist versus Machine Matchmakers

- **notebook source_id:** `1c0f9a09`
- **extraction method:** direct PDF read (NotebookLM unavailable)

## Summary
Standard machine-learning recommenders (content filtering, collaborative filtering) on two-sided platforms tend to recommend the same small set of "popular" users to everyone, creating **congestion**: recommendation lists that concentrate too heavily on a few super-star users, which both lengthens the time it takes users to find a reciprocated match and discourages participation by users who realize the algorithm rarely surfaces them. The authors propose grafting a classical economic separable matching model (Choo & Siow 2006, transferable utility) onto standard ML recommenders — using the model's implied "matching cost" (an equilibrium congestion price) to re-rank candidates and steer recommendations away from over-demanded users — and evaluate the resulting "equilibrium" recommenders on real data from a large Taiwanese online dating platform (490,000+ users). The equilibrium recommenders raise the out-of-sample hit rate by roughly 19x versus the plain baseline algorithms, more than double the fraction of distinct users who ever get recommended (coverage), and in counterfactual Gale-Shapley-style simulations cut the number of rounds needed to clear the market roughly in half (accelerating matching by up to 200%).

## Method
Three-module pipeline: **(1)** a baseline machine-learning preference estimator — either "content filtering" (a flexible fixed-effects OLS/linear-probability model of like-probability on user attributes and pairwise distance features, y_mw = α₀ + α₁'z_w + α₂'d(x_m,z_w) + u_mw) or "collaborative filtering" via Matrix Factorization (latent factors p_m, q_w fit by regularized least squares on the sparse like/no-like matrix); **(2)** a "pseudo-market" construction that, for a given user, walks the click graph outward (a user's clicked profiles, then who else clicked those profiles, then who those people clicked, etc.) to build a computationally tractable local matching market of direct/indirect competitors and potential partners; **(3)** the Choo–Siow (2006) separable transferable-utility matching-equilibrium model, solved on the pseudo-market: each pair (m,w) has social surplus Φ_mw = α_mw + γ_mw (from the ML step), and the equilibrium price/"matching cost" τ_xz clears demand and supply so that the number of type-x men wanting type-z women equals the reverse; recommendations are then ranked by the equilibrium **net utility** (α_mw − τ_mw) rather than by raw predicted preference — i.e., popular users are algorithmically "priced" higher, pushing their recommendation frequency down and diversifying who else gets surfaced. This yields two concrete "equilibrium recommenders": Equilibrium Content Filtering (OLS + pseudo-market + CS equilibrium) and Equilibrium Collaborative Filtering (MF + pseudo-market + CS equilibrium).

## Datasets and Baselines
**Data:** confidential/anonymized behavioral data from SweetRing, a major Taiwan online dating platform, July 2016–October 2017: 379,448 male and 115,552 female accounts (~3:1 male:female ratio), with click, like, and message actions; like caps (5/12hr unpaid men, 15/12hr VIP men, 40/12hr women regardless of status) create an explicit reciprocal-capacity structure. Preference regressions use 31.3M (men) and 16.3M (women) clicked-profile observations; overall like-data sparsity is 0.04% of all possible pairs (vs. ~1% for Netflix).
**Baselines/comparisons (7 recommenders evaluated):** OLS alone; MF alone; Pseudo-Market alone (random draw from the pseudo-market, no ML ranking); OLS+Pseudo-Market; MF+Pseudo-Market; OLS+Pseudo-Market+CS (Equilibrium Content Filtering); MF+Pseudo-Market+CS (Equilibrium Collaborative Filtering).

## Results
- **Hit rate** (probability at least one of N=10 recommended women is liked by the man, out-of-sample, 5,000 men who sent 20–30 likes): plain OLS ≈ 2%, plain MF ≈ ~0% (near-zero predictive power alone). Restricting to the pseudo-market (random draw) alone already reaches ~20% at N=10. Adding OLS ranking to the pseudo-market ("green triangle") raises hit rate by a **factor of 1.8** over random draws from the pseudo-market; adding MF ranking **doubles** it. Adding the CS equilibrium model on top of OLS further improves hit rate (Equilibrium Content Filtering, blue triangle); adding CS on top of MF *slightly decreases* raw hit rate (Equilibrium Collaborative Filtering, blue circle) but the two equilibrium curves converge and both still outperform pure random pseudo-market draws. (Abstract states the fully-combined equilibrium recommender's hit rate outperforms baseline content filtering "by a factor of 19" — consistent with comparing the ~2% plain-OLS hit rate to the ~35%+ combined-method hit rate reported.)
- **Coverage rate** (share of distinct users ever appearing in top-1 recommendations, 500 simulated markets of 1,000 men / 305 women): without the CS equilibrium module, OLS coverage ≈ 10%, MF coverage ≈ 25% (both "highly clustered" — congestion). Adding the CS equilibrium module raises OLS coverage to ~85% (several-fold) and MF coverage to **near 100%** ("almost full coverage").
- **Congestion / market-clearing speed** (simplified Gale-Shapley simulation, 500 markets of 1,000 men): median matches made in round 1 — MF alone 75 (7.5% of men), OLS alone 25 (2.5%); with CS equilibrium added, MF+CS median >400 matches (>40% of men, a **5x** increase) and OLS+CS median ~200 matches (20% of men, an **8x** increase). Rounds needed to clear 95% of the market: median ≈10 for MF alone, ≈15 for OLS alone; with CS equilibrium, <5 rounds for MF+CS and ~6 rounds for OLS+CS — the CS module **cuts required rounds roughly in half** (stated in the abstract as accelerating the matching process by 200%).

## Limitations
Authors' own caveats: (1) the separable-matching framework assumes unobserved preference heterogeneity is additively separable from observed attributes, which "less problematic" but not eliminated even with rich covariates; (2) the model is a one-to-one matching approximation, while online dating is not a strict one-to-one market ("the use of the one-to-one matching model is rather a simplified approximation to the complex truth"); (3) the simplified Gale-Shapley congestion simulation assumes men behave *myopically* (mechanically follow the recommendation list) and does not model deferred-acceptance/strategic search behavior, so it isolates the algorithmic congestion effect but does not capture real user behavior fully; (4) the MF-based hit-rate evaluation is admittedly partly in-sample (all women in the pseudo-market were used in MF training) — the authors partially address this with 50%/20% held-out training subsets and report similar results, but flag it as a limitation; (5) the paper explicitly notes a **misaligned-incentives** limitation at the platform level: since many platforms monetize via subscription, a platform "may not have the incentive to reduce congestion or minimize time spent" — i.e., the paper's technical fix may not be adopted under current business models; they suggest alternative pricing (e.g., "matching guarantee" one-time fees) as future work rather than analyzing it themselves.

## Heavily Cited Prior Works
- Choo, Siow (2006) — "Who marries whom and why," JPE — source of the separable transferable-utility (TU) matching-equilibrium model (CS model) at the core of the method.
- Galichon, Salanié (2015) — "Cupid's Invisible Hand: Social Surplus and Identification in Matching Models" — existence/uniqueness proof for the TU matching equilibrium used.
- Koren, Bell, Volinsky (2009) — "Matrix factorization techniques for recommender systems" — basis of the collaborative-filtering baseline.
- Hitsch, Hortaçsu, Ariely (2010a) — "Matching and Sorting in Online Dating," AER — precedent for using online-dating data with a matching-theoretic lens (and the NTU-vs-TU framing debate the authors address).
- Hitsch, Hortaçsu, Ariely (2010b) — "What Makes You Click? Mate Preferences in Online Dating" — precedent for click-data-based preference estimation, the basis of the paper's OLS specification.
- Che, Koh (2016) — "Decentralized College Admissions," JPE — cited for the observation that congestion in matching markets lacks a standard economic benchmark model.
- Gale, Shapley (1962) — "College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage" — basis of the Gale-Shapley congestion/market-clearing simulation.
