# Strategic Behavior in Two-sided Matching Markets with Recommendation-enhanced Preference-formation

## Summary
Two-sided matching markets (e.g., school choice, refugee placement) increasingly use predictive models trained on past interaction outcomes to help agents form preferences before the matching mechanism runs. This paper shows that when the "returning" side of the market (e.g., schools, which persist across rounds) interacts with newly-matched partners, they can sacrifice short-term utility in how they treat that match in order to bias the training data and thus future predictions/matches in their favor — a new strategic-behavior type the authors call an "adversarial interaction attack." They build a formal repeated-market model (modeling → matching → interacting phases) and, in a simplified two-school/two-student-type setting, prove that forward-looking schools have an incentive to attack, that attacks escalate toward a mutually-worse Nash equilibrium, that the incentive to attack grows as predictions become more accurate and trusted, and that the attack disproportionately harms disadvantaged ("expensive") students, increasing inequality.

## Method
Three-phase repeated system: **(1) Modeling** — a hypothesis h_θ is fit on interaction history H^t via a loss L, predicting expected interaction outcome for any (agent, agent) pair; **(2) Matching** — a matching procedure M maps (possibly prediction-influenced) preferences of both sides to an assignment μ, using e.g. random serial dictatorship (RSD); **(3) Interacting** — matched pairs interact and produce an outcome o, which yields value/cost/utility for each side and becomes new training data for the next round's model. A returning agent's outcome choice is a dynamic program (Bellman equation, discount factor β): the "truthful" strategy always picks the 1-step-utility-maximizing outcome; an "adversarial interaction attack" is any deliberate deviation from that, at some attack level l, chosen to bias the future hypothesis. Two knobs govern the system: **accuracy α** (probability the prediction reflects the true best recent outcome) and **trust θ** (probability an agent adopts the prediction-based ranking over their own prior). In the simplified model (2 schools — cheap C, expensive E — capacity 1 each, RSD matching), the authors derive best-response and Nash-equilibrium conditions analytically (game-theoretic proofs, Theorems 1-7), and additionally run an agent-based simulation (k-Nearest-Neighbors predictor, more agents, comparing RSD/Deferred Acceptance/Boston mechanisms) to check robustness.

## Datasets and Baselines
No real-world dataset — this is a theoretical/game-theoretic paper. The only empirical component is a synthetic agent-based simulation (described as being in the appendix, not extracted here) that uses k-Nearest-Neighbors as the prediction model and compares three matching mechanisms (RSD, Deferred Acceptance, Boston mechanism). There are no baseline recommender or matching algorithms being benchmarked against — the contribution is the discovery/formalization of a strategic-behavior class, not a proposed algorithm to evaluate against competitors.

## Results
All results are formal theorems on the simplified 2-school model (schools Y={C,E}, capacity 1 each; students of type C or E; outcome o∈[0,1]; cost to school of a chosen outcome is 0 for cheap students, 3/4·o for expensive students):
- **Theorem 1:** With perfect accuracy and trust (α=θ=1) and one student of each type, if schools are sufficiently forward-looking (discount factor β>2/3), schools have an incentive to deviate from truthful interaction and attack.
- **Theorems 2-3:** Truthful interaction remains a Nash Equilibrium in restricted cases — when only expensive students are present, or when all students induce equal negative utility for schools with enough students available.
- **Theorem 4:** If there is exactly one student who gives schools negative utility for any outcome, sufficiently forward-looking schools have an incentive to attack.
- **Theorem 5:** Once one school attacks at level l, the other school's best response (for β>4/5) is to attack at level min{1, l+1/L} — a strictly escalating best response.
- **Corollary 1:** The strategy profile where both schools attack at the maximum level (l=1) is a Nash Equilibrium, but it is Pareto-dominated by the profile where both schools interact truthfully (i.e., the equilibrium makes everyone worse off than truthful play would).
- **Theorem 6:** The expected utility gain from an adversarial interaction attack increases monotonically in both accuracy (α) and trust (θ).
- **Theorem 7:** Truthful interaction is a Nash Equilibrium when predictions have 50% accuracy (α=0.5, i.e., random) or zero trust (θ=0).
- Simulation results (appendix, not fully extracted) reportedly confirm the theoretical takeaways and add: different matching mechanisms are differently susceptible depending on the distribution of student attributes; letting schools express true (cardinal) preferences instead of using lottery-based mechanisms can sometimes eliminate the attack incentive (except when utilities are negative and students are scarcer than seats); the welfare loss from attacks falls disproportionately on expensive students, increasing inequality. No numeric utility values, effect sizes, or p-values are given for the simulation in the pages read — the paper's evidence in the main text is entirely analytic (theorem/proof), not statistical.

## Limitations
The authors explicitly state this is a deliberately simplified 2-school/2-student-type toy model built for tractable analysis, not a realistic deployment scenario. They flag as open future work: whether adversarial interaction attacks remain attractive in more realistic settings (more agents, more matching mechanisms, richer strategy spaces), whether attacks can be made "undetectable," and extending beyond the specific assumptions used (symmetric value between agents, zero cost to the non-returning side, agents that cannot inject fake data unlike classical recommender-system poisoning attacks).
