## Summary
Lyft redesigned its core rider-driver matching algorithm using an online reinforcement learning (RL) approach that estimates, in real time, the future earnings ("value") of an available driver at their current spatiotemporal state, and uses this driver value function as the edge weights in Lyft's existing batch bipartite-matching optimization. Unlike prior offline-RL approaches in ridesharing (which train on historical data and redeploy periodically, e.g. weekly), Lyft's system updates the value function continuously and on-policy from live dispatch feedback (trip fares, idling), making it the first documented full-scale industry deployment of an online RL system in a critical, real-time operational role. A month-long, all-market switchback (time-split) experiment across the U.S. showed the approach reduced driver unavailability by 13%, cut rider cancellations by 3%, raised five-star ratings by 1%, and drove over $30 million/year in incremental revenue — with no extra budget for driver relocation — leading Lyft to roll the algorithm out globally in 2021.

## Method
Lyft solves the online rider-driver matching problem via **batch matching**: every few seconds, it collects the current pool of unmatched requests and available drivers and solves a maximum-weight bipartite matching problem, max Σw_{i,j}a_{i,j} subject to each driver/rider matched at most once (solved via the Hungarian method). Historically, the weight w_{i,j} for a potential match encoded heuristics like pickup time; the paper's contribution is that these matching **weights are the output of an online RL value function**, so "selecting matching weights translates into a choice of policy" in RL terms. The problem is modeled as a (semi-)Markov decision process **decomposed around individual drivers** (state = driver's spatiotemporal position + local supply/demand context; action = serve a specific request or idle; reward = trip fare or zero). The **driver value function V(s)** estimates a driver's expected future dollar earnings if they keep driving from state s (worked example: a driver starting in Oakland at 7am who is expected to earn $225 over the following hours, averaged across simulated continuations). Matching decisions are scored by the **advantage** Δ(s,a) = r + (1−p)(γ^d V(s') − V(s)), i.e., the true long-run value of a trip beyond simply staying put, where p is cancellation probability, d is trip duration (or 4 seconds for idling), and γ is the discount factor. To handle data sparsity in a huge state space, V(s) is approximated via **linear factorization with coarse coding**: V(s) ≈ Σ_{k∈K(s)} w_s(k)v(k), where states are decomposed into overlapping factors (geohash spatial cells, time buckets, vehicle types, and interaction terms) with pre-fixed relevance weights (e.g., inverse distance from geohash-cell center), and only the factor values v(k) are learned online. The learning rule is a **temporal-difference (TD) update**, applied on-policy from real-time dispatch outcomes: V(s_i) ← V(s_i) + α(r_{ij} + γ^{d_ij}V(s_ij) − V(s_i)). The overall system is an **online approximate value-iteration** loop: (1) given the current driver value function, compute matching weights/decisions for the live batch; (2) apply one-step TD updates to the driver value function from the resulting real-time feedback (trip fares, idleness) — repeated continuously and adapting automatically to nonstationary conditions (e.g., a documented shock during New Year's Eve in Miami, Figure 8).

## Datasets and Baselines
No public benchmark dataset — this is a production system evaluated on live Lyft marketplace data across essentially all U.S. Lyft markets during 2021. The baseline is Lyft's pre-existing production matching algorithm (myopic/heuristic weights such as pickup-time minimization, described as "the outcome of years of continuous experimentation and refinement" — i.e., an already highly-tuned incumbent, not a naive baseline). The comparison method is a "control" variant of the same batch-matching system without the RL-learned value function, run against the "treatment" (RL-augmented) variant in a **time-split switchback experiment** (not a user-level A/B test, because treatment and control users interact through the shared marketplace and would contaminate each other). Buckets were 1-hour (later found to need >4 hours to reach steady state, since marketplace metrics took 2–3 hours to stabilize after a switch), run across two weeks per region with a randomized-then-paired/anti-symmetric assignment scheme to debias day-of-week effects, discarding burn-in/burn-out periods around each switch. Experiments were run in two batches: (1) high-transaction-volume/high-density regions, and (2) more sparse/smaller regions.

## Results
Reported treatment effects of the RL approach vs. the prior production algorithm, from the month-long, all-region switchback experiment (Table 2; no confidence intervals or p-values are reported in the read pages):
- **Unavailability** (ride requests for which no driver could be found, ÷ total requests): **−13.0%**
- **Rider cancellations** (rides canceled by rider ÷ total requests): **−3.0%**
- **Five-star ratings** (completed rides rated 5 stars ÷ total completed rides): **+1.0%**
- **Revenue (annualized)**: **> $30 million** in incremental annualized revenue vs. baseline, per Lyft's internal estimation models, attributed to (1) higher driver earnings opportunities and (2) millions of additional rides served per year — achieved via algorithmic change alone, with no additional budget for driver relocation/incentives.
The paper frames this as a rare case where the change improved the *entire Pareto frontier* simultaneously for drivers, riders, and the platform, rather than trading one stakeholder off against another. Following the experiment, Lyft rolled the algorithm out to all Lyft rideshare coverage areas in late 2021.

## Limitations
- **Supply side has essentially unlimited "reply capacity"** relative to a dating market: a driver can serve many riders sequentially over a shift, and the matching objective explicitly maximizes each driver's *cumulative* future earnings across many trips — there is no analogue of a single recipient's limited capacity to respond to a bounded number of suitors. This is a significant disanalogy for a dating-app context, where the scarce resource is a person's finite attention/reply bandwidth, not a resource that regenerates trip-by-trip.
- The matching decision is between a request and a driver, not requiring *mutual* consent from both sides (a rider request doesn't need a driver's affirmative preference in the way a dating "like" requires reciprocation) — so there is no two-sided reciprocity/desirability-skew problem being solved here, unlike dating markets where a "match" requires both sides to independently opt in.
- Authors explicitly acknowledge the exposition is simplified relative to the real system: doesn't model cancellations by either party, driver "swapping" mid-trip, matching a driver before a current dropoff completes, or multiple simultaneous ride types, all of which added real engineering complexity.
- Evaluation relies on Lyft's own internal simulators (acknowledged as imperfect) plus a single month-long live experiment; no long-run (multi-year) causal effect is reported, and the $30M figure is from Lyft's internal estimation models, not an independently audited financial figure.
- Trusting a self-updating (online) algorithm in a live, safety/revenue-critical system was called out by the authors as itself a major organizational and technical challenge, implying the approach requires substantial engineering investment to reproduce.

## Project Relevance
Touches **Layer 2 (capacity-aware exposure allocation)** at the mechanism level: the driver value function and advantage calculation are exactly the kind of forward-looking, system-value-optimizing weight function that a reciprocal-scoring + capacity-aware exposure layer for dating would want to learn (learn a "value of this exposure to the recipient's downstream capacity," not just immediate CTR). It also demonstrates a real, production-scale **Layer 4 (experimentation under interference)** pattern — the switchback/time-split design exists specifically because naive A/B testing is invalid under marketplace interference, which is directly relevant to experimentation methodology for a dating platform's capacity-aware exposure changes. However, **the core scarcity assumption does not transfer**: Lyft's "capacity" (driver-hours) is abundant and renewable within a shift, while dating's capacity (a person's willingness/ability to reply) is scarce and does not regenerate on the same timescale, so the paper is a mechanism-pattern reference rather than a directly portable capacity model.
