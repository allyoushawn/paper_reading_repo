# Beyond Match Maximization and Fairness: Retention-Optimized Two-Sided Matching

- **notebook source_id:** `ac33368a`
- **extraction method:** direct PDF read (NotebookLM unavailable)

## Summary
Two-sided matching platforms (online dating, recruiting) that optimize for total match count concentrate matches on a small set of already-popular users while leaving many others under-matched and prone to churn; naive fairness-of-exposure objectives don't reliably fix this either, since exposure equalized in proportion to merit doesn't necessarily track what actually keeps a given user on the platform. The paper formally reframes the objective as maximizing *user retention* directly (not match count, not an axiomatic fairness criterion), and introduces MRet (Matching for Retention), a dynamic learning-to-rank algorithm that scores each candidate recommendation by the sum of the retention-probability gain to the receiving user and the retention-probability gain to the recommended user, using a real per-user retention curve learned from profile + interaction history. Because exactly maximizing this joint-gain objective is NP-hard, MRet uses a Jensen's-inequality lower bound plus a concavity-based linear bound to reduce the problem to a simple per-item score computable via argsort in O(N log N). On both synthetic data and real interaction data from a large Japanese online dating platform, MRet achieves higher user retention than max-match, uniform, and fairness-of-exposure (FairCo) baselines, using substantially fewer total matches than a match-maximizing policy.

## Method
MRet models a per-user retention function f(x, m) — the probability a user x stays on the platform given cumulative matches m — learned via regression on user context and interaction history, under the assumption that f is concave in m (diminishing retention return to additional matches; empirically supported by the platform's real login-continuation-vs-match-count curve). At each arrival of a user x, the ideal ranking σ* maximizes the *joint* retention-probability gain: the receiver x's own gain from the new matches, plus the sum, over the K recommended candidates y, of each candidate's own retention gain from being shown to x (weighted by rank-position visibility α_k). This exact objective (Eq. 9) is NP-hard to optimize directly. MRet's trick: (1) a Jensen-inequality lower bound decomposes the receiver-side concave gain into a sum over positions; (2) a concavity-based linear bound converts each candidate's non-linear retention gain into a term linear in the position weight α_k. Combining both yields a per-candidate additive Score(y) (Eq. 13) such that ranking candidates by Score(y) via a simple argsort provably maximizes a lower bound on the true joint-retention objective (proved via the rearrangement inequality). This reduces an NP-hard combinatorial ranking problem to individual score computation, at the same computational cost as standard learning-to-rank.

## Datasets and Baselines
**Synthetic:** 1,000 users per side, K=5 recommendations, T=2,000 time steps, with a synthesized match probability r(x,y) blending cosine-similarity compatibility and a popularity-skew term (parameter κ controls skew strength), and a retention function with a per-user "satisfactory match count" threshold b_x beyond which retention gain flattens. Churn simulated stochastically (0.2% per-step check probability) over the match/retention dynamic.

**Real-world:** Interaction data from a large-scale Japanese online dating platform with millions of registered users; 1,000×1,000 male-female matching submatrix (ALS-imputed for unobserved pairs) built from February 2025 interactions; retention labeled as whether the user logged in again in March 2025; the retention function f is estimated separately from a 60,000-record dataset via k-means clustering (5 clusters per gender) plus per-cluster average retention-by-match-count curves.

**Baselines:** Max Match (rank purely by match probability), Uniform (random ranking), FairCo (Morik et al. 2020 — dynamic learning-to-rank enforcing fairness of exposure proportional to merit, λ=100), and MRet (best) — an oracle upper bound with access to the true (rather than estimated) retention function f.

## Results
**Synthetic experiments:** As the time horizon τ increases, Max Match accumulates by far the most total matches, Uniform the fewest, yet Uniform and Max Match achieve almost the same user retention rate — demonstrating that raw match volume does not track retention. MRet achieves a higher retention rate than all baselines (Max Match, FairCo, Uniform) while using **only about 70% of the total matches obtained by Max Match**. Under varying popularity skew (κ), MRet maintains high retention across the whole range, while FairCo's retention degrades toward Uniform's level as skew increases (FairCo pushes more matches to already-popular users under skew, increasing disparity). A direct distributional comparison (Fig. 5) shows FairCo allocates matches in a spread-out, roughly normal distribution around the satisfactory-count threshold, while MRet concentrates allocations to be mostly non-negative deviations from each user's individual satisfactory match count — i.e., MRet targets sufficiency per user rather than equal-in-expectation exposure.

**Real-world experiment (Japanese dating platform):** Because the true matching matrix is extremely sparse (most entries are 0 — matches are rare events), fairness-oriented methods (FairCo, Uniform) perform poorly, achieving significantly lower retention than Max Match. MRet achieves the **highest retention of all methods tested**, even under this sparse-match regime, and remains effective despite the real-world retention function violating the concavity assumption the theoretical bound relies on — demonstrating empirical robustness beyond the paper's own theoretical guarantee.

No single scalar retention percentage or p-value is reported; results are presented via comparative curves across methods (Figures 3, 4, 6) and the ~70% relative-match-volume figure quoted in text.

## Limitations
The authors' own stated limitations (Appendix A): (1) the pairwise match probability matrix r(x,y) is assumed given in advance rather than estimated online — incorporating online preference estimation is left as future work; (2) retention functions f are learned from offline data rather than adaptively updated online with an explicit exploration-exploitation mechanism; (3) the formulation treats both sides of the platform as equally important, whereas in practice a platform's business model may prioritize one side's retention over the other's (e.g., the paying side in dating apps, or job-seeker supply in recruiting) — the paper does not address asymmetric-weighting extensions.

## Heavily Cited Prior Works
1. Morik, Singh, Hong & Joachims (2020) — "Controlling Fairness and Bias in Dynamic Learning-to-Rank" — source of the FairCo baseline and the dynamic-LTR framework MRet extends.
2. Singh & Joachims (2018) — "Fairness of Exposure in Rankings" — foundational fairness-of-exposure objective the paper argues is an unreliable proxy for retention.
3. Su, Bayoumi & Joachims (2022) — "Optimizing Rankings for Recommendation in Matching Markets" — doubly-stochastic-matrix approach to two-sided ranking, explicitly noted as computationally intractable in the dynamic-arrival setting MRet targets.

5. Chen, Hsieh & Lin (2023) and Celdir, Cho & Hwang (2024) — empirical evidence (including a field experiment on online dating) for match-count imbalance and its retention consequences, motivating the paper's problem framing.
6. Hu, Koren & Volinsky (2008) — Alternating Least Squares for implicit-feedback collaborative filtering, used to impute the real-world matching matrix.
7. Wu, Wang, Hong & Shi (2017); Zou, Xia, Ding, Song, Liu & Yin (2019) — long-term/return-visit user-engagement optimization in recommender systems, part of the broader "beyond short-term optimization" literature this paper extends to two-sided matching.

## Project Relevance
Directly addresses **Layer 2 (capacity-aware exposure allocation)**: MRet is essentially a redistribution mechanism — reranking by joint retention gain instead of raw match probability, functionally similar in spirit to LiJAR-style redistribution but keyed on a learned retention curve rather than exposure counts. It also speaks to **Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics)** by using retention itself — not matches or exposure fairness — as the optimization target, and its head-to-head result that a fairness-of-exposure baseline (FairCo) actually *underperforms* on retention under popularity skew is a direct, mechanism-level validation of the project's framing that fairness proxies are not the goal. It does not address **Layer 1 (reciprocal scoring)** — the pairwise match probability r(x,y) is taken as given/estimated upstream, not modeled here — nor **Layer 3 (market-design levers)**, since the paper only varies ranking policy, not like-limits, curated batches, or signaling.
