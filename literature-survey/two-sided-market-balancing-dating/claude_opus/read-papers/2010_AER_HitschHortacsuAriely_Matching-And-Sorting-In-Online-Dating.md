# Matching and Sorting in Online Dating

- **notebook source_id:** `b22c1eb9`
- **extraction method:** direct PDF read (NotebookLM unavailable)

## Summary
Using detailed browsing and first-contact e-mail data from a 2003-era online dating site (San Diego and Boston markets), the authors estimate revealed mate preferences via a discrete-choice model, then ask whether a well-known centralized matching mechanism — Gale-Shapley deferred acceptance — run on those estimated preferences predicts the sorting patterns and match "efficiency" actually observed on the decentralized (browse-and-contact) site. They find the site's decentralized matches are approximately efficient relative to the Gale-Shapley stable-matching benchmark, and that the same estimated preferences, out-of-sample, also predict assortative-mating (sorting) patterns observed among offline dating, cohabiting, and married couples in national survey/census data — though the model underpredicts the magnitude of sorting along education and race/ethnicity, suggesting search frictions may play some role offline that they don't in the frictionless online-dating environment studied.

## Method
**Preference estimation:** A threshold-crossing revealed-preference model — a user sends a first-contact e-mail to a browsed profile iff the expected utility from a match exceeds a person-specific reservation value (an equilibrium object interpretable via Adachi's (2003) search model, which is shown to converge to Gale-Shapley outcomes as search frictions vanish). Preferences are parametrized as a linear-plus-quadratic-difference utility function over own/partner attributes (age, looks, height, BMI, income, education, race, religion, marital status) and estimated via a fixed-effects binary logit, with person-specific fixed effects absorbing the reservation-value threshold. A correction procedure (using a first-stage estimate of reply probability) checks for confounding by strategic behavior (e.g., avoiding costly rejection) — found to be negligible (the estimated cost-of-rejection-and-e-mailing coefficient is small and statistically insignificant).

**Matching benchmark:** Using the estimated preference profiles, the authors simulate the Gale-Shapley deferred-acceptance algorithm (both man-optimal and woman-optimal stable matches) as a "planner" counterfactual, and compare the resulting matches — via attribute correlations, attribute-difference statistics, and individual rank-based efficiency measures — against the matches actually observed on the site.

**Offline extension:** The estimated preferences (reweighted to match census demographics) are used to simulate Gale-Shapley matches for a synthetic offline population, and compared against observed sorting patterns among dating, cohabiting, and married couples (NSFG, Census IPUMS).

## Datasets and Baselines
Estimation sample: 3,004 men and 2,783 women from a major online dating site's San Diego and Boston markets, restricted to single/divorced/"hopeful" users seeking long-term or casual relationships (excludes married users); full activity logs over 3.5 months in 2003 (385,470 profile browses and 49,223 first-contact e-mails by men; 172,946 browses / 14,178 e-mails by women). Comparison "offline" datasets: NSFG Cycle 6 (7,643 women, dating/cohabiting/married couples) and the 2000 Census IPUMS 5% sample for Boston and San Diego MSAs (marriage sorting patterns).

Baseline/benchmark: the Gale-Shapley (1962) deferred-acceptance algorithm computed on the estimated preference profiles, used as the theoretical efficiency and sorting-pattern benchmark against which the actually observed decentralized matches are compared.

## Results
- First-contact-to-match conversion: 4.3% of men's first-contact e-mails lead to an eventual match; 6.4% of women's. Median 6 / mean 11.6 (men) and mean 12.6 (women) e-mails exchanged before a match is achieved.
- Logit preference estimates (Table 3): looks-rating coefficient ≈0.56–0.58 for both sexes; income valued roughly twice as heavily by women as by men; strong negative same-race preference terms for interracial pairings (e.g., white-men-on-black-women coefficient ≈ −0.83); the reciprocal-of-reply-probability term (proxy for combined e-mailing + rejection cost) is small and statistically insignificant for both sexes, indicating little evidence of strategic (cost-driven) contact behavior.
- Observed attribute correlations in matched pairs (Table 5): age ρ=0.70, looks ρ=0.31, height ρ=0.15, BMI ρ=0.13, income ρ=0.15, education ρ=0.12. Simulated Gale-Shapley matches from the estimated preferences reproduce age (ρ=0.70, identical) closely, but underpredict looks correlation (ρ=0.16 predicted vs. 0.31 observed) while closely matching height/BMI/income/education.
- Attribute differences (Table 6): in a typical matched pair, men are on average 1.3 years older, 5.9 inches taller, have higher BMI, lower looks rating, $48,960 more annual income, and 0.49 more years of education than their matched female partner; Gale-Shapley predictions broadly agree in direction, with the largest discrepancies in BMI and income (predicted income difference $22,770 vs. observed $48,960).
- Efficiency: the average difference in preference-rank between the Gale-Shapley-predicted match and the observed match is 64 (men) / 57 (women) out of the full ranking — 4.6% / 3.8% of the maximal achievable rank. A bias-correction simulation shows this entire gap is attributable to estimation bias (using unconditional rather than match-conditional preference draws), i.e. the true efficiency loss from the decentralized market vs. the Gale-Shapley benchmark is close to zero — "the decentralized search protocol ... does not result in significant efficiency losses." The average gap between a user's absolute first choice and their Gale-Shapley-predicted match is only 2.6% (men) / 1.8% (women) of the maximal rank, reflecting substantial preference heterogeneity.
- Offline sorting: married couples show age ρ=0.85 and education ρ=0.60 (income ρ=0.11, rising to 0.17 using imputed income); the reweighted Gale-Shapley simulation predicts age ρ=0.73 and education ρ=0.53 — qualitatively similar but somewhat smaller in magnitude, and race/ethnicity endogamy is qualitatively but not fully quantitatively predicted (e.g., simulated 38% of black men matched to black women vs. 72.2% observed in the census IPUMS data). Setting the model's "horizontal" (idiosyncratic) preference terms to zero sharply overstates assortativeness, showing horizontal preferences are essential to explaining the observed sorting.

No formal significance-testing framework (e.g., a single omnibus p-value) is reported for the efficiency claim; standard errors/bootstrap SDs are reported throughout the tables (e.g., Table 3, Table 5, Table 7).

## Limitations
The authors flag several caveats themselves: a "match" is only an indirect proxy (exchange of contact info / a keyword like "let's meet"), not confirmed offline dates or marriages; comparing online preferences to offline (marriage) sorting requires assuming the dating-site users' preferences, conditional on observable attributes, are representative of the general population — an untested assumption; the Gale-Shapley model is a non-transferable-utility framework, while marriages might be better described by a transferable-utility framework; the measured efficiency gap between observed and Gale-Shapley-predicted matches is likely biased toward finding "gains" (because unobserved match-specific utility draws are systematically higher for realized matches), which the authors correct for via simulation but which leaves some residual uncertainty; the predicted correlations underpredict observed sorting along education and especially race/ethnicity in the offline (marriage) data, which the authors suggest may indicate a role for search frictions offline not present in this frictionless online environment.

## Heavily Cited Prior Works
1. Gale, D. & Shapley, L.S. (1962) — the deferred-acceptance algorithm used as the paper's theoretical efficiency/sorting benchmark.
2. Adachi, T. (2003) — two-sided search-and-matching equilibrium model whose asymptotic equivalence to Gale-Shapley (as search costs vanish) motivates using Gale-Shapley as a tractable stand-in.
3. Roth, A.E. & Sotomayor, M.A.O. (1990) — foundational matching-market design text (stability, Pareto-optimality-within-stable-matches results cited).
4. Becker, G.S. (1973) — "A Theory of Marriage," the seminal assortative-matching / sorting-in-marriage-markets reference.
5. Laumann, E.O. et al. (1994) — National Health and Social Life Survey; documents similar sorting patterns across marriages, cohabitations, and short-term relationships, used to justify comparing online-dating preferences to offline marriage patterns.
6. Kalmijn, M. (1998) — survey of empirical marital-sorting literature (educational, age, ethnic homogamy).
7. Kurzban, R. & Weeden, J. (2005); Fisman, R. et al. (2006, 2008); Eastwick, P.W. & Finkel, E.J. (2008) — speed-dating revealed-preference studies used as external validation/comparison for the estimated preference patterns.

## Project Relevance
**Low-to-medium project relevance.** This paper is a diagnostic/measurement study, not an allocation policy, and predates any recommendation-driven exposure allocation — the 2003 site studied had users browse and contact each other directly with no ranking algorithm at all. It doesn't address Layer 1 (reciprocal scoring; the first-stage reply-probability estimate is used only as a bias-correction covariate, not as a deployed like-back score), Layer 2 (no capacity-aware exposure or redistribution mechanism exists to allocate), or Layer 3 (no market-design levers — like-limits, signaling, curated batches — are tested). Its relevance is narrowly to Layer 4 (ecosystem measurement): it is a foundational use of Gale-Shapley as an efficiency benchmark for a real two-sided dating market, and its central finding — that idiosyncratic ("horizontal") preferences, not just a single vertical desirability scalar, drive most observed sorting — is a useful caution for building a reciprocal scorer that must capture genuinely heterogeneous taste, not just rank users on one attractiveness axis. **Disanalogy flag:** the market studied here had effectively unlimited browsing/search rather than a capacity-constrained ranked-recommendation feed, so its finding that "decentralized search needs no ranking algorithm to be near-efficient" does not transfer to a modern app where an algorithm actively allocates a scarce impression budget under reply-capacity constraints — the exact opposite of this paper's frictionless setting.
