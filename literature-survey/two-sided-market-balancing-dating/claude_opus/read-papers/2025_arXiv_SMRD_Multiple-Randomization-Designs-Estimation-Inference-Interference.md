## Summary
Standard completely-randomized A/B tests assume no interference (SUTVA), which is routinely violated in marketplaces where two populations of agents (e.g., buyers and sellers, viewers and content creators) interact strategically and treatment of one unit can spill over onto others. The paper gives the first formal finite-sample analysis of **Multiple Randomization Designs (MRDs)** — experiments where two (or more) populations are independently randomized and a pair/tuple is only "treated" if all its constituent units are — and in particular of **Simple MRDs (SMRDs)**, the leading two-population case. Under a tractable "local interference" assumption (a pair's outcome depends only on its own treatment plus the treated-fraction of its row and column, not the full assignment matrix), they derive unbiased plug-in estimators, exact finite-sample variances, conservative variance estimators, and a design-based finite-population central limit theorem for four causal estimands: average treatment effect (ATE), buyer-side spillover, seller-side spillover, and a "direct effect" purged of spillovers. Simulations of a strategic two-sided marketplace (Nash-equilibrium content creators and advertisers) show that a standard single-sided A/B test can recover the **wrong sign** of a platform intervention's profit effect, while the SMRD-based ATE estimator recovers the correct sign, and that the conservative SMRD test detects a true spillover effect with 99.5% power in their simulated setting.

## Method
- **Setting:** I buyers, J sellers (generalized to any two interacting populations, e.g., creators/advertisers, riders/drivers). Outcome `Y_ij(w)` for pair (i,j) is a potential outcome indexed by the full I×J binary treatment-assignment matrix **w**.
- **Local interference (Assumption 2.4):** `y_ij(w) = y_ij(w')` whenever (a) the pair's own assignment `w_ij` is unchanged, (b) the fraction of treated sellers for buyer i is unchanged, and (c) the fraction of treated buyers for seller j is unchanged. This is weaker than full SUTVA (no interference at all) but far more tractable than unrestricted interference; it is motivated by a worked Nash-equilibrium example (Example 2.5) where strategic content creators and advertisers respond to their own average treatment exposure, producing exactly this structure.
- **Simple Multiple Randomization Design (SMRD, Definition 3.4):** independently select `I_T` of I buyers and `J_T` of J sellers into "eligible" groups; pair (i,j) is treated only if *both* i and j are eligible (`W_ij = T` iff `min(w^B_i, w^S_j) = 1`). This induces **four assignment types** per pair: `cc` (neither eligible), `ib` (buyer-only eligible), `is` (seller-only eligible), `tr` (both eligible/treated) — all four types are mutually comparable pre-treatment (randomization), but not necessarily comparable post-treatment, which is precisely what lets SMRDs detect and measure spillovers (a two-arm single-population experiment cannot).
- **Estimands:** linear combinations of the four type-level population averages `ȳ_γ`, e.g. `τ_ATE = ȳ_tr − ȳ_cc`, buyer-spillover `τ^B_spill = ȳ_ib − ȳ_cc`, seller-spillover `τ^S_spill = ȳ_is − ȳ_cc`, direct effect `τ_direct` (a `[1,-1,-1,1]` contrast that cancels spillovers).
- **Estimators:** the plug-in type-average `Ŷ_γ` (mean outcome within each observed assignment-type bucket) is proven unbiased for `ȳ_γ` (Lemma 4.1/Theorem 4.2) under local interference; linear combinations of these estimate any of the estimands above unbiasedly.
- **Variance:** exact finite-sample covariances of the four type estimators are derived (Theorem 4.3), decomposed into buyer-level, seller-level, and buyer×seller interaction variance components; a conservative (Young's-inequality-based) variance estimator is given (Theorem 4.4/Eq. 15) since the true covariance across types is not point-identifiable without stronger assumptions.
- **Inference:** a design-based finite-population Central Limit Theorem (Theorem 4.8) extending Li & Ding (2017) / Shi & Ding (2022) from single- to multi-population design-based inference, giving a Berry-Esseen-type bound on the Studentized statistic's deviation from normality; enables standard hypothesis tests (e.g., testing for zero spillover) using the conservative variance estimator.

## Datasets and Baselines
- **No real marketplace data used** — validation is entirely via **simulation**. Primary simulation: a strategic-agents Nash-equilibrium two-sided marketplace (Example 2.5) with content creators i ∈ [I] and advertisers j ∈ [J], I=200, J=150, incentive level η=5%, treatment group sizes I_T=100, J_T=80; compensation rates and base compatibility parameters drawn i.i.d. (`m_ij ~ Exp(1)`, `r^c_i, r^a_j ~ Uniform([0, 1/5])`); revenue/profit at Nash equilibrium given in closed form (Eq. 2), producing potential outcomes that satisfy local interference by construction. 10,000 independent re-randomizations used to empirically verify estimator and variance-estimator properties. A second, additive-Gaussian-spillover simulation is reported in an appendix (not read in this pass).
- **Baseline design compared against:** standard single-sided randomization — randomize 50% of creators into treatment, treat all their interactions as under a simple buyer-randomized design (Eq. 3), and estimate with the standard difference-in-means estimator `τ̂_SRD`.

## Results
- **Unbiasedness/variance verification (Figures 1–2):** across 10,000 Monte Carlo re-randomizations, the type estimator `Ŷ_cc` is centered exactly on the true population value, and its variance estimator `Σ̂_cc` is centered on the true variance, matching the paper's theorems; the spillover estimator `τ̂^B_spill` is approximately Gaussian as predicted by the CLT, and the conservative variance estimator's distribution is centered above (conservative relative to) the true variance.
- **Spillover-detection power:** the conservative Studentized test `ẑ_0 = τ̂^B_spill / {Var̂^hi(τ̂^B_spill)}^{1/2}` rejects the null of no spillover **99.5% of the time** in their simulated setting (i.e., Type-II error ≈ 0.5%), demonstrating substantial power to detect true positive spillovers despite the deliberately conservative variance bound.
- **Headline comparison vs. standard single-randomization (Figure 4):** across 10,000 re-randomizations of each design type, the standard single-sided difference-in-means estimator `τ̂_SRD` is centered clearly on the **negative** side of zero (roughly the −6 to 0 range in the reported distribution, mean noticeably below zero), while the SMRD-based `τ̂_ATE` estimator is centered clearly on the **positive** side (roughly 0 to 9 range) — i.e., in this simulated strategic-complementarity marketplace, the standard single-randomization design usually estimates the **wrong sign** of the platform intervention's effect on total profit, while the SMRD design usually recovers the correct sign.
- **QQ-plot check (Figure 3):** the Studentized spillover statistic tracks the theoretical Gaussian quantiles closely, empirically supporting the CLT (Theorem 4.8).
- No point estimates of real-platform metrics (e.g., dollar revenue, match rate) are reported anywhere in the paper — all quantitative results are simulation-based verification of statistical properties, not applied business results.

## Limitations
- **Sparsity (Remark 4.9, self-flagged):** the CLT's error bound requires the effective "signal density" `C_β̄` to be small relative to `{I⁻¹+J⁻¹}⁻¹`; with sparse potential outcomes (many zero entries, common in pairwise buyer-seller interaction data), the theorem's guarantees can require the population to be very large relative to sparsity — the authors explicitly call generalizing to heavy-tailed/sparse settings "an important direction for future work."
- The local interference assumption is **not testable** within an SMRD itself — the paper proves that "differences between expected outcomes for the comparison groups can always be rationalized in a way that is consistent with local interference" under the SMRD's four assignment types; testing local interference requires a richer design (illustrated in Section 6/eq. 17 extensions).
- **No unbiased covariance estimator** exists across assignment types without further assumptions on potential outcomes — only a conservative upper bound is available (an intrinsic limitation of design-based inference here, analogous to the classical Neyman variance-bound issue for single-population RCTs).
- All empirical validation is **synthetic simulation**, not real platform data; the paper is explicitly a statistical-theory contribution with simulation-based validation of correctness rather than a field experiment or applied deployment study.
- The core SMRD design only handles two populations and a specific (multiplicative "AND"-style) treatment-assignment rule; more general MRDs (three+ populations, non-binary/scored eligibility, clustered variants) are sketched as extensions in Section 6 but not analyzed with the same rigor.

## Project Relevance
Addresses **Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics and experimentation under interference)** squarely and by name — this is a methods paper for exactly the "interference-aware A/B testing" problem the project's framing calls out. If the dating app wants to test a change to exposure allocation, like limits, or reciprocal scoring, a standard single-sided randomized test risks exactly the failure mode this paper demonstrates empirically: getting the *sign* of the platform-level effect wrong because of cross-side strategic spillovers (e.g., a change that looks good in a viewer-only A/B test could actually be reducing total matches once provider-side reply-capacity effects are accounted for). The four-assignment-type SMRD design (independently randomize both the "sender" and "receiver" side) is a directly implementable recipe for measuring both direct effects and cross-side spillovers of a dating-app intervention, which is exactly what's needed to validate changes to capacity-aware allocation or market-design levers without contaminated estimates.

**Disanalogy to flag:** none regarding capacity per se — this is a domain-agnostic statistical framework, not tied to any particular notion of scarce reply capacity; it applies equally to unlimited-capacity marketplaces (e.g., generic buyer-seller settings) and scarce-capacity ones like dating. The relevant caveat is method-fit rather than domain-fit: the local interference assumption (outcome depends only on own treatment + row/column treated-fraction) is a simplifying approximation that may not fully capture richer strategic dynamics in a reciprocal dating market (e.g., a user's behavior might depend on *which specific* other users were treated, not just the treated fraction), and this is not testable within the basic SMRD design itself.
