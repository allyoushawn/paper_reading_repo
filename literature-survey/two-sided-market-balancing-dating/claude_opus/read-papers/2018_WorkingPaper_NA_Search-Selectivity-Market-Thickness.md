## Summary
Standard matching theory holds that "thicker" markets (more potential matches) mechanically produce more and better matches, but on decentralized two-sided platforms — where matches require costly, mutual, sequential consent — users are strategically selective, adjusting how choosy they are based on beliefs about their odds of being accepted and of finding something better later. The paper causally identifies this effect via a large randomized field experiment on a real, global dating app that manipulates users' *beliefs* about local market size and competitor count (via an in-app pop-up), then combines the resulting exogenous belief variation with a structural two-sided sequential-search model to run platform-design counterfactuals. The headline finding: naively growing both sides of a two-sided platform can *decrease* matches per user because rising selectivity offsets the larger candidate pool — an effect that raising the per-user like-limit (proposal cap) can partially counteract.

## Method
**Experiment:** A randomized field experiment on a mobile dating app where users view profiles sequentially and must like/not-like each one before seeing the next (a match requires mutual likes). Upon opening the app, treatment-group users see a pop-up ("There are at least m men and w women nearby!") with independently randomized values of m and w (a V-shaped multiplicative factor between 0.75× and 1.25× the true local population), isolating belief-driven effects of market size and competition size from confounded actual-population effects. The core identification is the difference in behavior between users shown different treatment values, not treated-vs-untreated.

**Structural model:** A dynamic, finite-horizon, two-sided sequential-search model of a single search session: at each step the agent incurs a search cost to view a profile, then decides to like/not-like (incurring a screening cost and updating expected date utility if a match forms), with an explicit endogenous state for the number of likes remaining under the platform's like-limit. Agents form beliefs about others' behavior as a function of market/competition size (a Bayesian game of incomplete, imperfect information), estimated via a two-step method in the style of Hotz-Miller (1993) / Bajari-Benkard-Levin (2007): first-stage nonparametric belief extraction from pre-experimental historical data (logit regressions of like-probability on own/partner quality-percentile bins and market/competition-size grid points), second-stage individual-level maximum likelihood using Ackerberg's (2005) importance-sampling method to make estimation computationally tractable across ~66,000 agents (a naive full-MLE approach was estimated to take ~75 days; importance sampling made it feasible). Counterfactual equilibria (platform growth, gender gating, like-limit changes) are computed by iterating each side's beliefs to a fixed point.

## Datasets and Baselines
**Historical observational data:** 34-day window in 2015, >50,000 women and >100,000 men, heterosexual users in 2 (unnamed) countries, used to document the raw correlation between market thickness and selectivity and to estimate first-stage beliefs.
**Field-experiment sample:** 225,680 active users, randomized at the session level (sessions ≥6 hours apart), 90% shown the treatment pop-up / 10% control (an earlier passage in the paper also reports a treatment/control split of 203,170/22,510 for the same experiment).
**Structural-estimation sample:** 26,092 women and 40,647 men (first search session only, free users — i.e., users subject to a binding like-limit).

There is no algorithmic ML baseline; the "baseline" is the counterfactual of unchanged market thickness/like-limit against which population-growth and like-limit-doubling policies are compared.

## Results
**Reduced-form experimental effects:** When users believe their market size is 50% larger, they become 3% less likely to like low-quality-type users and 2.8% more likely to like high-quality-type users. When users believe they have 50% more competition, they become 2.3% more likely to like low-quality-type users and 4.5% less likely to like high-quality-type users. Average search intensity itself does not change significantly with beliefs — only selectivity does.

**Structural counterfactual — market growth:** A simultaneous 25% increase in both the male and female population reduces matches per user in *small* markets by 12% (men) and 18% (women), and has a roughly neutral-to-negative effect in *large* markets (+0.4% for men, −6% for women) — because rising selectivity (driven especially by men reacting more strongly to increased market size) offsets the larger candidate pool. Doubling the per-user like-limit for all users under this growth scenario is shown to mitigate some of the negative effect (the paper does not report a single re-quantified match-count figure for this mitigated scenario in the sections read).

**Structural counterfactual — gender gating (targeted one-side growth):** A 25% increase in women only, in small markets, increases men's average match count but *decreases* average match quality (conditional on matching) by 0.6%, and results in 7% fewer men finding an actual date (a mutually pursued match). In large markets, men get more matches and are more likely to find a date, but match quality increases by only 1.6%. Doubling the like-limit for women only, under gender gating, is shown to mitigate some of the negative effects for women.

**Validity checks:** A randomization check (Appendix Table 9) confirms the market-size treatment factor is uncorrelated with pre-treatment age, gender, account age, profile views, like rate, and match count (all correlations ≈0, p-values 0.079–0.940). A model-fit check (Figure 20) shows the structural model's predicted relative like-probability curves track the observed data reasonably well, especially for women.

No p-values or standard errors were located for the specific 12%/18%/0.4%/6% and 0.6%/7%/1.6% counterfactual match-count figures in the sections read; treat these as point estimates from the paper's simulated counterfactuals.

## Limitations
Self-flagged by the author via explicit numbered "Assumptions": Assumption 2 — an agent gets utility from a match only if a date actually occurs (ignores any standalone value from browsing/chatting); Assumption 3 — the utility of ending a search session without a date is normalized to 0; Assumption 4 — at time t an agent does not reconsider pursuing a date with an earlier match formed at t′ < t, a strong simplification the author defends empirically (most matched users get only a single match); Assumption 5 — within a session, an agent believes the quality draws they see are i.i.d. over time, validated empirically by showing no statistically significant correlation between shown-profile quality and search order; Assumption 6 — prior to the experiment, agents hold *rational* beliefs about market thickness (needed to bootstrap first-stage estimation from historical, non-experimental data). Separately, the counterfactual simulation shows profiles served completely at random rather than through the platform's real quality-sorting matching algorithm — explicitly flagged by the author as a simplification for computational tractability, with incorporating the real algorithm left as future work. The author also explicitly states the data cannot distinguish which specific channel (new-user-composition shift, algorithmic quality-sorting, or purely psychological/expectation effects) drives the observed market-size belief effect.

## Project Relevance
