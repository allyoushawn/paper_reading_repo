## Summary
Hayashi, Goda, and Saito formalize off-policy evaluation (OPE) and off-policy learning (OPL) for reciprocal "matching markets" (their running example is job-seeker/company scouting on a job platform, with dating explicitly named as an analogous domain), where a positive outcome requires two sequential actions: a first-stage action (e.g., a scout/like sent) and a second-stage response (e.g., a reply/like-back), and the ultimate reward — a match — is their product and therefore extremely sparse. They show standard OPE estimators (DM, IPS, DR) perform poorly in this setting because they rely only on the sparse match label, and propose two new estimators, **DiPS** and **DPR**, that explicitly exploit the denser first-stage reward signal to reduce variance (DiPS) and further control bias-variance trade-offs (DPR), with matching policy-gradient extensions for learning new policies offline. On synthetic data and real A/B-testing logs from the job-matching platform Wantedly Visit (21,736 companies, 17,460 job seekers, 1.2% reward sparsity), DiPS and DPR achieve substantially lower MSE and policy-selection error than DM/IPS/DR baselines, and DiPS-PG/DPR-PG learn better policies than PG variants of the baselines.

## Method
Let s ∈ {0,1} be the first-stage reward (e.g., scout sent), r ∈ {0,1} the second-stage reward (reply, conditional on a scout having been sent), and m = s·r the ultimate match reward. Define q_s(c,j) = P(s=1|c,j) and q_r(c,j) = P(r=1|c,j,s=1); the match probability is q_m(c,j) = q_s(c,j)·q_r(c,j). The policy value is V(π) = (1/|C|) Σ_c Σ_j π(j|c)·q_s(c,j)·q_r(c,j) — the expected number of matches under policy π.
- **DiPS** (Direct and Propensity Score): V̂_DiPS(π;D) = (1/|C|) Σ_c [π(j_c|c)/π₀(j_c|c) · s_c] · q̂_r(c,j_c) — applies importance-weighting (IPS) to the *first-stage* reward s (which is dense/unbiased and directly observed for every logged interaction), then multiplies by a regression estimate q̂_r of the second-stage reply probability, avoiding direct reliance on the sparse match label m.
- **DPR** (Direct, Propensity, and doubly Robust): V̂_DPR(π;D) = (1/|C|) Σ_c { π(j_c|c)/π₀(j_c|c) · (s_c·q̂_r(c,j_c) − q̂_m(c,j_c)) + E_π[q̂_m(c,j)] } — extends DiPS by additionally incorporating a match-probability regression model q̂_m(c,j) (as in DM/DR), giving a doubly-robust-style correction term for further bias-variance control.
- Both estimators have matching **policy-gradient** forms (∇V̂_DiPS-PG, ∇V̂_DPR-PG) that let a policy π_θ be learned offline via gradient ascent on the estimated policy value, rather than only evaluated.
- Theoretical results (Theorems 4.1–4.6): DiPS's bias depends only on the error of the second-stage reward model q̂_r (which is easier to estimate than the sparse match probability q_m directly), and its variance is provably lower than IPS's by an amount ≥ (1/|C|²) Σ_c E[w²(c,j)·q_s(c,j)·σ_r²(c,j)] ≥ 0 whenever q̂_r does not overestimate q_r; DPR has exactly the same bias as DiPS but lower variance whenever q̂_m is reasonably accurate. The appendix also extends the same two-stage-reward idea to Switch-DR and MIPS (ExtendedSwitch-DR, ExtendedMIPS), though these extensions are not empirically evaluated.

## Datasets and Baselines
- **Synthetic:** companies and job seekers represented as 10-dimensional Gaussian context vectors; nonlinear sigmoid reward functions q_s(c,j), q_r(c,j) parameterized by random matrices/vectors and a tunable sparsity parameter θ_sp; logging policy π₀ built via softmax over q_m(c,j) (inverse-temperature β = −0.5 default); target policy π is ε-greedy on q_m (ε = 0.2 default). Varied: number of companies |C| ∈ {500,...,5000}, number of job seekers |J| (action-space size), and a reward-sparsity parameter (0.5–2.5).
- **Real-world:** A/B-testing logs from *Wantedly Visit*, a Japanese job-matching platform with 4M+ registered users and 40,000+ companies; processed dataset has |C| = 21,736 companies, |J| = 17,460 job seekers, and 1.2% reward sparsity (Table 1). Both the logging policy π₀ and candidate target policies π were estimated via Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT) with 5-fold cross-validation, since exact original action probabilities were unavailable.
- **Baselines:** DM, IPS, and DR (and their policy-gradient variants DM-PG, IPS-PG, DR-PG for the learning task). Evaluation metrics: MSE (= squared bias + variance) and ErrorRate (probability of misordering two policies' true values when selecting between them, Eq. 5).

## Results
- **Varying number of companies (synthetic, Fig. 2):** every estimator except DM shows falling MSE and rising variance as |C| grows (more logged data); DiPS and DPR achieve "significantly lower MSE than the baseline methods across most experimental configurations," with much lower variance than IPS/DR at the cost of a small bias increase from the estimated q̂_r model, and correspondingly lower ErrorRate in policy selection.
- **Varying number of job seekers / action-space size (Fig. 3):** DiPS and DPR show markedly greater robustness — substantial variance reduction relative to IPS and DR as the action space grows, while bias remains consistently smaller than DM's; larger |J| improves policy-selection ErrorRate for DiPS/DPR relative to baselines.
- **Varying reward sparsity (Fig. 4):** as sparsity worsens, DM's MSE rises sharply (match-probability regression gets harder); DiPS and DPR "consistently outperform IPS and DR" across sparsity levels with lower ErrorRate throughout.
- **Real-world data (Fig. 5, sampling 2,000–8,000 companies, 20 repetitions):** DiPS and DPR achieve the lowest MSE overall; DPR in particular achieves "significantly lower MSE than the baseline methods across most configurations," from both reduced bias and reduced variance. DiPS has lower bias than IPS; DPR has lower bias than DR. Fig. 6 (8,000 companies) shows IPS's estimated-policy-value distribution sitting well above the true V(π) (large overestimation), while DiPS's distribution is tightly centered on the true value.
- **Reward-model quality (Table 2, appendix):** ROC-AUC of the reply-prediction model ranges 0.680–0.703 and the match-prediction model 0.598–0.707 across company-sample sizes of 2,000/5,000/8,000 — only moderate discriminative power, consistent with the sparse-reward difficulty motivating the paper.
- **Reward-model bias (Table 3, appendix):** the reply model underestimates the true reply rate by **−23.3% to −38.5%**, and the match model underestimates by **−38.5% to −53.4%**, depending on sample size.
- **Off-policy learning (Fig. 7, appendix):** DiPS-PG and DPR-PG achieve the highest relative policy values (normalized to the logging policy π₀) in most configurations across #companies, #seekers, and sparsity levels, "demonstrating their effectiveness not only in policy evaluation and selection but also in learning."
No explicit p-values are reported; results are presented as means with shaded variance/uncertainty bands over repeated trials (20 repetitions for real-world experiments).

## Limitations
- The formulation is presented using only company/job-seeker indices for exposition; extending it to context features (x_c, x_j) is noted as straightforward but is not the paper's main empirical focus.
- In the real-world experiment, the authors "were not able to replicate the exact action choice probabilities" of the true deployed policies; both the logging policy π₀ and the new target policies π had to be estimated via GBDT rather than known exactly — an approximation layered on top of the estimator comparison itself.
- The reward-regression models underlying DiPS/DPR show substantial underestimation (up to −53.4%) and only moderate ROC-AUC (0.60–0.71), meaning the bias-reduction advantage of DiPS/DPR still rests on imperfect regression models.
- DiPS/DPR trade a small increase in bias for a large reduction in variance relative to IPS/DR — an explicit trade-off, not a strict dominance in every regime.
- To the authors' knowledge, no prior work had addressed OPE/OPL for matching markets at all, so there is no matching-specific prior baseline to compare against beyond DM/IPS/DR and the authors' own appendix extensions (ExtendedSwitch-DR, ExtendedMIPS), which are not empirically tested in the main experiments.
- The evaluated domain is job-seeker/company matching, not dating; dating is named only as a motivating analogous use case in the introduction and related work, never used as an evaluation dataset.

## Project Relevance
One of the most directly transferable papers in this batch. **Layer 1 (reciprocal scoring):** the paper's core object, V(π) = (1/|C|)ΣΣ π(j|c)·q_s(c,j)·q_r(c,j), *is* a decomposed reciprocal like-back model (probability of showing/liking × conditional probability of reciprocation), and DiPS/DPR exist specifically to make learning and evaluating such reciprocal scorers statistically reliable when true matches (mutual likes) are sparse but the first-stage action (a like sent) is dense — exactly the data-sparsity problem a dating platform's reciprocal-scoring layer faces, since likes vastly outnumber mutual matches. **Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics and experimentation under interference):** the explicit motivation — "A/B tests impose substantial costs in time, resources, and risk... OPE plays a crucial role in evaluating recommender systems" before committing to costly live tests — maps directly onto the project's need for interference-aware, offline-first experimentation on matching/allocation policies. Two disanalogies must be flagged: (1) the evaluated market is job-seeker/company scouting, not romantic reciprocity — a company reviewing candidates is not capacity-constrained the way a popular dating profile's finite attention/reply bandwidth is, so this paper has **no notion of a depletable reply capacity**, no exposure-redistribution mechanism, and no market-design levers (like limits, curated batches, signaling); (2) success here is measured purely by estimator accuracy (MSE, ErrorRate against ground-truth policy value), not by ecosystem-health metrics — there is no match-Gini, no wasted-likes rate, and no two-sided-retention measurement. This paper supplies the statistical machinery to evaluate and learn a reciprocal-scoring policy safely offline; it would need to be paired with a genuine capacity-aware allocation mechanism (layer 2, e.g., LiJAR-style redistribution or the TSFD ranker also reviewed in this batch) to address the project's central wasted-likes/exposure-fairness problem.
