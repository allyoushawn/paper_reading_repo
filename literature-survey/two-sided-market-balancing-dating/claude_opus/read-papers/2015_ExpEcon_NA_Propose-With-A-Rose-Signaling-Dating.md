# Propose with a rose? Signaling in internet dating markets

- **notebook source_id:** `180f76b2`
- **extraction method:** direct PDF read (NotebookLM unavailable)

## Summary
The paper tests whether a costly-because-limited, non-binding "preference signal" increases the chance that a dating proposal is accepted, using a field experiment on a South Korean online dating company. Participants in two special dating events (613 people total) could attach a free "virtual rose" to at most one of up to ten proposals sent to opposite-sex participants; 80% of participants were endowed with two roses, 20% (randomly) with eight. The authors find that attaching a rose significantly raises acceptance probability, that participants endowed with more roses are more successful (initiate more dates), and — importantly for market design — that roses increase the *total* number of accepted dates rather than merely reallocating existing offers, i.e., signaling is not a pure zero-sum crowding-out mechanism. Despite this benefit, a substantial share of participants under-used their rose endowment or "wasted" roses on top-desirability recipients who did not need signaling to accept.

## Method
Field experiment with a scarce, free, binding-cost-by-limited-supply signal. In a 5-day proposal stage, each participant could browse profiles and send up to 10 proposals ("dating requests"), each proposal optionally carrying one virtual rose (roses are a fixed per-person endowment, either 2 or 8, randomly assigned and *not* disclosed as varying). In a subsequent 4-day response stage, recipients see each proposal and whether it carries a rose, and accept/decline (no info on other proposals' outcomes). An accepted proposal becomes a "match" (contact info exchanged). Desirability is measured via the company's own pre-existing "desirability index" (0–100, sex-specific, predictive of attractiveness as a spouse/dating partner), used to bucket participants into bottom/middle/top desirability terciles (30/40/30 split). The core empirical model regresses proposal acceptance on a rose-attached dummy plus recipient fixed effects, sender's age/verification/location controls, and desirability-group dummies for the sender (OLS with recipient FE, "FE-R"); an instrumental-variables version instruments the rose-attached dummy with the sender's randomly assigned rose-endowment treatment (2 vs. 8) to address possible endogenous rose-targeting.

## Datasets and Baselines
Field data from a major South Korean online dating company (also operating in China, Singapore, US), two special one-time dating events (July and August 2008) restricted to college-educated, never-married Koreans aged 26–38 (men) / 22–34 (women): 212 participants in session 1, 401 in session 2 (613 total, ~50% female). 1,921 total proposals sent; 295 accepted. No machine-learning baseline is used — the "baselines" are alternative regression specifications (OLS with recipient fixed effects; IV using rose-endowment treatment as instrument; logit with recipient fixed effects; restriction to only the 2-rose subgroup; restriction to recipients who responded to at least one proposal) used to check robustness of the core causal estimate, not competing recommender algorithms.

## Results
- **Baseline effect (Model A, FE-R):** attaching a rose increases acceptance probability by **3.3 percentage points** (significant at 5%), a **20% relative increase** over the overall acceptance rate. This is comparable in magnitude to being in the "middle" vs. "bottom" desirability tercile as a sender (+5pp), and to about three-quarters of the effect of being in the "top" tercile (+18pp) vs. bottom.
- **IV estimate (rose-endowment as instrument, F-stat 442.45):** effect of **4.1 percentage points**, larger than but not statistically distinguishable from the OLS estimate; the IV point estimate itself is not significant at conventional levels, but the paper argues (via a second, Model-B specification not detailed on the pages read) that endogeneity does not appear to explain away the baseline effect.
- **Raw acceptance rates:** proposals to men — 19.7% accepted without rose vs. 23.6% with rose; proposals to women — 12.3% without vs. 12.9% with (small/insignificant raw gaps; effect becomes significant only after controlling for observables).
- **Robustness:** effect is qualitatively unchanged when restricting to active recipients (column 3, FE-R "Active"), using OLS instead of within-recipient FE (column 4), using a fixed-effects logit (column 5), and restricting to the 2-rose subgroup only (column 6, 3.4pp, "virtually identical to the baseline result"). Effect magnitude is similar for men and women when estimated separately.
- **Overall acceptance rate is low:** only 295 of 1,921 proposals accepted; average number of accepted offers per recipient is 0.8 (men) / 0.7 (women); women receive on average 5.9 offers but accept only 16% of them vs. men's 29% (p<0.01) — evidence the reactive side (here, largely women in this dating-request structure) is capacity/selectivity constrained.
- **Non-strategic use of the signal:** 32% of male participants and 69% of female participants did not exhaust their rose endowments even though roses were unused inventory; ~30% of roses sent were "wasted" on top-desirability-group recipients who did not respond more positively to a rose than to a plain offer.
- Individuals who received at least one rose accepted **more** total dating offers than counterparts who received none — offered as evidence that roses increase the total number of matches rather than simply reallocating (crowding out) existing acceptances.

## Limitations
The authors themselves note: (1) the acceptance-rate difference for offers with/without roses is small and statistically insignificant in raw (non-regression-adjusted) form, and only becomes significant after controlling for observables — leaving room for concern about specification sensitivity (mitigated by their several robustness checks); (2) the overall effect estimated by the IV regression is not statistically significant at a conventional level on its own, even though point estimates are similar to OLS; (3) participants do not use roses strategically/optimally (large under-utilization and misallocation to top-desirability recipients who don't need signaling), meaning the experiment measures signaling's effect under *actual*, not *optimal*, usage — the true potential gain from preference signaling could be larger if users were educated on strategic use; (4) the environment is a decentralized dating market with a company-provided desirability index, which may not generalize to markets without such a measure or to markets (e.g., college admissions, job markets) with more institutional gatekeeping around signal interpretation.

## Project Relevance
Directly relevant to **layer 3 (market-design levers)**: the rose mechanism is a real-world implementation of a scarce, sender-limited "signal" (functionally similar to a limited super-like), and the paper's central finding — that signaling raised acceptance rates and *increased total matches rather than only reallocating them* — is strong evidence bearing on whether adding a scarce-signal feature could reduce wasted-likes/increase spread of matches on a dating platform, rather than simply reshuffling who wins. It also has secondary relevance to **layer 1 (reciprocal scoring)**, since the paper explicitly documents that responsiveness to a signal depends on the *relative* desirability of sender vs. recipient (Hypotheses 3–4: high-desirability recipients don't need signals; signals from higher- to lower-desirability senders work best) — directly analogous to reciprocal like-back-probability conditioned on relative desirability/capacity. Does not address exposure-allocation algorithms, ranking, or ecosystem-level interference metrics (layers 2 and 4).
