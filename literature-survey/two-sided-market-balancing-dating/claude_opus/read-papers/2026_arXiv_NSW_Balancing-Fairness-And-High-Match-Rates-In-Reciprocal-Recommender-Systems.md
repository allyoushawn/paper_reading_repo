# Balancing Fairness and High Match Rates in Reciprocal Recommender Systems: A Nash Social Welfare Approach

- **notebook source_id:** `6a44f3f3`
- **extraction method:** direct PDF read (NotebookLM unavailable)

## Summary
Extended (journal) version of a RecSys'24 paper on fairness in reciprocal recommender systems (RRS) for two-sided matching platforms like online dating. The authors observe that maximizing expected matches (Social Welfare, SW) concentrates recommendation exposure on popular users who cannot reciprocate everyone's interest, producing unfair "opportunity" distributions. They adapt envy-freeness from fair-division theory to define "double envy-freeness" (both sides simultaneously envy-free over their recommendation opportunities) for RRS, and propose maximizing Nash Social Welfare (NSW, the product of expected-match utilities) via an alternating Frank-Wolfe algorithm as a scalable, near-envy-free alternative to SW maximization. A Sinkhorn-based fast approximate algorithm adds scalability, plus a tunable α-SW method interpolating between SW and NSW. On synthetic data and two real dating datasets (a Japanese online dating platform, and the public Speed Dating Experiment dataset), NSW achieves near-zero envy while keeping competitive match counts, at some cost in total matches relative to pure SW maximization.

## Method
Two-sided matching market: n agents on the left (N), m agents on the right (M). Each pair (a_i, b_j) has estimated one-directional preference probabilities p̂1(i,j) and p̂2(j,i). Recommendations are doubly stochastic matrices A_i (agent a_i's ranking distribution over M) and B_j (agent b_j's ranking distribution over N). Using a position-based examination model e(k) (probability of examining the k-th ranked candidate), the probability a_i applies to b_j is p̂1(i,j)·Σ_k e(k)A_i(j,k); a match occurs only if **both** sides apply — a genuine mutual-application reciprocal mechanism, distinct from prior work that used one-sided relevance or assumed symmetric preferences. Utility U_i(A,B) = expected number of matches for agent i; Social Welfare SW(A,B) = Σ utilities (total expected matches). Fairness: an agent envies another same-side agent if it would prefer that agent's "opportunity" (the recommendation rows it receives from the other side) to its own; a policy is "double envy-free" if no agent envies another on either side. Key algorithms: (1) **SW method** — alternating maximization of total expected matches via Frank-Wolfe (non-convex, converges to a local optimum); (2) **NSW method** — alternating maximization of the Nash Social Welfare (product of utilities) via Frank-Wolfe on log NSW, proven (Theorem 5.2, building on Saito & Joachims 2022) to be approximately doubly envy-free when both sides are NSW-best; (3) **α-SW** — a tunable interpolation W^α_i(A,B) = (1/α)ΣU_i(A,B)^α that recovers SW at α=1 and converges to NSW as α→0; (4) a **Sinkhorn-algorithm** fast approximate solver (entropy-regularized optimal transport) replacing the LP subproblems, giving large scalability gains (GPU-parallelizable; ~200s vs >1,000s for LP at n=50–75, and tractable up to n=300 where LP becomes infeasible).

## Datasets and Baselines
Synthetic data: two-sided markets with m=50 fixed, n∈{50,75} (balanced/unbalanced), popularity-skew parameter λ∈{0,0.2,...,1.0} controlling how much preferences are dominated by a shared popularity ranking vs uniform-random idiosyncratic preference, under two examination functions (e(k)=1/log2(k+1) "log", e(k)=1/k "inv"). Real-world: (1) a Japanese online dating platform, 200 male + 200 female users sampled by interaction density, preferences estimated via Alternating Least Squares (ALS) matrix factorization from like/dislike and match/sorry signals; (2) the public Speed Dating Experiment dataset (Fisman et al. 2006, via Kaggle), 274 gender-0 and 277 gender-1 participants, ALS-estimated preferences from accept/reject decisions after ~10 four-minute speed-dating conversations each. Baselines: Naive (rank purely by own-side preference, ignoring reciprocity), Prod (rank by product of both sides' preferences), TU (a heuristic transferable-utility matching method from Tomita et al. 2023), IterLP (a greedy maximum-weight-matching LP baseline the authors propose), plus the paper's own SW/NSW/α-SW methods in both LP-exact and Sinkhorn-approximate forms.

## Results
Synthetic data (n=75, m=50, e=log, Table 2): at λ=0.8 (strong popularity skew), NSW achieves Expected Matches 79.6 with Left Envy 2.10±1.37 and Right Envy 0.00±0.00, vs SW's Expected Matches 90.5 with Left Envy 1728.9±29.1 and Right Envy 735.7±11.7 — SW gets ~14% more matches but roughly 800x more left-side envy and non-zero right-side envy where NSW has none. At λ=1.0, NSW: 71.3 matches, Left Envy 2.50±0.52, Right Envy 0.00±0.00 vs SW: 79.5 matches, Left Envy 2701.0±0.00, Right Envy 1176.0±0.00. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirm NSW's envy reduction vs SW is statistically significant (p<0.05, up to p<0.001 in several rows) across all tested λ. NSW is the fairest method by Gini index across λ and both examination functions; SW sits between Naive/Prod and NSW. Sinkhorn scalability (Section 8.4): NSW_Sinkhorn maintains near-zero envy up to n=300 (where LP-based NSW is computationally infeasible, capped at n≤75); Sinkhorn execution time ~200s vs >1,000s for LP at n=50–75. Real-world Japanese dating data (Table 3, e=log): NSW_LP achieves 90.39 expected matches with Envy(Men)=31, Envy(Women)=14, Gini(Men)=0.381, Gini(Women)=0.477, vs SW_LP's 111.37 matches (highest of all methods) with Envy(Men)=434, Envy(Women)=331; NSW_Sinkhorn (fully scalable variant) gets 79.04 matches with the lowest envy of all methods (Men=42, Women=19). Speed dating data (Table 4, e=log): IterLP has the highest expected matches (180.47); NSW_Sinkhorn gets 131.99 matches with by far the lowest envy (17/18) and lowest Gini (0.40/0.42) of any method.

## Limitations
Authors' own stated limitations (Section 9): (1) validated only on synthetic data plus two real datasets of modest size (200×200 and ~270×270); large-scale platform data (millions of users) remains untested; (2) even the Sinkhorn-accelerated algorithm requires substantial computation time for very large instances — further algorithmic work is needed for million-user deployment; (3) the framework assumes preference probabilities are estimated in advance and fixed, whereas in practice preferences and recommendations update incrementally as new interactions are observed — extending to an online/incremental setting is left as future work. The authors also note the SW method can fall into a poor local optimum under extreme popularity skew (λ=1.0), and that computing exact maximum Nash social welfare is NP-hard in general (only approximated here).

## Project Relevance
Strong, direct hit on Layers 1, 2, and 4. **Layer 1 (reciprocal scoring):** the mutual-application match probability (both sides must independently "apply," via a position-based examination function) is a more realistic reciprocal-scoring formalism than plain product-of-preferences fusion. **Layer 2 (capacity-aware exposure allocation):** NSW-maximization is precisely a mechanism for redistributing exposure away from over-subscribed popular users — directly analogous to LiJAR-style redistribution named in the project brief — validated to sharply cut envy/exposure-concentration at a modest cost in total matches. **Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics):** uses Gini index of match distribution and an envy-count metric, both directly reusable as ecosystem-health measures for this project (envy-count is a novel candidate metric beyond the brief's named match-Gini/wasted-likes/share-with-≥1-match list). **Gap/caveat:** the model's "capacity" is emergent from the exposure/envy formalism, not an explicit hard cap on replies per user (unlike e.g. per-user daily like/reply limits used by real platforms) — a production capacity-aware allocator would likely need to combine this NSW-style fairness objective with an explicit capacity constraint (pacing/quota) rather than relying on envy-freeness alone to prevent overload of the most-desirable users. **Note:** this file's source is the extended/journal version of Tomita & Yokoyama's RecSys'24 conference paper; this same read-papers folder also contains `2024_RecSys_NSW_Fair-Reciprocal-Recommendation-Matching-Markets.md` (source_id `ad4c549e`), which appears to be that earlier conference version by the same authors — the two files are companion pieces (extended vs. conference length) and should be read together, not treated as fully independent sources.
