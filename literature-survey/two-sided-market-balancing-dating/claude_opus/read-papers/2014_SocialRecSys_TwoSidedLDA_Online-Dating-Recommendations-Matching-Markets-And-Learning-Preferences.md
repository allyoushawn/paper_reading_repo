## Summary
Online dating recommendation must balance both sides' interests — recommending a suitor to a receiver only helps if the receiver is also likely to reply — yet most prior systems learn preferences only from self-declared profile data or the suitor's own behavior. This paper formulates online dating as a two-sided matching market (an LP-style flow optimization over reciprocal probabilities, subject to daily message-capacity constraints per user) and learns both suitor-initiate and receiver-reply preferences jointly with a Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) model trained on message-exchange behavior rather than stated profile preferences. On three months of real message data from Baihe.com (a large Chinese dating site, 2 million male-female message-exchanging pairs), incorporating the learned two-sided preference into recommendations increased the successful (replied) match rate by up to 45-48% relative to suitor-preference-only recommendations, and the learned preferences predicted replies better than users' own stated preferences.

## Method
**Two-sided matching market formulation:** maximize Σ f(s,r)·g(r,s)·x_sr over all sender-receiver pairs (s,r), where f(s,r) is the probability s initiates contact with r, g(r,s) is the probability r replies, and x_sr is the recommendation probability — subject to per-user daily capacity constraints (C_S(s): max messages user s can send; C_R(r): max messages user r should receive). This generalizes classical 1-1 stable matching (Gale-Shapley) to an LP relaxation allowing multiple simultaneous recommendations per user, bounded by daily capacity. Because separately estimating f and g from limited data hurt sample efficiency, the paper assumes f≡g (same preference function regardless of sender/receiver role) for estimation purposes.

**Learning preferences via LDA:** raw 21-feature user profiles are reduced via ChiMerge discretization plus information-gain/variable-importance feature selection to 5 relevant features (age, weight, income difference, presence of children, height difference), forming a feature-tuple vocabulary V. Each user is modeled as a "document" whose "words" are the feature tuples of everyone they've contacted; users have a latent type distribution θ over T topics (user types), each type has a distribution φ_t over feature tuples V — a standard LDA generative model fit via collapsed Gibbs sampling. The learned mixture gives f(s,r) = g(s,r) as a topic-weighted sum: Σ_t μ_t^(s)·φ_{v_r|t}, i.e., user s's probability of contacting/being interested in someone with r's feature profile, aggregated over s's latent types.

## Datasets and Baselines
Real data: Baihe.com, 200,000 users sampled November 2011 (139,482 male / 60,518 female), three months of message exchanges, 2 million distinct male-female pairs. Synthetic validation dataset: 20,000 simulated users (8 latent types, 4 per gender) with known ground-truth type-preference distributions, used because the real Baihe data has no ground truth for user "type." Comparisons (via 10-fold cross-validation on held-out sent messages): (a) random recommendation, (b) one-sided suitor-preference-only recommendation f(s,r), (c) their two-sided recommendation f(s,r)·g(r,s).

## Results
- **Synthetic validation:** LDA correctly recovers the true number of dominant user types (4 of the maximum T=10 allowed) for both genders; classifies ~99.5% of males and ~99.6% of females into the correct dominant type. Per-type precision ranges 93.3%-100%, recall 86.8%-99.9% (Tables 1-2); K-L divergence between true and learned type-preference distributions is on the order of 1e-4 to 1e-5 (very close).
- **Real Baihe data — baseline (one-sided) reply rates:** 12.2% average success rate for male suitors, 21.7% for female suitors (statistically indistinguishable from random selection, per the authors).
- **Two-sided vs. one-sided gain:** median relative improvement in reply/success rate from adding the learned receiver preference is **46.84% for male suitors** and **16.5% for female suitors** (Figure 3, box plot of the distribution of gains across users). The abstract states "up to 45%" and the introduction states "up to 48%" improvement in successful-match rate — these are the headline maximum figures for the same experiment.
- **LDA vs. stated preferences:** the LDA-learned receiver preference predicts actual replies better than the receiver's self-declared stated dating preference across message-count bins (Figure 4); exact numeric recall values are shown only in the bar chart, not stated in text.
- No p-values or confidence intervals are reported for the real-data experiments; the box plot in Figure 3 shows variance across users but no formal significance test.

## Limitations
The authors state explicitly: the framework has not been implemented or tested in a live/online system (fully offline, retrospective evaluation only); the framework has no principled way to incorporate active user *search queries*, only passive recommendation; the f≡g assumption (treating suitor-role and receiver-role preferences as the same function) is a data-driven approximation the authors acknowledge is not fully accurate — cited social-science literature documents that users behave differently as senders vs. receivers — adopted only because separate estimation hurt performance given limited data; whether more psychologically principled preference models could outperform their simple LDA model is left open. The max-min-fair variant of their optimization objective is mentioned as a "trivial" extension but not implemented or evaluated (only the max-utility objective is run).

## Project Relevance
This is one of the strongest direct hits in the corpus for the project's stated framing. It addresses **Layer 1 (reciprocal scoring)** via the f(s,r)·g(r,s) joint suitor-initiate/receiver-reply probability, learned from real message-exchange behavior rather than self-reported preferences (directly analogous to "like-back probability conditioned on the other side"). It addresses **Layer 2 (capacity-aware exposure allocation)** explicitly: the optimization is an LP with a per-user daily receiver-capacity constraint C_R(r) and sender-capacity constraint C_S(s), which is structurally the same idea as LiJAR-style redistribution — cap and reallocate exposure away from users who cannot absorb more inbound interest. Two caveats limit direct reuse: (1) the max-min-fair variant of the objective (which would more directly target "spread of matches" rather than total utility) is mentioned but never implemented or evaluated — only the max-utility LP is run; (2) the f≡g simplification collapses sender-role and receiver-role preference into one function, which cuts against the project's specific interest in a receiver-side reciprocal signal that differs from the sender's own taste. The paper does not touch **Layer 3 (market-design levers)** beyond the capacity cap itself, and does not touch **Layer 4 (ecosystem metrics/interference)** — its evaluation is purely reply-rate lift via offline cross-validation, not match-Gini, share-of-users-with-a-match, or two-sided retention.
