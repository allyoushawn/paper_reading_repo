## Summary
Centralized school-assignment systems (using the deferred acceptance algorithm) require families to costly-search and rank schools under uncertainty about market congestion (how many seats exist, how competitive admission is). Policymakers routinely disclose information — market congestion, admission cutoffs, or which schools are likely to be a good fit — to help families search, but the welfare effect of such disclosure is not obvious because search decisions create a negative externality (one family searching/applying more crowds out others). Using a large-market model with the Gale-Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm and costly sequential search, the authors prove: (1) search exhibits strategic complementarities (students search more when others search more), producing over-search in equilibrium relative to the social optimum; (2) fully disclosing market congestion/capacity is *never* welfare-optimal — a "rat race" externality dominates the private value of information; (3) disclosing each family's personally most-preferred school reduces search and congestion and is welfare-improving, while disclosing a poorly-matched (bottom-ranked) school increases congestion and can reduce welfare; (4) capping the number of applications a student can submit is a simple, robust way to raise welfare by curbing over-search.

## Method
- **Model:** a continuum of students i ∈ [0,1] and a finite set of N ≥ 2 ex-ante identical schools, each with random capacity k drawn from a known distribution μ over a finite set K (capturing uncertainty about market congestion). Each student draws an idiosyncratic valuation u_s for each school s, i.i.d. across student-school pairs from a distribution F with non-decreasing hazard rate (e.g., log-concave densities, including uniform).
- **Costly sequential search:** a student chooses how many schools n ∈ {0,...,N_0} to inspect (à la Stigler 1961); inspecting reveals n independent valuation draws; searching costs c·n. The student then submits a rank-order list of the n inspected schools.
- **Centralized assignment:** students submit rank-order lists; schools rank applicants via i.i.d. random scores (multiple tie-breaking); the student-proposing Gale-Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm produces a stable matching, characterized in the large-market limit by a single market-clearing admission cutoff p ∈ [0,1] satisfying a closed-form equation (Eq. 2.2/2.3) linking the profile of search intensities to school capacity.
- **Equilibrium (search game G):** a Nash equilibrium is a search-intensity profile where each student's chosen n maximizes her expected utility (expected assigned-school value minus search cost c·n) given the admission cutoff induced by everyone else's search behavior (Eq. 2.4-2.6).
- **Key theoretical results:** Proposition 1 — the benefit function B(n, n̄, k) is increasing/concave in own search n but non-increasing in others' search n̄ (negative search externality). Theorem 1 — under a "non-negligible capacity" assumption (Assumption 1), best responses are non-decreasing in others' search, i.e., the game is supermodular with strategic complementarities (Milgrom & Roberts 1990). Proposition 2 — Nash equilibria form a lattice with a smallest (Pareto-dominant, symmetric) and largest equilibrium; students over-search in equilibrium relative to the social planner's optimum (Proposition 3), so capping the number of *applications* (not search) at the socially optimal level n^SP is welfare-improving.
- **Information design:** the planner commits to a public "experiment" (Kamenica & Gentzkow 2011 Bayesian persuasion framework) revealing a signal about capacity k before students search; students update beliefs via Bayes' rule and then play the equilibrium search game given posterior beliefs. Theorem 2 — perfectly revealing the true capacity k is always suboptimal (an "uninformative" experiment, i.e. no disclosure, dominates full transparency about congestion) because the negative strategic externality from revealing high congestion (triggering a rat race) outweighs the private value of better-tailored search decisions. Theorem 3 — disclosing each student's personally top-ranked school (an idealized "perfect recommender") strictly reduces equilibrium search/congestion and strictly improves every student's welfare, because it resolves uncertainty about the single most valuable option without much increasing the incremental value of additional applications. Proposition 4 — the mirror-image policy, disclosing each student's *least*-valued (bottom-ranked) school, increases congestion and (for small search cost c) reduces welfare, because it cheaply adds a low-value "free" application to lists without resolving top-choice uncertainty, worsening the externality.

## Results
All "results" are theorems/propositions, not empirical numbers:
- **Theorem 1 (Strategic complementarities):** best-response search intensity is non-decreasing in the search intensity of others, under a non-negligible-capacity condition (Assumption 1).
- **Proposition 2:** the Nash equilibrium set is non-empty, has a Pareto-ranked smallest and largest symmetric equilibrium, and both are non-increasing in beliefs about capacity (first-order stochastic dominance sense).
- **Proposition 3 (Limiting applications):** for any Nash equilibrium of the unrestricted game G, individual search n_i exceeds the socially optimal n^SP; restricting the number of *applications* (but not search) to n^SP strictly raises welfare for all Nash equilibria of the restricted game G_L relative to any equilibrium of G.
- **Theorem 2 (Disclosing congestion):** for any prior belief μ, perfectly revealing the realized school capacity k is a suboptimal information policy; when the prior already induces minimum search intensity, providing no additional information is optimal.
- **Theorem 3 (Disclosing top schools):** revealing a student's most-preferred school to her strictly lowers equilibrium application-list length and strictly raises every student's welfare, relative to the baseline game with no disclosure.
- **Proposition 4 (Disclosing bottom schools):** under a uniform valuation distribution F, revealing a student's least-preferred school raises equilibrium congestion, and for search costs below some threshold c̄, strictly lowers welfare relative to no disclosure.
- **Extensions (§6):** results are shown to be robust to (a) an alternative "directed search" technology where students observe their true top-n schools rather than random draws; (b) correlated (single) tie-breaking, under which — notably — full transparency about both capacity and school quality becomes Pareto-dominant (the opposite of the baseline multiple-tie-breaking result), because search decisions become independent of others' behavior; (c) geographically correlated preferences (schools on a circle, distance-penalized utility), which preserves the qualitative results.

## Limitations
- Purely theoretical; no calibration against real school-choice or matching-market data, so magnitudes of the welfare effects are not quantified — the paper only signs the effects.
- Schools are assumed ex-ante *identical* in capacity distribution and use *purely random* applicant ranking (no meaningful reciprocal preference on the school side) — a strong simplification relative to real matching markets where the "other side" has genuine preferences.
- The large-market (continuum-of-students) assumption sidesteps finite-market strategic subtleties.
- Assumption 1 (non-negligible capacity, valuations independent across schools with monotone hazard rate) is explicitly required for the core strategic-complementarity result (Theorem 1) to hold; the authors show the result can fail without it.
- Dynamic search (students adjusting search effort based on realized past outcomes) is explicitly flagged as left for future research, not modeled here.
- The disclosure-policy results (Theorems 2-3, Proposition 4) are derived for public (not personalized-then-private) signals in the main analysis, with personalized top/bottom-school disclosure treated as idealized benchmarks assuming the planner has perfect knowledge of each family's preferences — a strong assumption relaxed only qualitatively ("our main insights are robust... when the information provided by the authority is noisy") rather than formally re-derived under realistic noise levels.

## Heavily Cited Prior Works

- Azevedo & Leshno (2016) — large-market matching model with market-clearing cutoffs, the technical framework this paper's cutoff characterization (Eq. 2.2-2.3) builds on directly.
- Milgrom & Roberts (1990) — supermodular games / strategic complementarities, the game-theoretic toolkit used to characterize and rank the Nash equilibria.
- Kamenica & Gentzkow (2011) — Bayesian persuasion / information design framework used to formalize the planner's optimal disclosure problem (public experiments, Bayes plausibility).
- Arteaga, Kapor, Neilson, Zimmerman (2022) — empirical evidence from Chile/New Haven school choice that students search/apply more when told their admission odds are low; used to motivate the strategic-complementarity assumption.
- Arnosti, Johari, et al. (2021) — congestion in decentralized matching markets, alleviated by restricting application lists — closely related mechanism (application caps) cited as precedent for Proposition 3.
- Stigler (1961) — foundational costly-search model, the basis for the paper's search-cost formulation.
- Roth (1982) — establishes incentive-compatibility (truthful reporting) of deferred acceptance, a background assumption this paper relies on.

## Project Relevance
Directly relevant to layer 3 (market-design levers) and, more loosely, layer 2 (capacity-aware exposure allocation). Proposition 3 — that capping the number of applications a searcher can submit reduces market-wide congestion and raises everyone's welfare, because search decisions are strategic complements that create a negative externality when uncapped — is a formal justification for "like limits" as a market-design lever. Theorem 2/3 and Proposition 4 together give a specific, actionable rule for the project's exposure/signaling design: showing users their personally best-fit option (a curated top-match) reduces wasteful additional search/likes and improves welfare, whereas showing users raw information about how congested/competitive the market is (e.g., "you're competing with N other likes for this person") backfires by triggering a rat race, worsening the very over-concentration-on-desirable-users problem the project's north star describes. **Disanalogy flag:** the model's "schools" have no real preferences — they rank applicants by i.i.d. random score, not by genuine mutual interest — so nothing here informs reciprocal scoring (layer 1); capacity is a fixed, non-human school-seat quota rather than a person's fluctuating human reply capacity.
