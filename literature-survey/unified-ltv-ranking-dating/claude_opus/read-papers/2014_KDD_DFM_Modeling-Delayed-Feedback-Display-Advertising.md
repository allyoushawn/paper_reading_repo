# Paper Analysis: Modeling Delayed Feedback in Display Advertising

**Source:** /Users/fox/Projects/Awesome-Deep-Learning-Papers-for-Search-Recommendation-Advertising/04_Ranking/Delayed-Feedback-Problem/2014 (Criteo) (KDD) [DFM] Modeling Delayed Feedback in Display Advertising.pdf
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-16

## 1. Summary

Olivier Chapelle, of Criteo Labs, Palo Alto, addresses conversion-rate (CVR) prediction for cost-per-action (CPA) display advertising, where a conversion can occur "up to a month" after the click that produced it. The paper's key contribution is the **Delayed Feedback Model (DFM)**: rather than picking a fixed matching/attribution window and labeling anything outside it as negative, DFM trains two generalized linear models jointly — a logistic-regression conversion model p(x) = Pr(C=1|X=x) and an exponential hazard delay model Pr(D=d|X=x,C=1) = λ(x)exp(−λ(x)d) — and treats every click without an observed conversion as **unlabeled** (censored) rather than negative. The two models are inseparable: whether a click without conversion means "will not convert" or "will convert, just not yet" is ambiguous without both models, and the paper is explicit that the resulting negative log-likelihood is non-convex (illustrated with a toy example, Figure 3, showing two competing local optima — low conversion rate / short delay vs. high conversion rate / long delay). Training uses an EM algorithm (E-step: posterior probability w_i that a still-unconverted click will eventually convert, computed in closed form from the current p(x) and λ(x); M-step: a weighted logistic regression for p jointly with a weighted exponential regression for λ, where the unlabeled samples' "survival time" is their elapsed time since click, weighted by w_i) or, alternatively, direct gradient descent with L-BFGS on the joint likelihood. The paper reports real Criteo traffic-log experiments (30-day fixed attribution window, hashed categorical + cross features, 2^24-dimensional) showing DFM reduces negative log-likelihood (NLL) versus a Naive baseline that labels all not-yet-converted clicks as negative, and versus several other engineered heuristics, with the largest gains on the most "recent" (least historically observed) campaigns — exactly where the delay problem bites hardest.

## 2. Experiment Critique

The experimental design is a single internal Criteo dataset (later released publicly at http://labs.criteo.com/tag/dataset, and this is the exact dataset later reused by the FSIW and DEFUSE papers in this batch), evaluated with a rolling-window protocol: each of 7 test days is preceded by roughly a 3-week training window (each training set has "less than 6M examples"), and results are reported both overall and restricted to "recent" campaigns (higher share of traffic from new/less-historically-observed campaigns, which the paper's own Figure 2 shows can reach 11.3% of traffic by day 26). The chosen metric is average NLL rather than AUC or PR-AUC, and the paper justifies this directly: because the predicted probability feeds an eCPM bid computation (Equation 1), the *absolute* calibration of the prediction matters, not just its ranking. This is a stronger and more relevant validity choice than a ranking-only metric would have been. The paper reports no confidence intervals or significance tests on the NLL differences (a gap later papers in this batch, e.g., FSIW and DEFUSE, close by bootstrapping 95% CIs and running t-tests) — so the ~3% overall and ~9%-to-oracle "recent" improvements should be read as point estimates without a formal significance claim. There is no online (live A/B) experiment; the entire evaluation is offline on historical logs. On reproducibility: the paper states plainly "we are not comparing our algorithm against previously published algorithms" because, to the authors' knowledge, none existed for this problem at the time — DFM's own strongest competitor is a heuristic (Short-Term Conversion, STC) the authors constructed themselves for this paper, not a prior published method. This is disclosed candidly rather than concealed.

## 3. Industry Contribution

DFM is explicitly framed for a production CPA bidding pipeline: the paper opens from the practical dilemma advertising engineering teams face — a short attribution/matching window mislabels true conversions as negative, while a long window means training on stale data, and neither choice alone is acceptable at scale. DFM's proposed fix requires no matching window at all: a click is trained as positive if a conversion is observed and otherwise as censored/unlabeled, which removes an entire class of pipeline configuration (window-length tuning) at the cost of a jointly-trained, non-convex, two-headed model. Both underlying models are simple GLMs on hashed sparse features (feature hashing to 2^24 dimensions, per Weinberger et al.), so serving cost is unchanged from an ordinary logistic-regression CTR/CVR pipeline — only p(x) is needed at serving time; λ(x) is discarded after training. The engineering cost is concentrated in training: EM is a nested optimization (each M-step itself requires an inner optimization), which the paper flags as slow to converge, and the joint gradient-descent alternative solves a non-convex objective with no guaranteed global optimum (mitigated in practice, per the paper, by having enough data). No latency, serving infrastructure, or feature-engineering pipeline details beyond feature hashing are discussed.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

This is the founding paper of the delayed-feedback-modeling literature that the rest of this batch (and much of the broader D7 sub-literature) builds on and cites. The paper states directly: "There has been several prior works on predicting conversion rates for display advertising … but none of them address the problem of conversion delay… we are unaware of any algorithm addressing the delayed feedback issue." Its explicit novelty is therefore the delay-aware joint model itself, plus the connection it draws to two existing bodies of theory: (1) **survival analysis** (Kalbfleisch & Prentice) — DFM's EM procedure is shown to reduce, in the always-eventually-converts limiting case, to a standard exponential-regression survival model with censored times; and (2) **learning from positive and unlabeled data** (Denis, Gilleron & Letouzey 2005; Elkan & Noto 2008) — the paper notes its problem differs from the standard PU-learning assumption of missing-completely-at-random labels, since here the probability of a missing label depends directly on elapsed time since click. Other heavily-cited/discussed prior works: Rosales, Cheng & Manavoglu (2012), post-click conversion modeling for non-guaranteed display advertising, the closest prior CVR-prediction work; Weinberger et al. (2009), the feature-hashing trick used for the feature representation; Nocedal (1980), L-BFGS, the optimizer used for the joint gradient-descent variant; and Agarwal, Agrawal, Khanna & Kota (2010), rare-event rate estimation with hierarchical models, cited as related prior CVR-estimation work that does not address delay.

## 7. Reference Card

1. **Title, authors, venue, year, URL:** "Modeling Delayed Feedback in Display Advertising," Olivier Chapelle, Criteo Labs, KDD 2014. http://dx.doi.org/10.1145/2623330.2623634
2. **Source type:** Industry paper (Criteo Labs; ACM KDD).
3. **Direction:** D7.
4. **Problem setting:** Post-click conversion-rate (CVR) prediction for cost-per-action (CPA) display advertising, where conversions can be observed anywhere from minutes to up to a month after the triggering click, and the predicted probability directly sets an eCPM bid.
5. **Objective and label definition:** Jointly predicts Pr(C=1|X=x) (eventual conversion probability, the training objective) and Pr(D=d|X=x,C=1) (an exponential-hazard delay model). Horizon: a fixed 30-day post-click attribution window (chosen "for the sake of simplicity"); the paper's own empirical delay distribution shows 35% of conversions within 1 hour, ~50% after 24 hours, and 13% after two weeks. Delay/censoring handled by treating every not-yet-converted click as **unlabeled** rather than negative, with an EM algorithm inferring a posterior probability that each unlabeled click will eventually convert (E-step, closed form) and jointly re-estimating both models (M-step) — structurally a censored/weighted survival-analysis regression, not a distributional label-correction or importance-sampling scheme.
6. **Prediction or incrementality:** Prediction only — the paper does not address incrementality.
7. **Model architecture:** Two generalized linear models sharing input features: logistic regression for conversion probability p(x) = 1/(1+exp(−w_c·x)), and log-linear exponential-hazard regression for the delay rate λ(x) = exp(w_d·x). Trained jointly by EM or by direct L-BFGS gradient descent on the non-convex joint negative log-likelihood.
8. **Credit assignment:** Not specified in source. The outcome unit already equals the decision unit — each conversion is attributed to a single click by an explicit post-click attribution rule (last-click attribution when multiple clicks precede a conversion; only the first of several matching conversions is kept). Delay here is purely a time-axis censoring problem, not a user-level-to-item-level aggregation problem.
9. **Training data and counterfactual handling:** Real Criteo traffic logs (see Dataset Availability); no counterfactual/importance-sampling correction is used — censoring is handled entirely through the EM posterior-weight mechanism described above. The paper does not claim or prove formal (asymptotic) unbiasedness of this estimator; it is presented and evaluated purely as a maximum-likelihood procedure, validated empirically via NLL rather than a stated consistency theorem.
10. **Offline and online evaluation:** Offline only — average NLL on a global test set and on a "recent campaigns" subset, compared against Naive, Oracle, Shifted (30-day-old training set), STC (a two-model short-term-conversion heuristic the authors construct), and Rescale (Naive divided by the global missing-label probability) baselines. No online (live A/B) evaluation is reported.
11. **Reported gains:** On the Criteo traffic-log dataset (Table 1), DFM reduces overall NLL from 0.4076 (Naive) to 0.3960 (an ~3% relative improvement, cited directly in the paper's Results section), versus an Oracle lower bound of 0.3889. On the "recent campaigns" subset, DFM's NLL of 0.4006 is closer to Oracle's 0.3640 than any other baseline (gap-to-oracle of 0.0366 vs. 0.0477 for the next-best method, STC).
12. **Applicability to a two-sided dating recommender:** Single-sided advertising conversion, one click to one conversion, with no reciprocity, congestion, or two-sided fairness treatment. The transferable core — an EM-based censoring correction, treating "not yet converted" as unlabeled rather than negative — is conceptually the closest fit in this batch to a 30-day retention label, but it requires the exponential-hazard assumption, and the paper's own empirical delay distributions (Figure 5) show the exponential fit systematically mismatching both very short and very long delays, a scale of hours-to-two-weeks far below the weeks-long revenue horizon this project needs.
13. **Unverified claims:** The paper states its exponential delay model "fits quite well the empirical delay distributions," but its own Figure 5 shows the fit is imperfect — under-predicting short delays and over-predicting long ones, with the authors themselves noting "the delay distribution is a mixture of a short delay and a long one" — so the goodness-of-fit claim is self-qualified, not independently verified with a formal test. The claim of non-convexity being a non-issue "in our experiments" is an empirical observation on this dataset only, not a general guarantee.

## Project Relevance

This paper speaks most directly to **Q3** (label and horizon definitions for delayed outcomes, and how delay/censoring are handled) — it is the foundational reference the rest of the D7 sub-literature (including FSIW, DEFUSE, and ESDF in this same batch) explicitly builds on or contrasts against. It does not address **Q1** (the objective here is conversion prediction, not retention/LTV), **Q2** (no user-level-to-item-level credit-assignment problem — the click-to-conversion attribution is already one-to-one), **Q4–Q5** (no fusion of short/long-term heads, no incrementality), or **Q7** (no two-sided/reciprocal market treatment). Its main transferable value for the dating-app project is methodological: the EM censoring mechanism is the cleanest illustration in this batch of "treat the not-yet-converted user as unlabeled, not negative," which is exactly the project's 30-day retention situation — but the mechanism's reliance on an unverified exponential-hazard assumption, evaluated only at hours-to-weeks scale, means it cannot be adopted for a multi-week retention/revenue label without first checking whether that hazard assumption holds at that horizon.
