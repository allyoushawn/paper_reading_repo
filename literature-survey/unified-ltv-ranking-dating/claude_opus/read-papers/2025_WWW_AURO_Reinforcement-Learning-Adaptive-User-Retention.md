# Paper Analysis: AURO: Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive User Retention Optimization in Recommender Systems

**Source:** /Users/fox/Projects/paper_reading_repo/literature-survey/unified-ltv-ranking-dating/claude_opus/pdfs/2310.03984.pdf
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-17

## 1. Summary

Zhenghai Xue (Nanyang Technological University), Qingpeng Cai (Kuaishou Technology, corresponding author), Bin Yang, Lantao Hu, Peng Jiang (Kuaishou Technology), Kun Gai (unaffiliated), and Bo An (NTU / Skywork AI), "AURO: Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive User Retention Optimization in Recommender Systems," WWW 2025 (The Web Conference, Sydney, April 28-May 2, 2025). AURO addresses non-stationarity in RL-based recommender retention optimization: user behavior patterns — immediate-feedback ratios (click/like/follow/comment/hate) and return-time distributions — drift measurably across dates, hours, and weeks (empirically shown in Fig. 2 from live-platform data), which destabilizes RL policies trained under a stationary-MDP assumption and creates an "implicit cold start" problem whenever the policy meets a new behavior pattern. AURO formalizes the recommendation task as a Hidden-Parameter MDP (HiP-MDP), where an unobserved parameter θ governs the current environment dynamics and reward distribution, and introduces two components on top of a TD3 (twin-delayed DDPG) actor-critic backbone: (1) a **state abstraction module** trained with a novel value-based contrastive loss that aligns its output φ(s) with the policy's own state-value function V(s) — states with similar V are pulled together in abstraction space, dissimilar-V states pushed apart — giving a "universal" signal of environment drift grounded in policy performance itself, rather than requiring an explicit hidden-parameter identification like the prior context-encoder baseline ESCP; and (2) a **guarded/safe exploration module** addressing implicit cold start via optimism under uncertainty: it generates several candidate exploration actions along the estimated upper-confidence-bound Q-value's gradient at varying step sizes and selects the one with the highest optimistic Q-value via rejection sampling, rather than committing to one fixed-step-size action (which the paper shows empirically can miss local optima of a highly non-convex, multi-modal Q-landscape, Fig. 4). The state-abstraction output φ(s) is concatenated onto the raw state as additional input to both actor and critic. Across a modified KuaiSim retention simulator, an offline MovieLens-1M evaluation, and a 14-day live A/B test on a real short-video platform (~25 million daily active users), AURO outperforms ten RL and non-RL baselines (CEM, DIN, DDPG, TD3, SAC, OAC, RND, ESCP, RLUR, OSPIA) on retention-specific metrics, and in the live test improves the 7-day retention rate by 0.138‰ (vs. 0.041‰ for TD3 and 0.123‰ for ESCP), while also achieving the largest drop in dislike/"unlike" rate among the compared algorithms — evidence, the authors argue, that AURO surfaces genuinely preferred content rather than clickbait.

## 2. Experiment Critique

The paper triangulates across three complementary evaluation settings — a purpose-built RL retention simulator, an offline counterfactual replay on a public dataset, and a genuine live online A/B test — which is unusually thorough for this literature. Simulator experiments (KuaiSim, Zhao et al. 2023) run five random seeds and report mean ± standard deviation (Table 2, Fig. 5), reasonable practice given RL's high run-to-run variance. The offline MovieLens-1M evaluation uses TD3+BC-style conservative training (behavior-cloning regularization to avoid distribution shift from a fixed logged dataset) and Normalised Capped Importance Sampling (NCIS, a standard off-policy counterfactual estimator) to produce a fair comparative retention-reward estimate (Table 3) without further online interaction. The live 14-day A/B test on ~25 million DAU compares AURO against only RLUR (the primary baseline), with TD3 and ESCP run in parallel buckets; the authors explicitly justify this narrower online comparison by the cost-sensitivity of live experiments and the risk of degrading real users' experience with unsafe or unstable policies — reasonable given the domain, but it does mean the live results validate AURO as a whole rather than isolating which of its two components (state abstraction vs. guarded exploration) drives the online gain; that isolation is only done offline, in the simulator ablations (Table 2, right). The paper states "all reported improvements are statistically significant under repeated daily measurements" but does not name the specific significance test used, and reports permillage effect sizes with ± ranges rather than p-values throughout. Reproducibility is a genuine strength: code is stated to be available on GitHub, all hyperparameters are tabulated (Table 5), and full loss-function derivations are given in Appendix A. The authors themselves state two concrete limitations in Appendix C ("Algorithmic Limitations and Failure Cases"): the paper does not address combining the retention reward with immediate-feedback rewards (left as future work), and the approach can underperform for users who exhibit unprecedented, sudden behavior shifts (e.g., abruptly disliking everything the state-abstraction module has not seen) or "stubborn" users unfamiliar with any offered content, where the guarded exploration module may still yield inferior performance relative to simple greedy recommendation.

## 3. Industry Contribution

AURO is deployed and A/B-tested on a live short-video platform (Kuaishou-affiliated, ~25 million daily active users, "billions of interactions" per day) as one stage of a larger candidate-ranking pipeline: the RL policy's k-dimensional action reweights a pretrained scoring model's per-candidate score vector, a fixed ranking function combines them into a final score, and downstream item filtering removes unsafe or illegal content before serving — this is real production-adjacent engineering, not simulation only. On computational cost, Appendix D.3 reports that AURO's exploration-action-selection step adds roughly 129% more compute to the action-selection sub-step specifically, but only about 9% more total training-step time (since environment interaction and gradient updates dominate wall-clock time), and the exploration module adds no inference-time cost since it is training-only. The architecture itself is comparatively lightweight (2 transformer layers, 64-dimensional feedforward network, 4 attention heads per Table 5) relative to today's largest industrial rankers, a reasonable production-latency-conscious design choice, though it also means the reported gains have not been shown to hold at much larger backbone scale.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

Claimed novelty: (1) a state-abstraction module trained via a new value-based contrastive loss that aligns abstraction-space distance with state-value-function similarity, framed as a "universal" (policy-performance-grounded, rather than explicit-hidden-parameter-grounded) signal of environment non-stationarity — explicitly contrasted against ESCP (Luo, Zhang, Chen, Ji, Yu & Yang, "Adapt to Environment Sudden Changes by Learning a Context Sensitive Policy," AAAI 2022), the meta-RL context-encoder baseline that "explicitly identifies hidden parameters as additional policy inputs" and which AURO's authors describe as unreliable or inefficient when applied directly to this cost-sensitive recommendation setting; (2) a guarded exploration procedure combining optimism-under-uncertainty (upper-confidence-bound Q-value gradient ascent, building on Ciosek, Vuong, Loftin & Hofmann, "Better Exploration with Optimistic Actor-Critic," NeurIPS 2019) with rejection sampling over multiple candidate step sizes, addressing the specific empirically-shown failure mode that a single fixed step size can select an action worse than the unperturbed policy output on a non-convex Q-landscape (Fig. 4). The most heavily cited/compared prior works are: RLUR (Cai, Liu, Zhang, Liu, Cai, Hu, Jiang, Gai, "Reinforcing User Retention in a Billion Scale Short Video Recommender System," WWW 2023 — already a core reference in this survey, and the sole baseline in AURO's own live A/B test); OSPIA (a one-step policy-improvement algorithm biasing recommendations toward higher long-term engagement metrics); ESCP (the context-encoder meta-RL comparison point, as above); TD3 (Fujimoto, van Hoof & Meger, "Addressing Function Approximation Error in Actor-Critic Methods," ICML 2018 — the base off-policy actor-critic algorithm AURO builds on); SAC (Haarnoja, Zhou, Abbeel & Levine, ICML 2018 — a stochastic maximum-entropy RL baseline); OAC (Ciosek et al. 2019, the specific optimism-under-uncertainty formulation AURO's exploration module extends); and KuaiSim (Zhao, Liu, Cai, Zhao, Liu, Zheng, Jiang & Gai, "KuaiSim: A Comprehensive Simulator for Recommender Systems," NeurIPS 2023 — the retention simulator used for AURO's main experiments, itself a prior Kuaishou publication).

## 7. Reference Card

1. **Title, authors, venue, year, URL:** "AURO: Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive User Retention Optimization in Recommender Systems," Zhenghai Xue, Qingpeng Cai, Bin Yang, Lantao Hu, Peng Jiang, Kun Gai, Bo An, Proceedings of the ACM Web Conference 2025 (WWW '25), Sydney, Australia. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03984
2. **Source type:** Industry paper (Kuaishou Technology, deployed and A/B-tested live), with academic co-authors (Nanyang Technological University / Skywork AI).
3. **Direction:** D2.
4. **Problem setting:** Optimizing user retention with reinforcement learning in a large-scale short-video recommender, under environment non-stationarity caused by continually evolving user behavior patterns (interaction-type ratios and return-time distributions drift over dates, hours, and weeks), which destabilizes standard RL policies and creates an implicit cold-start problem whenever the policy meets a new behavior pattern.
5. **Objective and label definition:** The training objective is retention itself, not a proxy: reward r_θ(s0,a0,...,sT) = λ × (user return time), where λ = -0.1, assigned **only at the last (terminal) step of a session/episode** and zero at every other step. Return time is measured in days; evaluation metrics include average return days, return probability at day 1, and — in the live deployment — 7-day retention rate. No explicit right-censoring or survival model is used; the RL formulation's ongoing training implicitly handles long or unresolved returns rather than a fixed observation window with censoring correction.
6. **Prediction or incrementality:** Prediction/policy optimization of an outcome (expected discounted retention reward under the learned policy) rather than a causal-effect estimate of a specific exposure — the paper does not frame its objective in potential-outcomes or treatment-effect terms; it maximizes expected accumulated return under a Hidden-Parameter MDP, which is a sequential-decision-optimization framing, not an incrementality/uplift framing.
7. **Model architecture:** TD3 (twin-delayed deep deterministic policy gradient) actor-critic backbone, extended with (a) a state abstraction network φ(s) trained via a value-based contrastive loss (Eq. 1-5) that clusters states by their estimated state-value V(s) and aligns φ(s) accordingly, concatenated onto the raw state as additional actor/critic input; and (b) a guarded exploration module that generates candidate exploration actions along the gradient of an upper-confidence-bound Q-value estimate at several step sizes and selects among them via rejection sampling (Eq. 6-7). State is encoded from user features, item features, and item interaction history via embedding networks and a transformer (2 layers, 4 attention heads, 64-dim feedforward). The RL policy outputs a 7-dimensional continuous action that reweights a pretrained scoring model's per-item score vector; a fixed ranking function combines them into the final ranking score.
8. **Credit assignment:** The retention reward is placed entirely at the terminal step of a session (zero everywhere else); credit is then propagated backward to every earlier state-action pair in the episode through standard TD3 temporal-difference/Bellman bootstrapping with discount factor γ=0.9 — an implicit, learned credit-assignment mechanism rather than an explicit attribution rule. The state-abstraction module's own training signal is the state-value function V(s) itself, so the accuracy of this backward-propagated credit is central to how the system detects and adapts to environment drift. This is the most directly worked example in this batch of a delayed, user-level outcome being credited back to individual earlier item-level (impression/action) decisions.
9. **Training data and counterfactual handling:** Three modes: (a) online interaction with a purpose-built, live-recalibrated RL simulator (KuaiSim); (b) offline replay on MovieLens-1M using TD3+BC conservative offline RL with Normalised Capped Importance Sampling (NCIS) for counterfactual correction against the fixed logged policy; (c) genuine live online interaction (on-policy RL, no counterfactual correction needed) in the 14-day A/B test.
10. **Offline and online evaluation:** Offline — simulator metrics (average return days, return probability at day 1, retention reward, Table 2) and MovieLens-1M offline-RL retention reward via NCIS (Table 3). Online — 14-day live A/B test on a real short-video platform against an RLUR baseline (with TD3 and ESCP run in parallel), metrics: 7-day retention rate, dwell time, click-through rate, like rate, comment rate, unlike/dislike rate (Table 4).
11. **Reported gains:** In the KuaiSim retention simulator at 20K training steps, AURO achieves an average return of 1.531 days (lower is better) vs. 1.726 for the next-best baseline OSPIA, a return probability at day 1 of 0.824 vs. 0.803 for RLUR, and a retention reward of -0.015 vs. -0.017 for RLUR (Table 2). On MovieLens-1M, AURO+BC achieves the highest retention reward of 1.798 ± 0.032 among offline baselines including TD3+BC (1.714 ± 0.054) and RLUR+BC (1.784 ± 0.060) (Table 3). In the live 14-day A/B test (short-video platform, RLUR baseline), AURO improves 7-day retention rate by 0.138‰ (vs. TD3's 0.041‰ and ESCP's 0.123‰), dwell time by 0.263‰, CTR by 3.260‰, like rate by 2.821‰, and comment rate by 8.392‰ — nearly 5x TD3's comment-rate improvement — while achieving the largest reduction in unlike/hate rate (-1.874‰) among the three compared algorithms (Table 4).
12. **Applicability to a two-sided dating recommender:** AURO's specific techniques (state abstraction for detecting behavior-distribution drift; guarded exploration for implicit cold start) target a single-sided short-video feed with no reciprocity or congestion treatment. However, its retention-reward structure — a single delayed terminal signal (return time) credited backward via TD/Bellman bootstrapping to all earlier impression-level actions in a session — is the most directly transferable mechanism in this batch to the dating app's exact problem: a delayed, multi-week retention outcome that must be credited back to specific impression, like, and match decisions.
13. **Unverified claims:** No p-values or named significance test are reported for the simulator/MovieLens comparisons (only mean ± std across five seeds); the live A/B test's "statistically significant" claim is asserted without specifying the test. The interpretation that AURO's large drop in unlike/hate rate demonstrates it surfaces genuinely preferred (non-clickbait) content is an inference from a cross-algorithm correlation pattern in Table 4, not a directly measured causal decomposition, and should be read as the authors' interpretation.

## Project Relevance

This paper speaks most directly to **Q1** (the training objective is retention itself — a real-valued reward proportional to negative return-time — rather than a CTR-like proxy), **Q2** (credit assignment: a single terminal-step delayed reward is propagated backward across an entire session's impression-level actions via TD3's Bellman bootstrapping — the clearest worked example of delayed-outcome-to-item-decision credit assignment anywhere in this batch), **Q3** (label = user return time in days; reward horizon is effectively session-to-next-visit, separately validated at day-1 return probability and, in live deployment, 7-day retention rate; no explicit censoring model), and **Q6** (a three-tier validation template — simulator, offline counterfactual replay via NCIS, and a genuine 14-day live A/B test — for how to de-risk a retention-objective model before full launch). It says less about **Q4** (the RL policy reweights a separately pretrained scoring model's outputs via a fixed ranking function, rather than learning a joint short-term/long-term head architecture itself) and does not address **Q7** (no reciprocity, congestion, or two-sided fairness treatment) or **Q8** (no migration-path narrative from an existing non-RL CTR/CVR production system, since all comparisons are against other RL algorithms). "Adaptive" in the paper's title refers to adapting to non-stationary environment/behavior-distribution drift over time (dates, hours, weeks), detected via a state-value-grounded abstraction signal computed per state (and therefore implicitly per user-context) — not to personalizing the retention objective per individual user profile; the adaptivity signal is the policy's own estimated performance (the state-value function V(s)), not an explicit per-user or per-cohort indicator.
