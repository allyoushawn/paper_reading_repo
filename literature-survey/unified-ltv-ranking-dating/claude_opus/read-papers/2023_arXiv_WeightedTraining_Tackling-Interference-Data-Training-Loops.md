# Paper Analysis: Tackling Interference Induced by Data Training Loops in A/B Tests: A Weighted Training Approach

**Source:** `/Users/fox/Projects/paper_reading_repo/literature-survey/unified-ltv-ranking-dating/claude_opus/pdfs/2310.17496.pdf` (arXiv:2310.17496v5)
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-17

## 1. Summary

Nian Si (Booth School of Business, University of Chicago) formalizes a potential-outcomes framework for **user-side** A/B tests in which the machine learning model itself is continuously retrained on data logged during the experiment — the "data training loop." At time t, model M_t is trained as M_t=F(M_{t-1},D_{t-1}), and D_{t-1} is pooled across both treatment and control users: D_E = p·D_T + (1−p)·D_C. Because this pooled distribution differs from either the pure-treatment or pure-control distribution, the retrained model M_t differs from what it would be under a counterfactual global-treatment or global-control regime, biasing the naive treatment-effect estimator — **even when each individual user's outcome is causally independent of other users' treatment assignments** (no direct SUTVA violation at the user level; the bias is entirely mediated through the shared retrained model).

To fix this, the paper introduces **weighted training**: rather than the "data pooling" baseline (train one model on all pooled data — biased) or the "data splitting"/"data-diverted" baseline of Holtz et al. (2023) (train two models, each exclusively on its own arm's data — unbiased in expectation but data-inefficient and high-variance), weighted training trains two models (M_θT, M_θC) using the *same* pooled data but with per-example loss weights W_T(X_E,Y_E,Z)=E[Z|X_E]/p and W_C=(1−E[Z|X_E])/(1−p). Lemma 1 proves these weights exactly recover the treatment and control distributions in expectation (W_T·D_E =^d D_T, W_C·D_E =^d D_C), and Theorem 1 proves this specific weighting is the **minimum-variance** choice among all weight functions satisfying that no-distributional-shift constraint. Since E[Z|X_E] is unknown, it is estimated online by a small auxiliary classifier G_θW, trained jointly alongside the two production models (Algorithm 1). Extensive Monte Carlo simulations (a two-video-type recommendation environment, N=100 candidates per user, T=10,000 time steps, 1.28M total users, 100 independent A/B-test replications) compare weighted training against data pooling, snapshot (no retraining), and data splitting on bias, variance, and a simulated A/A test for Type-I error.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design.** Purely simulation-based: a controlled Monte Carlo environment with known ground-truth parameters (finishing rate and stay duration for short vs. long videos, generated by specified logistic/exponential models with deliberate model misspecification), 100 independent replications per configuration, at two treatment probabilities (p=1/2 and the more realistic p=0.2), plus a separate A/A-test study (α_C=α_T=10, p=1/2) to check Type-I error control. No real production A/B test is reported anywhere in the paper — this is the clearest limitation, explicitly a step behind this batch's companion paper (Zhu, Cai, Zheng, and Si, 2024), which validates its (related but distinct) feedback-loop claim on live Tencent production data.

**Statistical validity.** Table 1 (p=1/2) and Table 3 (p=0.2) report bias, empirical standard deviation (STD, across the 100 replications), and per-experiment standard error (SE, from a two-sample t-test) for each of three metrics. The paper explicitly flags a real statistical hazard here: for the data-splitting baseline, "the true variance... is considerably larger than the standard error estimated from a two-sample t-test," meaning naive SE-based confidence intervals on a data-split experiment would understate the true uncertainty — a genuine, quantified methodological warning. The A/A-test study (Table 5) reports weighted training has elevated Type-I error for one metric (0.45 for "proportion of short videos" vs. nominal 0.05), attributed (without rigorous diagnosis) to that metric's sensitivity to the burn-in period before the auxiliary weighting model G_θW is trained; data splitting fares far worse across all three metrics (0.94/0.65/0.60 Type-I error), which the authors state "suggest[s] that new inference methods should be developed" for that baseline.

**Online experiments.** None. This is a simulation-only paper.

**Reproducibility.** Full algorithm pseudocode (Algorithm 1), all simulation ground-truth parameters (β coefficients for finishing rate and stay duration, learning rate 0.1, batch size 128, T=10,000, auxiliary-model architecture: 2-hidden-layer 64-neuron MLP with ReLU, Adam optimizer, lr=0.001), and closed-form estimator derivations are given in the paper text — highly reproducible from the paper alone, though no code repository is referenced.

**Overall.** A rigorous theoretical contribution (Lemma 1 and Theorem 1 give an exact optimality proof for the proposed weighting) validated only in a synthetic environment; the two acknowledged open problems — the two-model training cost, and unresolved inference/variance-estimation methodology — are stated candidly by the author as future work, not resolved in this paper.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability.** A drop-in modification to an existing continuously-retraining recommender pipeline: keep the two production models (treatment-candidate and control-candidate) as under data splitting, but retrain both on the *full* pooled data stream with reweighted losses instead of only on their own arm's data, and additionally train one small auxiliary classifier online to estimate the propensity weight. No change to the randomization mechanism or serving path is required.

**Problems solved.** Removes the forced choice between naive pooled retraining (silently contaminated by the other arm's data — the paper measures this at a −0.059 bias on stay duration at p=1/2, the largest bias of any method tested) and data splitting (unbiased-ish but "lacks data efficiency" and shows "significantly higher variance" and worse experimentation cost per Table 2, since each model only sees a p or 1−p fraction of the full traffic).

**Engineering cost.** Still requires training two full production models simultaneously (the same 2x cost as data splitting) plus one small auxiliary model — not cheaper in absolute terms, though it uses 100% of available data for each model rather than a p/(1−p) split. The paper explicitly names "single model training" as unresolved future work, precisely because the current 2-3-model requirement is expensive "especially when dealing with large machine learning models," and the paper separately notes that platforms running "thousands of experiments each day" cannot ignore this multiplied training cost.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty.** First formalization of a potential-outcomes model specific to "data training loop" interference — bias induced purely through a continuously-retrained shared model, with no direct user-to-user interference — and the first weighting scheme proven (Theorem 1) to be minimum-variance among all weightings that avoid shifting the recovered treatment/control training distributions.

**Prior work.** Holtz, Brennan, and Pouget-Abadie, "A study of 'symbiosis bias' in A/B tests of recommendation algorithms," arXiv:2309.07107 (2023) — introduces the closely related "symbiosis bias" concept and the cluster-randomized and "data-diverted" (= this paper's "data splitting" baseline) designs used as the primary comparison point. Jadidinejad, Macdonald, and Ounis, "Using exploration to alleviate closed loop effects in recommender systems," SIGIR 2020, and Chaney, Stewart, and Engelhardt, "How algorithmic confounding in recommendation systems increases homogeneity and decreases utility," RecSys 2018 — foundational recommender-system feedback-loop literature, cited as background but addressing model-quality degradation rather than experiment bias per se. Zhu, Cai, Zheng, and Si, "Seller-side experiments under interference induced by feedback loops in two-sided platforms," arXiv:2401.15811 (2024) — the direct companion paper in this batch, sharing an author (Si) and citing this paper as prior work; it studies a sibling but mechanistically distinct interference source (a pacing algorithm's internal state, not model retraining — see Project Relevance). Ha-Thuc, Dutta, Mao, Wood, and Liu, "A counterfactual framework for seller-side A/B testing on marketplaces," SIGIR 2020, and Nandy, Venugopalan, Lo, and Chatterjee, "A/B testing for recommender systems in a two-sided marketplace," NeurIPS 2021 — cited as the seller-side counterpart problem, addressed by a structurally different design (counterfactual interleaving) rather than model reweighting. Kingma and Ba, "Adam: A method for stochastic optimization," 2014 — technical reference for the optimizer used.

## Project Relevance

This is the batch's most direct answer to **Q6** for the specific failure mode the batch brief flags as the subtlest and most important: interference through the training data itself, where a unified model retrained on logged experimental data silently inherits the treatment's own effects and contaminates the control arm through the shared model. Precision note distinguishing it from this batch's companion paper (Zhu, Cai, Zheng, and Si, 2024, arXiv:2401.15811): that paper's feedback loop runs through a *pacing algorithm's internal state* (cumulative budget/exposure consumption) reacting within a fixed model, with no model retraining involved; this paper's feedback loop runs through *retraining the ML model itself* on pooled experimental data. Both are real, distinct mechanisms a unified retention model could face — this paper's is the more directly applicable one if the project's staged migration plan (Q8) involves continuously retraining the unified model during a live A/B rollout, since exactly that retraining loop is what the paper's weighted-training method targets. The paper does not address Q1–Q5, Q7, or Q8 as ML-modeling questions beyond this narrow evaluation-bias point: there is no retention/revenue label design, no reciprocity or congestion treatment (it is explicitly a user-side, not candidate-side, design), and no migration-path guidance beyond "reweight your retraining loss."
