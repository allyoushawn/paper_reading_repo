# Paper Analysis: Managing Congestion in Two-Sided Platforms: The Case of Online Rentals

**Source:** arXiv:2308.14703 (preliminary draft, dated 2023-08-22), econ.GN. `/Users/fox/Projects/paper_reading_repo/literature-survey/unified-ltv-ranking-dating/claude_opus/pdfs/2308.14703.pdf`
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-17

## 1. Summary

Caterina Calsamiglia (ICREA, IPEG, IZA), Laura Doval (Columbia University), Alejandro Robinson-Cortés (University of Exeter), and Matthew Shum (Caltech) study how a platform's ranking algorithm can itself be a source of unnecessary congestion in a two-sided room-rental marketplace. Using proprietary click/request data from "one of the largest peer-to-peer rental markets in the world for medium-term rooms and apartments" (searches and listings restricted to Barcelona for the analysis), they show that the platform's status-quo ranking algorithm — which determines a *partial* order by simple criteria (date posted, whether the landlord's bank account is linked/"registered") and then breaks ties using **the same random number across all renters**, making the final room order invariant across users — induces renters to view, click, and request a narrow, repeated set of rooms while leaving comparably valuable, lower-ranked rooms unrequested.

**Methodology.** Users' room *requests* are modeled as a rank-ordered logit with ties (Beggs, Cardell, and Hausman, 1981; Allison and Christakis, 1994): utility U_is = X1sβ1 + X2sβ2 + (X2s⊗Zi)βXZ + εis (extreme-value shocks), where X1s (price, tenant count, location) is visible pre-click and X2s (amenities, days listed, landlord-preferred renter attributes) is visible only after clicking. Because a user may request more than one clicked room, the data reveal only a *partial order* (chosen utilities exceed non-chosen, no ranking within either group) — estimated via maximum likelihood. Users' *clicks* are modeled as a second rank-ordered-logit-with-ties stage whose propensity depends on position (a squared-polynomial function of rank with point masses at the first three positions) and the *expected* request utility conditional on pre-click information (projected from stage 1's fitted values), interacted with position. Estimation proceeds backward: fit the request-utility model first, project X2s onto X1s and position to get expected utility, then fit the click-propensity model using that projected utility as a covariate.

**Data.** 100% of Barcelona-area listings and searches, drawn from a random 10% sample of all platform users over a two-year window (January 2018–February 2020), restricted to users who predominantly search Barcelona (≥75% of searches) and made at least one request. Final estimation sample: 1,202 users, 45,462 rooms, over 2 million search results, an average of 144 searches, 75 clicks, and 7 requests per user.

**Counterfactual simulation.** The authors introduce a continuum of ranking algorithms indexed by α∈[0,1], mixing a "fully personalized" order (α=1, ranked purely by each user's estimated preferences) with a "random order" (α=0, fully randomized within each search), using estimated model parameters but *not* re-drawing utility shocks (a stated conservative choice that likely understates the benefit of personalization, since it captures only vertical, not the model's full horizontal, preference variation). Congestion is measured via Lorenz curves and Gini-type concentration coefficients over per-room totals of appearances, clicks, and requests.

**Main results.** Motivating evidence: under the actual (status-quo) ranking, the top 20% of rooms by search-result appearance account for ~65% of all search-result impressions, ~70% of all clicks, and — most strikingly — **100% of all requests** (i.e., 80% of rooms never receive a single request). Counterfactually, full personalization (α=1) substantially *increases* congestion relative to the status quo, while pure randomization (α=0) only mildly *decreases* it, because estimated renter preferences are found to be primarily **vertically** differentiated (most users agree on which rooms are best) rather than horizontally differentiated — a finding the authors explicitly note echoes Chen, Hsieh, and Lin (2023), who found the same pattern of excess congestion when surfacing dating prospects by predicted preference order. The central quantitative finding: the status-quo (data) ranking's realized (utility, congestion) point lies strictly **below** the possibility frontier traced out by α — meaning the platform could raise average requested-room utility at the same congestion level, or lower congestion at the same utility level, simply by choosing a different point on the α continuum (the data ranking corresponds to an implied α of roughly 0.34 for clicks and 0.25 for requests, but under-performs what that congestion level could deliver). A hypothetical "garbling" exercise — randomly relabeling room IDs across searches to destroy cross-user correlation in preferences while preserving each search's utility distribution — eliminates the trade-off entirely: personalization then raises utility with no congestion cost, a "free lunch" that the authors attribute to the resulting extreme horizontal differentiation.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design.** A genuine structural discrete-choice estimation on the platform's full historical click/request panel, not a lab or survey study. The two-stage backward estimation (request model first, then click model using projected expected utility) is methodologically standard for rank-ordered-logit-with-ties settings and is applied consistently across both the request and click decisions.

**Statistical validity.** The core econometric results (Tables 3–4, 7–8, 9–10 in the full paper) are maximum-likelihood point estimates with standard errors; several coefficients (price, gender/age/occupation preference indicators, days-listed) are statistically significant at conventional levels. The counterfactual comparison across ranking algorithms is *not* itself a statistical test — it is a simulation exercise using point estimates, without propagating parameter uncertainty into the reported Lorenz curves or utility-congestion frontiers.

**Online experiments.** None. This is entirely an offline structural-econometric exercise: preferences are estimated from historical data, and every ranking-algorithm comparison (data ranking vs. random vs. personalized vs. intermediate α) is a model-based counterfactual simulation, never a live A/B test on the platform.

**Reproducibility.** Model specification, sample construction, and estimation procedure are described in detail (Section 2.3); the paper is explicit that its own robustness extension (Appendix A.1, clustering users by search-filter behavior via k-medoids to capture unobserved preference heterogeneity) is preliminary and "still need[s] to be updated," per the authors' own footnote. Underlying data are proprietary to the platform and not stated to be released.

**Overall.** The core congestion finding (Lorenz-curve concentration under an invariant ranking) is descriptive and robust; the causal claim that congestion is *caused* by ranking-algorithm design (rather than, e.g., genuine underlying scarcity of good rooms) rests on the counterfactual simulation's structural assumptions — most importantly, that changing the ranking algorithm would not change users' underlying search behavior (number of searches conducted), a simplifying assumption the authors state explicitly rather than test.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability.** High for the diagnostic (any platform can compute Lorenz curves / Gini concentration on its own click and request logs to check whether its ranking is invariant-induced congestion); moderate for the fix, since implementing an α-mixed personalized/random ranking, or determining the platform-specific optimal α, requires the platform to first estimate a rank-ordered-logit-with-ties preference model from its own click/request data — a real modeling investment, not a drop-in heuristic.

**Problems solved.** Gives a concrete, quantified answer to "is our ranking algorithm itself creating congestion, and by how much" and shows that the specific mechanism — an invariant ranking with shared random tie-breaking across all users — is sufficient by itself to concentrate demand even without any inherent scarcity of comparably good options.

**Engineering cost.** Moderate: requires (1) instrumenting click and request logs at the search-result level with position, (2) fitting a two-stage rank-ordered-logit-with-ties model (standard but non-trivial econometric infrastructure), and (3) replacing a shared-random-tie-break invariant ranking with a per-user personalized-order mechanism, parameterized by a tunable mixing weight α that the platform must choose based on its own utility-congestion frontier.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty.** First paper (per the authors) to use rich, real click-and-request data from a two-sided room-rental platform to *quantify* the trade-off between ranking personalization and congestion, and to show empirically that the platform's actual ranking choice is inefficient (lies below the achievable utility-congestion frontier) — rather than merely arguing theoretically that such a trade-off could exist.

**Prior work.** Fradkin, "Search, matching, and the role of digital marketplace design in enabling trade: Evidence from Airbnb," 2017 — search/matching design and rejection rates on Airbnb. Horton, "Buyer uncertainty about seller capacity: Causes, consequences, and a partial solution," Management Science, 2019 — signaling and matching-rate effects in online labor markets. Chen, Hsieh, and Lin, "Reducing Recommendation Inequality via Two-Sided Matching: A Field Experiment of Online Dating," International Economic Review, 2023 — explicitly cited as finding the same congestion pattern (recommendation algorithms concentrating demand) in an online-dating field experiment; the authors state their own result "echoes" this finding. Arnosti, Johari, and Kanoria, "Managing congestion in matching markets," Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2021 — theoretical welfare-loss-from-congestion framework this paper complements empirically. Immorlica, Lucier, Manshadi, and Wei, "Designing approximately optimal search on matching platforms," EC 2021 — theoretical study of directing/limiting consumer search to reduce congestion and "high-type" cannibalization of "low-type" seller demand. Ashlagi, Krishnaswamy, Makhijani, Saban, and Shiragur, "Assortment planning for two-sided sequential matching markets," Operations Research, 2022 (also in this survey batch, as arXiv:1907.04485) — cited as related theoretical work on the trade-off between expanding choice and increasing matching when designing two-sided-platform recommendation algorithms. Roth and Xing, "Turnaround Time and Bottlenecks in Market Clearing: Decentralized Matching in the Market for Clinical Psychologists," Journal of Political Economy, 1997, and Roth, "What Have We Learned from Market Design?," The Economic Journal, 2008 — foundational market-design references on congestion as a central design problem. Ursu, "The Power of Rankings: Quantifying the Effect of Rankings on Online Consumer Search and Purchase Decisions," Marketing Science, 2018 — cited both for the finding that presenting best alternatives at the top reduces search costs in (many-to-one, non-congested) hotel-booking platforms, and as a parallel to this paper's own conditional-on-click result that request probability is roughly flat across positions.

## Project Relevance

This paper speaks most directly to **Q7** (congestion for a shared limited resource in a two-sided market) and to the survey's evaluation-plan needs under **Q6**: it is a real-data demonstration, in a market structurally similar to dating (renters compete for landlord attention/acceptance; the platform's ranking lever alone can create or relieve congestion), that ranking personalization and congestion trade off against each other, and that this trade-off's direction and severity depends on whether preferences are vertically or horizontally differentiated — a finding the authors explicitly note echoes a same-mechanism result in online dating (Chen, Hsieh, and Lin, 2023). It provides no answer to **Q1–Q5 or Q8**: there is no learned retention/revenue objective, no delayed label, no credit-assignment problem beyond a single search session, and no incrementality or causal-effect treatment of exposure — the entire analysis is a structural-preference counterfactual simulation, not an evaluation of any live ranking-model change. Its finding that the current ranking is *inefficient* (below the achievable frontier) is itself a useful diagnostic pattern: a dating platform could run the same Lorenz-curve/Gini-concentration diagnostic on its own click/like data to check whether its own ranking is leaving matches on the table purely through invariant or under-personalized ordering, independent of any retention/revenue modeling work.

Horizon verdict: none — static (per-search click/request choice model within a single session; no retention or revenue horizon; a reduced-form structural demand model, not a repeated-game equilibrium).
Lever verdict: the ranking — specifically the order of search results, and the degree to which that order is personalized (α) versus invariant/randomized across users — is directly the project's lever, making this one of the two papers in the batch (with Paper 1) whose manipulable variable a ranking-only platform can apply without modification.
