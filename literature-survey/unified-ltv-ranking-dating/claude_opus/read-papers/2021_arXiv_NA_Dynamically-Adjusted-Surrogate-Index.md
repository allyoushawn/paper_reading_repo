# Paper Analysis: Estimating the Long-Term Effects of Novel Treatments: The Dynamically Adjusted Surrogate Index

**Source:** /Users/fox/Projects/paper_reading_repo/literature-survey/unified-ltv-ranking-dating/claude_opus/pdfs/2103.08390.pdf
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-17

## 1. Summary

Battocchi, Dillon, Hei, Lewis, Oprescu, and Syrgkanis (Microsoft Research, New England) extend the Athey et al. (2020) surrogate index — which estimates a novel treatment's long-term causal effect from short-term proxies plus a historical observational dataset — to the realistic case where the historical treatment policy is *dynamic* (treatments are assigned repeatedly and depend on past treatments and past short-term outcomes). A standard surrogate index trained on such data is biased: if past treatment predicts future treatment (auto-correlation) or future treatment depends on past surrogates, then effects properly attributable to a *future* treatment get wrongly credited to the *current* one, inflating the estimated causal effect. The paper's fix is a "dynamically adjusted surrogate index": before regressing the long-term outcome on surrogates, the authors first subtract out the estimated causal effect of the next-period treatment on the outcome (a dynamic-adjustment step drawn from Lewis & Syrgkanis 2020's dynamic treatment-effect framework), producing an adjusted outcome that behaves as if no future treatment occurred; the surrogate index is then built on this adjusted outcome. The paper also generalizes from a single binary treatment (Athey et al.'s setting) to multiple continuous treatments, combining this with double/debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al.) and Neyman-orthogonal moment conditions to get root-n-consistent, asymptotically normal estimates even when the nuisance functions (surrogate models, propensity/surrogate scores) are fit with flexible ML. Three equivalent estimator representations are derived (surrogate index, surrogate score, and a doubly robust orthogonal combination), together with a discrete-treatment doubly-robust variant and a linear structural-nested-mean-model (SNMM) parameterization that yields closed-form recursive estimates. The method is validated only on fully synthetic data and on semi-synthetic data calibrated to a real "major corporation" customer-investment panel (~10k customers, monthly revenue/investment/proxy time series, with the true correlation structure re-synthesized rather than used directly for confidentiality). No real-data or online validation is reported.

## 2. Experiment Critique

Offline-only; no online/production evaluation anywhere in the paper. The empirical evidence is (a) fully synthetic panels drawn from the paper's own linear Markovian data-generating process, and (b) semi-synthetic panels built by re-estimating a covariance matrix and lagged linear models from a real corporate dataset and then re-simulating data with newly drawn parameters (Appendix D). On ℓ2-error of recovered treatment-effect coefficients, the dynamically adjusted surrogate ("adj. surrogate") and the new-treatment variant ("new treat.") substantially outperform the naive total-outcome regression and the unadjusted (Athey et al.) surrogate index, especially as the number of long-term periods M grows — exactly where the bias from ignoring future treatments is largest. However, the paper's own appendix coverage tables (Figures 8–10) show the asymptotic-normal confidence intervals badly undercover their 99% nominal target on the semi-synthetic data: the (non-debiased) dynamically adjusted surrogate achieves only 26–53% coverage across sample sizes and horizons, and even the debiased/orthogonal version only reaches 52–87% — a substantial, self-reported miscalibration that the paper does not resolve or discuss in the main text.

## 3. Industry Contribution

Directly deployable by any organization with (a) an observational panel with a dynamic/serially-correlated historical treatment policy and long-run outcomes, and (b) a short-term experimental or observational sample for a *new* treatment not seen historically. No new instrumentation beyond existing observational and short-run experimental data; the added engineering cost is statistical (fitting nuisance regressions, propensity/surrogate-score models, and the dynamic-adjustment regression) rather than a change to a serving or ranking pipeline.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

Extends Athey, Chetty, Imbens & Kang (2020) "Combining Experimental and Observational Data to Estimate Treatment Effects on Long Term Outcomes" in two directions the source paper is explicit about: (i) removing the bias from serially-correlated/dynamic treatment policies via a dynamic-adjustment step built on Lewis & Syrgkanis (2020) "Double/Debiased Machine Learning for Dynamic Treatment Effects"; (ii) generalizing from single binary treatments to multiple continuous treatments under a semiparametric partially-linear model, using Chernozhukov et al. (2018a/b) double/debiased ML and Neyman-orthogonal moments for valid inference with high-dimensional nuisance estimators.

## Project Relevance

Speaks to **Q1** (surrogate-based validation of a short-horizon label as a stand-in for retention/revenue, a prerequisite for a direct long-horizon training objective), **Q3** (label/horizon construction and delay handling for a multi-period outcome), and **Q8** (a concrete migration-relevant mechanism: how to extrapolate a validated surrogate model to a genuinely *novel* treatment — directly analogous to launching a unified ranking model with no deployment history). This is the paper's core relevance to the project's specific situation, as flagged in the batch brief: the survey has no history of a unified retention-ranked system, so a surrogate calibrated on past CTR/CVR-style experiments may not transfer without exactly this kind of dynamic adjustment, and this paper states plainly what that adjustment needs — a historical observational panel with the *same* surrogate-outcome structural relationship (γ, B in the paper's notation) as the new regime, plus short-run data on the new treatment. As Reference Card field 8 states, it is **not** relevant to **Q2** (item-level credit assignment), **Q4** (fusing short/long-term heads inside a ranking model), **Q5** (uplift placement inside a ranker), or **Q7** (two-sided market mechanics) — the method operates entirely at the whole-population, per-treatment-arm level. **Low-to-moderate relevance to Q6**: the paper offers no real-data surrogate-validation protocol (unlike Athey et al.'s Surrogacy/Comparability tests) and its own coverage diagnostics show the inference is materially miscalibrated on the one semi-synthetic evaluation it reports.
