## 1. Summary

**Title:** Optimizing Audio Recommendations for the Long-Term: A Reinforcement Learning Perspective

**Authors:** Lucas Maystre, Yu Zhao (Spotify); Daniel Russo (Spotify and Columbia University)

**Abstract (paraphrased):** The paper presents a podcast recommender system deployed at industrial scale that optimizes personal listening journeys unfolding over months, for hundreds of millions of listeners. In contrast to the pervasive industry practice of optimizing for short-term proxy metrics, the system substantially improves long-term performance in A/B tests. The paper explains how the approach copes with attribution, coordination, and measurement challenges, and contextualizes the practical method within reinforcement learning (RL): the approach is a policy-improvement update to a single component of the existing recommender, using tailored value-function modeling and user-state representations. Illustrative offline experiments suggest this specialized modeling reduces data requirements by up to a factor of 120,000 compared to a generic (black-box) approach.

**Key contributions:** (1) a formal RL model of a user's recurring, multi-month relationship with a recommender system, and a precise characterization of improving one component of an incumbent policy via a partial state-action value function; (2) a "content-relationship state" — a novel, per-item component of user state that tracks a user's history with each individual piece of content; (3) "stickiness models" — item-level value functions that forecast the durability of a user's engagement with a specific item; (4) structural assumptions (no-indirect-impact, surrogacy) that decompose the long-horizon Q-function into a product of a short-term listen-probability model and a long-term stickiness model, making offline estimation dramatically more data-efficient.

**Methodology:** The paper models a user's recurring interactions as a sequence of daily periods with a random deactivation time, and formalizes improving one component π⋆ of the existing policy π⁰ (e.g., the logic governing recommendations on the app's home banner) via a partial Q-function Q_π⁰(s, a⋆) — the expected total future reward when item a⋆ is recommended at the position of interest and π⁰ governs everything else. This Q-function is a genuine causal/counterfactual quantity (validated via the incumbent policy's built-in randomization; Remark 2 explicitly connects it to Pearl's do-calculus). To make this tractable, user state is decomposed into a slow-moving taste vector u_t, fast-moving context X_t, and a novel content-relationship state Z_t (per-item, updated incrementally, e.g. via an exponential moving average of listening indicators). Under two structural assumptions — no indirect impact of a recommendation on other items' engagement, and "surrogacy" (future item-specific reward depends on the recommendation only through its effect on that item's next content-relationship state) — the Q-function decomposes (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) into [impact of the recommendation on today's listen probability] × [impact of listening today on long-term engagement with that item, i.e., the "stickiness" of that item for that user]. In the deployed prototype for podcast **discovery** (items never previously heard), the short-term term is approximated by an existing fine-tuned listen-probability model, and the long-term "stickiness" term is estimated by a new, separately trained linear model (least-squares regression of 59-day-forward listening-day counts on the user's taste vector, pooled across users who discovered an item more than 60 days ago) — yielding the simple deployed score Q̂(s,a) = clickiness × (1 + stickiness).

**Main results:** Discovery use case, small focused A/B test (Home banner, 12 markets, 1-week exposure, 60-day downstream tracking): the long-term personalized treatment increased 60-day listening minutes by **81%** and 60-day show-active days by 32%, while *reducing* immediate first-streams by 6% — direct evidence that optimizing for the long term selects systematically different items than optimizing for immediate engagement. A larger, 9-week persistent shelf-level A/B test showed +1.7% overall week-8 podcast listening minutes, +6.2% consumption from discoveries, +5.4% users with a "lasting" discovery. The paper also reports (illustrative, not purely simulated — based on repurposing real randomized A/B data) that its structured, item-level stickiness-model approach reduces the sample size needed to statistically distinguish two policies' long-term Q-values by a factor of over 120,000 compared to a generic "holistic, long-term" black-box estimator.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design:** The discovery A/B test explicitly validates its key counterfactual simplifying assumption — that organic (non-recommended) discovery of a specific new item is essentially impossible — using a randomized holdback group (Figure 5), showing an order-of-magnitude-plus difference in discovery rates between recommended and holdback users; this is a genuinely rigorous validation of a load-bearing modeling assumption, not just an asserted claim.

**Statistical validity:** Reported lifts include 95% confidence intervals in every figure (Figures 5, 6, 7, 9); the headline 81% lift and 32% lift are both reported with visible, non-overlapping confidence intervals versus control. The data-efficiency claim (120,000× reduction) is derived from a controlled comparison of estimator standard errors across sample sizes up to n=2,560,000, using real repurposed A/B logs, not a purely synthetic simulation — but it IS a stylized illustrative comparison (meta-actions A/B repurposed as if they were item-level recommendations) rather than a live deployment of the naive "holistic, long-term" estimator at production scale.

**Online experiments:** Two genuine, separate online A/B tests are reported for the **discovery** use case, with real (not simulated) 60-day and 9-week downstream outcomes. For the **resurfacing** use case (recommending already-familiar items), the paper reports **only offline, correlational analysis** (Section 5.2) and explicitly states: "we do not yet have such evidence for recommendations of familiar items" (Section 5.2.2) — no online result exists for resurfacing in this paper.

**Reproducibility:** Not reproducible outside Spotify — proprietary production traffic, proprietary pretrained user/item embeddings, and an internal experimentation platform are all dependencies. No code or data release; illustrative figures (e.g., Figure 8's resurfacing heatmaps) use hypothetical/anonymized aggregate values.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability:** High, and explicitly framed as a "policy improvement update to a single component" rather than a full-system RL redesign — the discovery-use-case deployment reuses an existing, already-optimized short-term listen-probability model unchanged, and adds only a new, lightweight, pooled linear "stickiness vector" per item, combined multiplicatively (Eq. 16) at serving time. The authors explicitly favor this "new representation, not fine-tuning" approach (Remark 4) because it requires far less item-specific data than fine-tuning would.

**Problems solved:** Directly tackles all three challenges the paper names up front — measurement (poor signal-to-noise in long-horizon rewards, solved via the decomposition into a lower-variance, pooled, item-level stickiness estimate instead of a diffuse app-level reward), attribution (which earlier recommendation gets credit for a later habit — sidestepped via the item-level "content-relationship state" and the no-indirect-impact assumption), and coordination (multiple product teams each maintaining separate short-term models — the long-term stickiness layer is maintained centrally and composes with those existing short-term models without requiring their retraining).

**Engineering cost:** Comparatively low for the discovery use case (reuses existing embeddings and short-term model, adds one new regression-trained vector per item). Explicitly higher, and this is why resurfacing was deprioritized, for the resurfacing use case: the paper states resurfacing recommendations "require near-real-time systems to maintain engagement states and compute stickiness model predictions on-the-fly, incurring significant infrastructure costs" (footnote 7) — a concrete, stated engineering-cost asymmetry between the two use cases.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

The paper is explicit about its relationship to prior RL-for-recommendation work: it credits Covington et al. (2016), *Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations*, as the influential precedent for the underlying learned user/item taste embeddings it builds on. It contrasts itself with Ie et al. (2019), *Reinforcement Learning for Slate-based Recommender Systems* (RecSim), which the paper describes as the closest related RL-for-recsys work but optimizing only a single viewing **session**, not a multi-month horizon; similarly, it contrasts with Zheng et al. (2018) (news recommendation RL, discount factor 0.9) and Zou et al. (2019) (RL to optimize long-term engagement, discount factor 0.4) as optimizing much shorter effective horizons. It positions itself alongside Wang et al. (2022), *Surrogate for Long-Term User Experience in Recommender Systems*, KDD 2022, as part of a broader surrogate/proxy-metric tradition (also citing Athey, Chetty, Imbens & Kang's 2019 "surrogate index" as an intellectual precedent for combining several observable outcomes into one scalar long-term proxy), but argues its domain-specific, hypothesis-driven ("item-level listening habits") structural modeling is what enables tractable, data-efficient estimation over month-long horizons where generic black-box RL/actor-critic approaches (e.g., Chen et al. 2019/2022 at YouTube) become intractable due to poor signal-to-noise.

## Project Relevance

Speaks directly to **Q1** (making long-term outcomes the training objective of a component of the ranking policy, via a formal RL policy-improvement framing), **Q2** (credit assignment via the item-level content-relationship state and stickiness model — a concrete, causally-grounded alternative to purely heuristic attribution), **Q4** (fusing an unchanged short-term model with a new long-term head via simple multiplicative combination), and **Q6** (the paper's central methodological contribution is precisely how to evaluate a long-horizon, low-signal-to-noise objective both offline, via calibration, and online, via appropriately long-running A/B tests — directly transferable to the project's 7–30 day delayed-retention-label problem). It does **not** address Q7 (no two-sided/reciprocal-market treatment, and its core no-indirect-impact assumption is a poor fit for a market with congestion) or Q8 in the sense of a full migration blueprint (it improves one component of one existing policy, not a full replacement of a CTR/CVR + uplift blend). The resurfacing/familiar-item results, which are the closer analogue to a dating app's repeat-exposure dynamics, remain offline-only and should not be cited as validated online evidence.
