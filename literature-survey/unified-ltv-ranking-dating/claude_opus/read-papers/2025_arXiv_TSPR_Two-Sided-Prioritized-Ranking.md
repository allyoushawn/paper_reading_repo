# Paper Analysis: Two-Sided Prioritized Ranking: A Coherency-Preserving Design for Marketplace Experiments

**Source:** `/Users/fox/Projects/paper_reading_repo/literature-survey/unified-ltv-ranking-dating/claude_opus/pdfs/2502.09806.pdf` (arXiv:2502.09806v2)
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-17

## 1. Summary

Mahyar Habibi (Lyft), Zahra Khanalizadeh (University of Washington), and Negar Ziaeian (University of Warwick) address pricing experiments in ranked-list marketplaces (their running example: hotel search) where items compete for user attention and demand within the same query's list, creating within-query interference (a SUTVA violation via substitution — discounting one item can steal bookings from another, biasing item-level A/B tests). Beyond interference, they identify a second, largely operational/legal constraint that prior interference-aware designs ignore: **coherency** — (i) *price parity*, every user must observe the same realized price for any given item (price discrimination across users is tightly regulated under EU competition law and carries acute reputational risk — the paper cites the 2025 Instacart "item price test" backlash and the 2000 Amazon DVD-pricing scandal as concrete examples), and (ii) *full catalog access*, no user's search results can have items removed. These two constraints rule out standard user-level randomization (which would show different prices for the same item to different users), standard two-sided randomization (TSR) as implemented by Johari et al. (2022) and Bajari et al. (2023), and any catalog-restricting design.

Their proposed design, **Two-Sided Prioritized Ranking (TSPR)**, exploits position bias — users pay disproportionate attention to top-ranked items — rather than item availability or price variation as the source of identifying variation. Items are partitioned into Treated, Untreated, and Placebo subsets (probabilities p, p, and 1−2p, p<0.5); only the Treated subset receives the actual intervention. Incoming queries (one per user) are randomized into group Q^A or Q^B: Q^A's ranked list is reordered to prioritize Untreated items at the top (block order: Untreated → Placebo → Treated), Q^B's list prioritizes Treated items at the top (Treated → Placebo → Untreated), with relevance-score ordering preserved within each priority block. Every item retains exactly one treatment status visible to all users (price parity satisfied) and every user still sees the full catalog (full-catalog-access satisfied) — only the *ordering* changes, which systematically shifts each group's effective exposure to treated vs. untreated items. Under five stated identifying assumptions (attention/treatment separability, multiplicative and symmetric re-ranking distortion, a partial-treatment-effect scaling function τ(l)→1 as the treated/untreated block grows, and a negligible-contamination assumption), the global lift Φ = E[Y|full treatment]/E[Y|full control] − 1 is recoverable from the empirical partial-lift function φ̂(l) via either a closed-form weighted-least-squares estimator or a nonparametric isotonic-regression estimator. The method is validated only in semi-synthetic Monte Carlo simulation, with click and cascade-booking behavioral models fit to real Expedia hotel-search data (a public Kaggle dataset), against Bernoulli-randomized and cluster-randomized baselines.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design.** A theoretical framework (Definitions 1–3, Assumptions 1–5) plus a semi-synthetic simulation study: click and booking behavioral models are separately estimated on real, public Expedia search-impression data (with a genuine identification strategy — ~30% of impressions in the raw dataset were randomly sorted, letting the authors separate position effects from relevance effects in a first stage before estimating utility coefficients in a second stage), and both models are shown to closely fit real held-out click-through-rate-by-position and booking-rate-by-click-order curves (Figure 2). Treatment is then introduced as a synthetic constant shift in latent utility, and 500 Monte Carlo replications of 20,000 queries each compare TSPR against two baselines.

**Statistical validity.** Table 5 (Φ_true=−0.0691) reports mean estimate, bias, RMSE, and empirical SD for all four estimators. TSPR (parametric) achieves the lowest RMSE (0.0210) despite a nontrivial bias (−0.0133); TSPR (nonparametric/isotonic) achieves the lowest bias in magnitude (0.0069) with a modestly higher RMSE (0.0283). Bootstrap confidence intervals for the nonparametric estimator achieve 93% empirical coverage against a nominal 95% target (Appendix A.3, referenced but not read in full) — a real, if minor, under-coverage the authors do not further explain. Sensitivity analysis across treatment probability p∈[0.10,0.45] is reported as showing TSPR remains "robust" (Appendix A.4, referenced but not read in full).

**Online experiments.** None. This is a purely simulation-based paper; no live marketplace deployment is reported anywhere in the sections read.

**Reproducibility.** The theoretical framework, both estimators (closed-form WLS in equation 11, isotonic regression in equation 12), and the click/booking model specifications and fitted parameters (Tables 3–4) are fully specified. The underlying Expedia dataset (Adam, Hamner, Friedman, and SSA_Expedia, "Personalize Expedia Hotel Searches," ICDM 2013 Kaggle competition) is public, making the calibration reproducible in principle, though the paper does not reference a released simulation code repository.

**Overall.** The strongest empirical grounding in this paper is the click/booking-model calibration to real data, not a real interference experiment — the interference-correction claims themselves (Table 5's bias/RMSE comparisons) rest entirely on synthetic ground truth (a simulated 6.9 percent booking decline under full treatment) that the authors themselves construct, not an independently observed effect. The authors' own discussion section is candid that the identifying assumptions "can fail if the treatment meaningfully changes user engagement or if high treatment probabilities induce ranking perturbations that users do not tolerate" — a risk named but not empirically tested.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability.** "TSPR can be implemented by adjusting ranking priorities within an existing recommender system, making it straightforward to deploy on platforms that already support re-ranking logic" — the authors explicitly frame this as avoiding the operational burdens of an actual pricing change, catalog partitioning, or interference-network modeling that competing designs require.

**Problems solved.** Gives platforms a legally and reputationally safer path to item-side pricing (or other item-attribute) experiments than user-level randomization, directly motivated by real backlash events the paper cites (the 2025 Instacart item-price-test controversy, which led Instacart to publicly announce it would end such tests in December 2025 per the paper's citation, and the 2000 Amazon DVD-pricing scandal) — while still recovering a usable treatment-effect estimate under within-query interference.

**Engineering cost.** Lower than cluster randomization: TSPR needs no item-clustering infrastructure (which the paper notes "requires detailed knowledge of the underlying network structure... difficult to specify and validate" and "can be computationally expensive to implement") — only a "priority" field injected into existing ranking logic. Real constraints acknowledged by the authors: each query needs "a sufficiently large set of relevant items" for the repositioning scheme to meaningfully shift exposure, and the design assumes **slack supply** — no binding user-side availability/capacity constraint over the experiment horizon (one user's booking must not meaningfully reduce availability for others) — explicitly flagged as a limitation the authors leave to future work for "supply-constrained settings with binding capacity constraints."

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty.** The first design to jointly satisfy interference-robustness and two hard coherency constraints — price parity and full catalog access — that the authors argue rule out standard two-sided randomization (which can show different price realizations of the same item to different users), cluster randomization (at acceptable variance, without detailed network knowledge), and any catalog-restricting design; achieved by using position bias itself, rather than item availability or price variation, as the source of identifying exposure variation.

**Prior work.** Johari, Li, Liskovich, and Weintraub, "Experimental design in two-sided platforms: An analysis of bias," Management Science 2022, and Bajari, Burdick, Imbens, Masoero, McQueen, Richardson, and Rosen, "Experimental design in marketplaces," Statistical Science 2023 — standard two-sided randomization (TSR), explicitly contrasted: standard TSR applies treatment only when a treated user interacts with a treated item, which can show different users different (price) versions of the same item, violating the paper's coherency requirement. Ha-Thuc, Dutta, Mao, Wood, and Liu, "A counterfactual framework for seller-side A/B testing on marketplaces," SIGIR 2020, and Nandy, Venugopalan, Lo, and Chatterjee, "A/B testing for recommender systems in a two-sided marketplace," NeurIPS 2021 (this survey's `2021_NeurIPS_UniCoRn_...` entry) — cited under "prior work on interference in ranking experiments," explicitly distinguished on framing: those designs treat the *recommender system itself* as the object of experimentation, whereas TSPR uses ranking purely as the *mechanism* through which item-side treatment exposure is shifted, with the underlying recommender never being evaluated or modified. Ugander, Karrer, Backstrom, and Kleinberg, "Graph cluster randomization: Network exposure to multiple universes," 2013; Ugander and Yin, "Randomized graph cluster randomization," Journal of Causal Inference 2023; Holtz, Lobel, Lobel, Liskovich, and Aral, "Reducing interference bias in online marketplace experiments using cluster randomization: Evidence from a pricing meta-experiment on Airbnb," Management Science 2024 — cluster randomization, used directly as the paper's second empirical baseline. Aronow and Samii, "Estimating average causal effects under general interference," 2017, and Harshaw, Sävje, Eisenstat, Mirrokni, and Pouget-Abadie, "Design and analysis of bipartite experiments under a linear exposure-response model," Electronic Journal of Statistics 2023 — exposure-mapping-based network-interference designs, contrasted as requiring a specified and validated exposure model that TSPR avoids. Goli, Lambrecht, and Yoganarasimhan, "A bias correction approach for interference in ranking experiments," Marketing Science 2024 — the direct methodological source for TSPR's isotonic-regression nonparametric estimator, though Goli et al. apply isotonic regression to improve recommender-system accuracy rather than to recover a global treatment effect. Zhan, Han, Hu, and Jiang, "Estimating treatment effects under recommender interference: A structured neural networks approach," arXiv:2406.14380 (2024) — an alternative structured-model correction approach.

## Project Relevance

Speaks to **Q6**: TSPR is a genuinely novel answer to how a platform can test an item-side (candidate-side) intervention under two-sided interference *without* showing inconsistent treatment to different viewers of the same candidate and without restricting any viewer's catalog — a constraint the survey's other D8 papers do not address (Ha-Thuc/Nandy's counterfactual interleaving and this batch's budget-split design both implicitly permit differential per-viewer serving of the same candidate). It also bears on **Q7** (congestion for a shared limited resource): the paper's own stated limitation is the most important caveat for this project specifically — TSPR's core "slack supply" assumption is exactly what the project's congestion constraint (B's attention is a shared, limited resource across many A's) violates, so TSPR cannot be applied to the dating app's candidate side as-is; it would require the paper's own flagged future extension to binding-capacity settings, making this paper's main contribution to the survey a well-specified problem statement and a partial solution rather than a ready-to-deploy design. The paper does not address Q1–Q5 or Q8: there is no retention/revenue training objective, no delayed-label handling, and no migration-path guidance — it is purely an evaluation-design contribution for a narrower problem (item-side pricing/attribute experiments) than the project's full ranking-model migration.
