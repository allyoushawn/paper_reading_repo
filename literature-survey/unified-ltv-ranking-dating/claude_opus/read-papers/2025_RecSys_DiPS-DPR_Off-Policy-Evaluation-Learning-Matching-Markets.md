## 1. Summary

Yudai Hayashi (Wantedly, Inc., Tokyo), Shuhei Goda (Independent Researcher, Tokyo), Yuta Saito (Cornell University), "Off-Policy Evaluation and Learning for Matching Markets," RecSys '25 (19th ACM Conference on Recommender Systems), Prague, Czech Republic, September 22–26, 2025. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3705328.3748047

**Abstract (paraphrased):** Matching users by mutual preference — job search, dating — depends on reciprocal recommendations, but A/B tests to evaluate new matching policies are costly and slow. Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE) offers a cheaper alternative using only logged data, but the bidirectional nature of matching interactions and the resulting extreme sparsity of the final "match" label make standard OPE estimators (Direct Method, Inverse Propensity Scoring, Doubly Robust) unreliable at matching-platform scale. The paper formalizes OPE and Off-Policy Learning (OPL) for matching markets for the first time and introduces two hybrid estimators.

**Key contributions:** (1) First formalization of OPE and OPL specifically for matching markets, modeling the reciprocal interaction as a two-stage reward process: a first-stage reward s (does the recommender's own side, e.g. a company, send a scout/contact?) and a second-stage reward r (does the recipient respond?), with the ultimate match m = s·r. (2) DiPS (Direct and Propensity Score): applies importance weighting only to the first-stage reward s, and imputes the second-stage reward r via a regression model q_r(c,j), avoiding the need to apply high-variance propensity weights to the sparse final match label. (3) DPR (Direct, Propensity, and doubly Robust): extends DiPS with an additional prediction model for the expected match probability q_m(c,j), for tighter bias-variance control, structurally analogous to how DR extends IPS. (4) Extension of both estimators to policy-gradient form (DiPS-PG, DPR-PG) for offline policy learning. (5) Empirical validation on synthetic data and on real A/B-testing logs from the job-matching platform Wantedly Visit.

**Methodology:** The platform recommends job seeker j to company c under policy π(j|c). Three binary rewards are defined: s (company sends a scout), r (job seeker responds, conditional on a scout), and m = s·r (successful match). Q-functions q_s(c,j) := E[s|c,j], q_r(c,j) := E[r|c,j,s=1], and q_m(c,j) := q_s·q_r are defined, and the policy value is V(π) := (1/|C|) Σ_c Σ_j π(j|c)·q_s(c,j)·q_r(c,j) — the expected number of matches under policy π. Given logged data D = {(j_c, s_c, r_c)} collected under logging policy π0, DiPS estimates V_DiPS(π;D) = (1/|C|) Σ_c (π(j_c|c)/π0(j_c|c)·s_c)·q̂_r(c,j_c): the importance-weighted first-stage reward times a regression-predicted second-stage reward. DPR adds a q̂_m(c,j) correction term in a doubly-robust structure. Both are extended to policy-gradient estimators (DiPS-PG, DPR-PG) for direct offline policy optimization via gradient ascent on V(π_θ).

**Main results:** On synthetic data (10-D company/job-seeker context vectors, softmax logging policy, ε-greedy target policy), DiPS and DPR achieve consistently lower MSE and ErrorRate (probability of misranking the target vs. logging policy) than DM, IPS, and DR across sweeps of dataset size, action-space size, and reward sparsity; DiPS-PG/DPR-PG achieve the highest learned policy values among the policy-gradient variants. On real-world data from Wantedly Visit's scouting service (|C| = 21,736 company-day-rank contexts, |J| = 17,460 job seekers, 1.2% baseline reward sparsity), reward and policy models are estimated via GBDT with 5-fold cross-validation. DPR achieves the lowest overall MSE; DiPS shows lower bias than IPS and DPR lower bias than DR (attributed to positive correlation between the estimated-logging-policy error and the second-stage-reward-model error). A notable negative/mixed finding: DiPS and IPS achieve a *lower* ErrorRate than DPR and DR despite DPR/DR having smaller MSE — IPS's systematic overestimation of V(π) happens to coincide with correctly ranking the target policy above the logging policy in this dataset, so lower MSE does not guarantee better policy-selection accuracy.

## 2. Experiment Critique

- **Design:** Combines a controlled synthetic environment (sweeping company count, job-seeker/action-space count, and reward-sparsity parameter independently) with a genuine industrial dataset built from real A/B-testing logs at Wantedly Visit — a stronger design than most of this survey's OPE-adjacent papers, since the real-world section validates offline estimator accuracy against *known* historical A/B outcomes rather than only against a synthetic ground truth.
- **Statistical validity:** Real-world experiments repeated 20 times over varying company-sampling sizes to characterize estimator distribution; reward/policy models trained via GBDT with 5-fold cross-validation. However, the underlying GBDT reward models are reported with only moderate discriminative power (ROC-AUC 0.680–0.707 for reply/match prediction) and substantial systematic underestimation (−23.3% to −53.4% depending on sample size and target), which directly bounds how much the DiPS/DPR bias-reduction claims can be trusted at this specific sparsity level.
- **Online experiments:** None in the sense of a live deployment of a DiPS/DPR-selected or DiPS/DPR-PG-learned policy. The "real-world" evaluation instead retrospectively checks whether the offline estimators can reproduce *already-known* historical A/B test results from logged data — a meaningful validation of estimator accuracy, but not evidence that deploying a DiPS/DPR-optimized policy improves outcomes prospectively.
- **Reproducibility:** The real-world dataset (Wantedly Visit scouting logs) is proprietary and not released; the synthetic data-generating process is fully specified in the paper (closed-form sigmoid reward functions, explicit parameter draws), so the synthetic results are reproducible in principle. Code availability is not stated in the extracted content.
- **Overall:** Theoretically rigorous (full bias/variance derivations for DiPS and DPR relative to DM/IPS/DR) and empirically validated on a real industrial reciprocal platform, but the absence of any live A/B test of the *proposed estimators' resulting policy* — as opposed to a retrospective check against known outcomes — is a real gap, and the ErrorRate-vs-MSE discordance the authors themselves report is a genuine negative/complicating result they do not fully resolve.

## 3. Industry Contribution

- **Deployability:** Moderate — DiPS/DPR require only (a) a first-stage reward model or its propensity-weighted estimate, (b) a second-stage reward regression model q_r(c,j), and optionally (c) a match-probability model q_m(c,j) for DPR — all standard supervised-learning artifacts (the paper uses GBDT) layered onto an existing OPE pipeline, not a new serving architecture.
- **Problems solved:** Directly addresses the practical problem of evaluating a candidate ranking-policy change for a reciprocal marketplace *before* committing to a live A/B test, specifically under the severe reward sparsity (1.2% in the reported dataset) that makes naive IPS/DM/DR unreliable at matching-platform scale — exactly the regime a dating app's like/match funnel sits in.
- **Engineering cost:** Requires logged (context, action, first-stage reward, second-stage reward) tuples, an estimated or known logging policy π0(j|c), and periodic retraining of the second-stage (and, for DPR, match-probability) regression models — a modest incremental cost on top of infrastructure a ranking team already needs for any supervised CTR/CVR model, fitting naturally as an offline gate in a ranking-policy release pipeline rather than a new serving component.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty:** First formalization of OPE and OPL specifically for matching (reciprocal) markets; the explicit exploitation of the observable *first-stage* reward (the recommender's own side's action) — rather than relying solely on the sparse final match label — to construct lower-variance hybrid estimators; and the extension of this two-stage decomposition to policy-gradient learning (DiPS-PG, DPR-PG).

**Prior work most heavily built on:**
- Yuta Saito and Thorsten Joachims, "Counterfactual Learning and Evaluation for Recommender Systems: Foundations, Implementations, and Recent Advances," RecSys 2021 — foundational counterfactual OPE/OPL framing this paper extends to the reciprocal setting.
- Miroslav Dudík, Dumitru Erhan, John Langford, and Lihong Li, "Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Optimization," Statistical Science 2014 — the DR estimator this paper's DPR structurally generalizes.
- Yuta Saito and Thorsten Joachims, "Off-Policy Evaluation for Large Action Spaces via Embeddings" (MIPS), ICML 2022 — cited for variance reduction under large action spaces; the paper notes its own methods can be combined with MIPS.
- Yu-Xiang Wang, Alekh Agarwal, and Miroslav Dudík, "Optimal and Adaptive Off-Policy Evaluation in Contextual Bandits" (Switch-DR), ICML 2017 — cited as an estimator the paper's hybrid approach can also be combined with.
- Yi Su, Magd Bayoumi, and Thorsten Joachims, "Optimizing Rankings for Recommendation in Matching Markets," WWW 2022 — prior work specifically on ranking optimization inside matching markets.
- Yi Su, Maria Dimakopoulou, Akshay Krishnamurthy, and Miroslav Dudík, "Doubly Robust Off-Policy Evaluation with Shrinkage," ICML 2020 — variance-reduction technique in the same estimator family.
- Haruka Kiyohara, Ren Kishimoto, Kosuke Kawakami, Ken Kobayashi, Kazuhide Nakata, and Yuta Saito, "Towards Assessing and Benchmarking Risk-Return Tradeoff of Off-Policy Evaluation," ICLR 2024 — source of the risk-return evaluation framing (MSE vs. ErrorRate) used in this paper's experiments.

## Project Relevance

This paper speaks most directly to **Q6** (offline/online evaluation under two-sided interference) and **Q7** (two-sided/reciprocal market handling): it is fundamentally an off-policy evaluation framework purpose-built for reciprocal markets, and reciprocity — a two-stage, bidirectional interaction — is its central subject. It partially informs **Q2** (credit assignment) and **Q5** (uplift/incrementality), but only in a way that must be stated precisely rather than assumed, per field 6 above: DiPS/DPR estimate a counterfactual **policy value** (the aggregate causal effect of switching from the logging policy to a candidate policy) — genuinely causal at the policy level — but this is neither a per-pair incremental-treatment-effect estimate (no CATE/ITE for an individual exposure decision, unlike a true uplift model) nor a purely bias-corrected point prediction (unlike the CFRR paper from an earlier batch). It occupies a third category: off-policy policy-value estimation. Readers should not conflate "this paper does OPE with IPS/DR-style machinery" with "this paper estimates the incremental effect of showing B to A" — it does neither in the project's sense; it estimates what would happen, in aggregate, under a different ranking policy.

On congestion/capacity: **not addressed** — there is no modeling of a job seeker's (or profile's) limited capacity to receive scouts/likes from many companies/viewers, which is a direct gap relative to the project's congestion constraint. On the off-policy correction's structure: it is **one-sided**, applying importance weighting only to the recommender's own controlled action (which job seeker is shown to which company); the reciprocal side's response is captured by an uncorrected predictive regression model — a materially weaker form of "bilateral" correction than the project's reciprocal-market needs would ultimately require. Credit assignment (Q2) is a same-session, single-decision attribution (see field 8) — a much simpler problem than the project's delayed, cross-day retention/revenue attribution.

Does not address **Q1** (no retention/revenue training objective — the optimized quantity is immediate match count), **Q3** (no time horizon, delay, or censoring treatment of any kind), **Q4** (no short-term/long-term head fusion — there is only one policy-value objective), or **Q8** (no migration path from a CTR/CVR + uplift blend is discussed).

Counterexample verdict: NO — the policy value optimized is an immediate match (m = s·r), not a long-horizon retention or revenue outcome, so despite genuinely causal (policy-level, counterfactual) machinery, it does not satisfy the "long-horizon objective" leg of the provisional conclusion.
