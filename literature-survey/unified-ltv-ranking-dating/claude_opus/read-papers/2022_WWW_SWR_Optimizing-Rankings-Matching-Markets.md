# Paper Analysis: Optimizing Rankings for Recommendation in Matching Markets

**Source:** WWW 2022 (arXiv:2106.01941, submitted 2021-06-03), Cornell University. `/Users/fox/Projects/paper_reading_repo/literature-survey/unified-ltv-ranking-dating/claude_opus/pdfs/2106.01941.pdf`
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-17

## 1. Summary

Yi Su (Cornell Statistics and Data Science), Magd Bayoumi, and Thorsten Joachims (Cornell Computer Science) formalize the problem of ranking in two-sided matching markets under a sequential, asymmetric "apply-accept" interaction protocol — the mechanism underlying job platforms, college admissions, and online dating. The paper's core claim: ranking each side purely by relevance (the Probability Ranking Principle, PRP) is highly sub-optimal once both (1) a match requires mutual relevance and (2) both sides have limited attention/capacity to evaluate candidates. Popular employers/candidates get crowded with applications they cannot all evaluate, while comparably good but less-visible options go undiscovered — bad for both individual outcomes and societal goals (e.g., minimizing unemployment).

The framework: proactive candidates c ∈ C browse a personalized stochastic ranking σ(c) of employers J and apply to relevant ones following a Position-Based Model (PBM), where the probability of applying to employer j is the product of true relevance f_c(j) and an examination probability v(rank(j|σ(c))) that decays with rank. Employers then reactively browse the ranked list of candidates who applied to them (ranked by employer-side relevance g_j(c)) and reply following the same PBM logic. A "match" requires both discovery events: the candidate applies (finds and pursues) and the employer replies (finds and accepts). The ranking policy is represented as a per-candidate doubly stochastic matrix M_c^π (via the Birkhoff–von Neumann decomposition), which compactly parameterizes any stochastic ranking policy with |J|² parameters and permits sampling a deterministic ranking in polynomial time.

The optimization objective is **social welfare** SW(π): the expected total number of successful matches in the market, equal to the sum of all candidate utilities (equivalently all employer utilities). The authors first prove (Theorem 2) that the social-welfare gap between the optimal ranking and the naive one-sided-relevance ranking can be **Θ(n)** — linear in market size — via an explicit adversarial construction (a circulant relevance matrix plus a steep, limited-depth examination function). They then show the exact SW objective is intractable to optimize directly, because it requires the exact distribution of a candidate's rank among a job's applicants — a Poisson Binomial random variable whose PMF involves a combinatorial sum over n!/((n-l)!l!) terms. To make the problem tractable, they derive a **convex lower bound** on SW (Theorem 4, valid whenever the examination function v(·) is convex — true for common choices like v(x)=1/x and v(x)=1/log₂(1+x)) via Jensen's inequality, then optimize this lower bound over the space of doubly stochastic matrices using either Frank-Wolfe (with CVXPY+SCS as the inner convex solver) or projected gradient descent (Sinkhorn-Knopp projection).

**Methodology and datasets.** Experiments cover (1) synthetic two-sided markets (default n=100 candidates/employers) varying crowding level λ, relevance-structure asymmetry (similar/reverse/random), examination function steepness, and market size; (2) a real-world **networking-recommendation dataset the authors built and fielded themselves at a large computer-science conference** (925 users, each acting as both proactive and reactive side; relevance estimated via importance-weighted L2-regularized logistic regression on co-authorship, citation, and article-similarity features; the authors state they will publish this dataset as a benchmark); and (3) the public **Libimseti online-dating benchmark** (500 males and 500 females selected by rating volume, missing ratings imputed via Alternating Least Squares under a simplifying opposite-sex-only-rating assumption).

**Main results.** Against two baselines — Naive-Relevance Ranking (rank by one-sided f_c(j)) and Reciprocal-Relevance Ranking (rank by the product f_c(j)·g_j(c), ignoring collision dependencies) — the proposed Social-Welfare Ranking wins substantially and consistently. On the real-world networking-recommendation dataset (10 runs, mean ± two standard errors): Naive-Relevance 604.0±0.11, Reciprocal-Relevance 763.8±0.06, Social-Welfare Ranking 824.1±0.18. On the Libimseti online-dating benchmark: Naive-Relevance 844.0±0.10, Reciprocal-Relevance 957.2±0.12, Social-Welfare Ranking 1199.2±0.14 — roughly a 42% improvement over naive relevance ranking and a 25% improvement over reciprocal-relevance ranking. On synthetic data, the advantage of Social-Welfare Ranking grows with crowding level and is largest for steep examination functions (where users only look at the top few results), where it nearly doubles the matches achieved by Reciprocal-Relevance Ranking.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design.** The synthetic experiments systematically sweep one factor at a time (crowding λ, relevance asymmetry, examination-function steepness, market size) holding others fixed at defaults, which cleanly isolates each mechanism's contribution. The two real-world validations — a self-collected live networking-recommendation deployment and a public dating benchmark — are a genuine strength; most papers in this space validate only on synthetic data or one dataset.

**Statistical validity.** Theorem 2 (Θ(n) sub-optimality gap) is proved rigorously via an explicit adversarial instance with a fully worked derivation in the appendix. The convex lower-bound derivation (Theorem 4) is a clean application of Jensen's inequality with a stated, checkable sufficient condition (convexity of v). Real-world results report standard errors (10 runs, Monte Carlo with 10,000 samples per run) and the improvement margins are large relative to the reported standard errors.

**Online experiments.** None in the causal/A/B sense. The "real-world" validation is offline: relevance probabilities are estimated once from historical/observed data, then all three ranking policies are evaluated by simulating the same PBM-based application/reply process from those fixed estimated relevances — this is a policy-simulation comparison, not a live deployment comparison of the ranking policies themselves. The networking-recommendation system was fielded to *collect* the underlying relevance data, not to A/B test the ranking policies against each other live.

**Reproducibility.** The paper states it will release code and further experiment details, and will publish the networking-recommendation dataset as a benchmark; the Libimseti dataset is a pre-existing public benchmark. Two full algorithms (Frank-Wolfe and projected gradient descent, with explicit hyperparameters — 50 timesteps, learning rate 0.2) are given in the appendix.

**Overall.** Theoretically rigorous with a genuinely dual real-world validation, but every quantitative comparison against baselines is a simulated-policy comparison built on separately estimated relevance probabilities, assumed accurate and unbiased — not a live ranking-policy A/B test.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability.** Moderate-to-high. The ranking policy is a doubly stochastic matrix decomposable (via Birkhoff-von Neumann) into a sampleable deterministic ranking in polynomial time, so serving is compatible with standard ranking-pipeline architectures. The optimization itself (Frank-Wolfe or projected gradient descent over doubly stochastic matrices, with a per-candidate LP-like subproblem) is heavier than a simple learned-score sort and requires solving a joint optimization across all candidates in the market simultaneously — a batch, market-wide computation rather than a per-request independent ranking call, which is a real engineering departure from typical online ranking serving.

**Problems solved.** Directly targets congestion from over-concentration of attention on a small number of highly-relevant-to-many items — exactly the survey's core congestion concern — by jointly optimizing all candidates' rankings rather than ranking each independently.

**Engineering cost.** Non-trivial: requires (a) accurate, unbiased two-sided relevance probability estimates as an input (explicitly assumed, not solved by this paper), (b) a market-wide joint optimization run periodically (not a per-request microservice call), and (c) infrastructure to sample and serve stochastic rankings. The two-stage approximation used in the real-world experiments (rank top-100 by reciprocal relevance, then re-optimize only that shortlist for social welfare) is the paper's own concession that full-market optimization is expensive, and is a practical pattern for production adoption.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty.** Fourfold, per the authors: (1) first formalization of ranking under the apply-accept interaction protocol in two-sided matching markets; (2) first recommender system that jointly optimizes personalized rankings across all candidates to maximize social welfare (rather than ranking each user independently or ranking to a single centralized matching outcome); (3) opens discussion of fairness and strategic behavior (adoption/retention) under this framework; (4) a self-built, real-world networking-recommendation system fielded at a large CS conference, released as a benchmark.

**Prior work.** Tu, Ribeiro, Jensen, Towsley, Liu, Jiang, and Wang, "Online dating recommendations: matching markets and learning preferences," WWW 2014 — explicitly named by the authors as "the closest" prior work; it proposes a two-sided matching framework for dating recommendation that maximizes reciprocated messages while bounding expected messages sent/received, but assumes each user exhaustively evaluates a fixed-size recommendation list rather than modeling uncertain examination depth via PBM. Ashlagi, Krishnaswamy, Makhijani, Saban, and Shiragur, "Assortment planning for two-sided sequential matching markets," arXiv:1907.04485, 2019 (also in this survey batch as a Related-priority paper) — cited for a similar simplifying exhaustive-evaluation assumption in an assortment-planning (not ranking) setting. Gale and Shapley, "College admissions and the stability of marriage," The American Mathematical Monthly, 1962 — canonical stable-matching reference, explicitly contrasted: this paper's ranking approach leaves participants full autonomy over final decisions rather than forcing a centralized matching outcome. Roth and Peranson, "The redesign of the matching market for American physicians," American Economic Review, 1999 — centralized matching procedure, contrasted as fundamentally different from this paper's decentralized, autonomy-preserving approach. Joachims, Swaminathan, and Schnabel, "Unbiased learning-to-rank with biased feedback," WSDM 2017 — source of the v(x)=1/x examination-function convention used throughout. Singh and Joachims, "Fairness of exposure in rankings," KDD 2018, and "Policy learning for fairness in ranking," NeurIPS 2019 — cited as candidate extensions for incorporating explicit fairness/exposure constraints into the optimization framework, flagged as future work. Pizzato, Chung, Rej, Koprinska, Yacef, and Kay and related work — the reciprocal-recommendation literature this paper's setting generalizes beyond (harmonic mean, sum-of-similarities, and other hand-crafted reciprocal scoring heuristics that ignore cross-candidate collision dependencies).

## Project Relevance

This paper is the batch's strongest match to **Q7** (two-sided/reciprocal market, congestion for a shared limited resource) because its lever — a personalized ranking per candidate, jointly optimized across the whole market — is exactly the project's lever, and its formal Θ(n) sub-optimality result gives a rigorous argument for why congestion-blind ranking underperforms specifically in a reciprocal, capacity-limited market like dating. It also bears on **Q4** in spirit: Social-Welfare Ranking is a single objective that supersedes ad hoc "reciprocal score" heuristics (harmonic mean, product of scores), which is analogous to the project's move from a blended CTR/CVR + uplift score to one unified objective, though the unification here is over match probability, not retention/revenue. It does **not** address **Q1, Q2, Q3, Q5, Q6, or Q8**: there is no learned retention/revenue label, no delayed outcome, no credit-assignment problem beyond a single match event, no incrementality treatment (relevance probabilities are assumed given, not estimated as causal effects of exposure), and no online evaluation or migration path from an existing production system. Its two-sided relevance probabilities being assumed accurate and given is the paper's central simplifying assumption and the main gap versus the project's actual problem, where those probabilities must themselves be learned, are delayed, and are confounded with base activity.

Horizon verdict: none — static (single-round expected-match-count objective; no repeated-interaction or retention/revenue horizon modeled).
Lever verdict: the ranking itself (a personalized stochastic ranking policy per candidate, jointly optimized across the market) — this is directly the project's lever and is in fact the paper's own optimization variable, making it the most usable paper in this batch for a ranking-only platform.
