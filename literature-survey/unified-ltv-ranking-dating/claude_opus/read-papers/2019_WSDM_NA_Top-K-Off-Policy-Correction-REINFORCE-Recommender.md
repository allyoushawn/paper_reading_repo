# Paper Analysis: Top-K Off-Policy Correction for a REINFORCE Recommender System

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02353 (arXiv:1812.02353; published as Chen et al., WSDM 2019)
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-16

## 1. Summary

**Title:** Top-K Off-Policy Correction for a REINFORCE Recommender System
**Authors:** Minmin Chen, Alex Beutel, Paul Covington, Sagar Jain, Francois Belletti, Ed H. Chi (Google, Inc.)
**Venue:** WSDM 2019 (Melbourne, VIC, Australia)

**Abstract (paraphrased from source):** Industrial recommender systems must choose from action spaces of many millions of items while serving billions of users, each with a complex, evolving state. Large quantities of logged implicit feedback (clicks, dwell time) are available, but learning from that feedback is biased because it reflects only the recommendations selected by previous versions of the recommender. The paper presents a recipe for addressing this bias in a production top-K recommender at YouTube built on the REINFORCE policy-gradient algorithm.

**Key contributions:**
1. Scaling REINFORCE to a production recommender system with an action space on the order of millions of items.
2. Applying off-policy correction to address data bias from learning on logged feedback collected under multiple historical behavior policies.
3. A novel top-K off-policy correction that accounts for the recommender presenting multiple items (a slate) at once, rather than a single item.
4. Demonstrating the value of stochastic (Boltzmann) exploration in a live, massive-scale production system.

**Methodology:** The recommendation problem is cast as a Markov Decision Process (S, A, P, R, ρ₀, γ) with a continuous user-state space S and a discrete, million-scale item action space A. The user state is encoded with a Chaos Free RNN (CFN) over the user's historical interactions; the policy π_θ(a|s) is a softmax over item embeddings. Because the learner trains on logged data generated by a mixture of historical policies rather than the current policy, the paper applies importance-weighted off-policy correction, using a first-order approximation of the chained importance weight to control variance. A second, context-dependent neural network β̂_θ'(a|s) (sharing the RNN state via a gradient-blocked connection) estimates the historical behavior policy needed for this correction. For the multi-item slate setting, the paper derives a top-K correction: it re-derives the gradient in terms of α_θ(a|s) = 1 − (1 − π_θ(a|s))^K, the probability that item a appears in the final non-repetitive top-K set, yielding a multiplier λ_K(s,a) = K(1 − π_θ(a|s))^(K−1) that aggressively pushes up the likelihood of under-weighted good items while zeroing out further gradient once an item's probability is high enough to reliably make the slate — preventing the "rich-get-richer" collapse onto a single top item that standard (K=1) off-policy correction produces. Variance is additionally controlled via weight capping (ω̄_c(s,a) = min(π(a|s)/β(a|s), c)); the paper tested but did not adopt normalized importance sampling or TRPO for further gains. Exploration is handled via Boltzmann sampling from the softmax rather than brute-force ε-greedy, using approximate nearest-neighbor lookup to keep sampling tractable at million-item scale.

**Main results:** The approach was evaluated in two stateless 10-item simulations (verifying that off-policy correction recovers the optimal policy under a skewed behavior policy, and that top-K correction retains probability mass on the second-best item rather than collapsing to a single winner) and in a sequence of live A/B experiments on YouTube's Homepage and Watch Next surfaces, measured primarily on ViewTime (total time users spent watching recommended videos) on the YouTube platform.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design.** The paper combines two small, fully controlled synthetic simulations (10 items, stateless) that isolate the correction mechanism from confounding real-world noise, with a sequence of live, full-traffic A/B tests on YouTube's Homepage and Watch Next candidate generators. The live tests are staged: first isolating the value of stochastic exploration (5% exploration bucket vs. 90%/5% deterministic), then off-policy correction, then top-K correction, then a hyperparameter sweep (K ∈ {1,2,8,16,32}; capping c ∈ {e³, e⁵}) run after top-K correction was already the production model.

**Statistical validity.** The paper reports several live results as "statistically significant" (e.g., +0.53% unique videos watched on YouTube live traffic for standard off-policy correction, and +0.85% ViewTime on YouTube live traffic for top-K correction) but does not consistently report confidence intervals for the live percentage changes in the extracted text, in contrast to the more rigorous CI reporting later adopted by the companion paper (Chen et al., User Response Models to Improve a REINFORCE Recommender System, WSDM 2021). The simulation results are illustrative (policy-visualization figures) rather than statistically tested.

**Online experiments.** This is the paper's strongest evidence: the paper explicitly notes that live experimentation is "rarely done in existing RL literature," and it reports a full staged sequence of production A/B tests on YouTube — exploration, standard off-policy correction, top-K correction, and a post-launch hyperparameter sweep — rather than offline-only validation.

**Reproducibility.** The core algorithm (MDP formulation, CFN state update, top-K correction derivation, weight-capping) is fully specified with equations. However, the production RNN candidate generator, the YouTube traffic, and the reward function's exact activity weighting are proprietary and not released, so the live-experiment numbers cannot be independently reproduced outside Google's infrastructure.

**Overall.** The paper's claims are well supported for the specific comparisons it runs (top-K vs. standard correction; K=16 vs. other K; capping c=e³ vs. e⁵), each backed by a live A/B test on YouTube's production traffic. The weakest link is the lack of uniformly reported confidence intervals for the live percentage deltas, and the fact that the top-K vs. standard-correction slate-quality story is demonstrated rigorously only in the small synthetic simulation, not measured directly (e.g., via a slate-diversity metric) in the live system.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability.** Every design choice in the paper is aimed at production feasibility at web scale: sampled softmax during training and approximate nearest-neighbor search at serving avoid computing the full million-item softmax; the behavior-policy head reuses the main policy's RNN state (with a gradient block) rather than adding a second expensive encoder; Boltzmann exploration is implemented via top-M nearest-neighbor retrieval followed by a small re-normalized softmax, keeping exploration compatible with low-latency serving.

**Problems solved.** The paper solves two concrete industrial ranking-pipeline problems: (1) learning stably from logged feedback that was generated by a mixture of past, unknown behavior policies (multiple historical model versions and ensembled candidate sources), and (2) correcting a page-level/slate ranking policy that a naive single-item off-policy correction would collapse into recommending only the single best-known item.

**Engineering cost.** The added engineering surface is a second, gradient-blocked softmax head (behavior-policy estimator) trained jointly with the main policy, a hyperparameter surface (slate size K, importance-weight cap c, exploration temperature T) that required a dedicated post-launch sweep to tune in production, and a continuous training pipeline that ingests new events with under-24-hour lag rather than a batch-retrained model. No new serving-time model is required beyond the existing RNN candidate generator — the top-K correction changes only the training-time gradient, not the serving-time architecture.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty.** The authors claim four contributions: scaling REINFORCE to a production, million-item action space; applying off-policy correction using a learned neural behavior-policy model (rather than a context-independent frequency estimate); a novel top-K off-policy correction for the slate-recommendation setting; and live-experiment validation of exploration value, which the paper notes is uncommon in the RL literature.

**Prior work named in the source (Query 2, part 3):**
- Williams, "Simple statistical gradient-following algorithms for connectionist reinforcement learning," 1992 — the foundational REINFORCE algorithm this paper scales up.
- Strehl et al., "Learning from logged implicit exploration data," 2010 — the starting point for estimating a behavior policy from logged data, which this paper extends from a context-independent frequency estimate to a context-dependent neural estimator.
- Schulman et al., "Trust region policy optimization," 2015 — cited both as a proximal-update technique from robotics and as a rejected variance-reduction alternative (TRPO gave no further gain over weight capping in this paper's experiments).
- Covington et al., "Deep neural networks for YouTube recommendations," 2016 — the production candidate-generation and ranking architecture this system builds on.
- Swaminathan and Joachims, works on batch learning from logged bandit feedback and off-policy evaluation for slate recommendation, 2015/2017 — cited for propensity overfitting and slate off-policy evaluation.
- Joachims et al., "Unbiased learning-to-rank with biased feedback," 2017 — cited for constructing unbiased ranking systems from logged feedback.
- Achiam et al., "Constrained policy optimization," 2017 — cited for the theoretical error bound on the first-order importance-weight approximation this paper uses.

## Project Relevance

This is a named seed reference for the survey. Its central technical contribution — a learned, context-dependent neural estimator of the historical behavior policy, used to reweight training gradients — is a direct candidate answer to **Q2** (attributing outcomes to item-level decisions under a changing policy) and to the batch-level question of where incrementality-adjacent correction can live inside a ranker: this paper shows a concrete mechanism for the gap between the logging policy (today's CTR/CVR-plus-uplift blend) and the target policy (the future unified retention/revenue model), which the unified model would need if trained on logs collected under the current system. It also bears on **Q4** (only a single reward stream is fused here — there is no separate short-term/long-term head combination to compare against) and **Q3** (the 4–10 hour reward horizon is far shorter than the dating app's 7–30 day retention horizon, so the horizon choice itself does not transfer, though the mechanism for handling any horizon's logged-data bias does). It does **not** address **Q5** (prediction vs. incrementality) — the paper optimizes an engagement proxy (ViewTime), not a causal effect of exposure — nor **Q7** (two-sided markets, congestion, reciprocity), which is absent throughout.
