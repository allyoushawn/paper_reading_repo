# Paper Analysis: A/B Testing for Recommender Systems in a Two-sided Marketplace

**Source:** NeurIPS 2021 (35th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems; arXiv:2106.00762), LinkedIn Corporation. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.00762
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-16

## 1. Summary

Preetam Nandy, Divya Venugopalan, Chun Lo, and Shaunak Chatterjee (LinkedIn) address producer-side (seller/content-creator/candidate) measurement in two-sided marketplace recommender systems. Consumer-side A/B testing is straightforward and SUTVA-compliant, but producer-side measurement is not: a producer's experience depends on the treatment assignment of every consumer connected to it in the bipartite consumer-producer graph, and it is structurally impossible to give a producer's connected consumers all the same treatment when that graph is dense. Existing fixes are graph-cluster randomization (loses statistical power as network density increases) or treatment-propagation models (require strong, often unverifiable, assumptions about how effects propagate, and lack strict error control); both typically require knowing the network structure a priori, which is unrealistic for dynamic production graphs.

The paper's contributions are: (1) a formal "design inaccuracy" quality metric — the squared error between an experiment design's realized rank for an item and the ideal, typically unrealizable, counterfactual rank that item would get if its producer's assigned treatment were applied to the whole consumer session; (2) UniCoRn (Unifying Counterfactual Rankings), an algorithm that blends control and treatment counterfactual rankings at the session level to approximate that ideal ranking; (3) a proof that UniCoRn is mathematically optimal with respect to the design-inaccuracy metric at its tuning parameter α=1 (Theorem 1), with accompanying bias/variance bounds (Theorem 2); (4) a tunable cost/accuracy trade-off via α∈[0,1] — α=0 rescores only treatment-assigned items with zero added scoring latency, α=1 rescores every mixed item for maximum accuracy at the highest computational cost; (5) network-structure-agnostic operation — no prior knowledge of the consumer-producer graph is required, unlike cluster or propagation-based alternatives; and (6) a production deployment in LinkedIn's edge (member-connection) recommender, serving tens of millions of members and billions of recommendations daily.

Methodology: for disjoint producer sets P0 (control) and P1 (treatment) and a session-level candidate set I_s, the ideal (unrealizable) rank R*(i,I_s) equals the counterfactual rank item i would get under its own producer's assigned treatment. Algorithm 1 (UniCoRn(P0,P1,α)), for each session: ranks all items by the control model T0; samples a subset of control producers P0* with probability α; identifies the rank slots occupied by the union of treatment items and the sampled control items; computes separate relative rankings of that mixing pool under T0 and T1; blends them into a rank_score and re-ranks the mixing pool into the recorded slots, leaving non-sampled control items locked at their original T0 rank. Evaluation combines (a) synthetic simulation — bivariate-Gaussian score pairs at four correlation levels across 50,000 sessions of 100 items, and a Beta(2,5)-quality producer simulation with 1,000 producers over 1,000 sessions — comparing UniCoRn(α) for α∈{0, 0.2, 1} against two baselines (OASIS, a score-based network-experiment method, and HaThucEtAl, a small-ramp-only counterfactual method), and (b) two live production A/B tests on LinkedIn's edge recommender at 40% viewer-side traffic: a candidate-generation change (normalized vs. raw shared-edges heuristic) and a ranking-model change (boosting candidates likely to accept a connection invitation), both measured with UniCoRn(0) to avoid added serving latency.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design.** Two complementary evaluation tracks: (a) synthetic simulation isolating design-inaccuracy (MAE/RMSE) and ATE-estimation-error trade-offs against α and treatment proportion, run against two named baselines; (b) a live production deployment with two separate online experiments. This is a stronger evidentiary design than the blog-post papers in this batch and than the purely theoretical companion paper in this batch (Li, Johari, Zhao, and Weintraub, WWW 2022), because it pairs formal proofs, controlled synthetic comparison, and a real online deployment.

**Statistical validity.** Theoretical optimality is proved (Theorem 1) with tight bias/variance bounds (Theorem 2, shown to be achievable in an adversarial case). The live production results report p<0.001 for all four reported metrics (Weekly Active Unique users and Sessions deltas, for both the candidate-generation and ranking-model experiments), a genuine significance claim, though no confidence intervals are given in the extracted text. The authors explicitly note they could not run a 100% viewer-side ramp due to other parallel experiments running concurrently, so the reported 40%-viewer-traffic effect sizes are stated to be an underestimate of the true full-ramp treatment effect.

**Online experiments.** Yes — the strongest online-validated paper in this batch. UniCoRn(0) (α=0, chosen specifically to add zero measurable serving latency) was deployed in LinkedIn's production Java serving pipeline; the paper states this added "no statistically significant serving latency."

**Reproducibility.** Full algorithm pseudocode (Algorithm 1) and all key definitions and theorems, with appendix proofs, are given; simulation code is referenced as available in supplementary material. The production deployment is not independently reproducible (internal LinkedIn data and infrastructure), but the method itself is fully specified and re-implementable from the paper alone.

**Overall.** The best-evidenced paper in this batch — theory, offline simulation against two named baselines, and a real online deployment with significance testing. The authors themselves flag a real limitation: UniCoRn cannot capture a treatment's effect on a viewer's own overall attention or session behavior, since every viewer sees a blend of treatment and control rankings by design (see Reference Card field 13). The offline ATE-estimation comparisons also rely on synthetic, not real, producer/consumer data.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability.** Proven in production: implemented in Java inside LinkedIn's real-time distributed serving pipeline for edge (connection) recommendations, tuned to α=0 specifically to avoid adding scoring latency, and serving tens of millions of members and billions of recommendations per day. This is a directly deployable pattern for any existing ranking pipeline: score with the control model, identify the rank slots occupied by treatment-eligible items, rescore only those items with the treatment model, and merge.

**Problems solved.** Gives platforms a way to run producer-side (in a dating context, candidate-side / viewee-side) A/B tests without knowing or clustering the consumer-producer graph, and without the variance penalty of cluster or switchback designs — directly relevant to measuring the effect of a ranking change on the "shown" side of a marketplace, not just the "viewing" side.

**Engineering cost.** Explicitly tunable: α=0 adds no rescoring cost (only treatment-assigned candidates are scored by the treatment model) at the cost of higher design inaccuracy; α=1 rescores every mixed item, maximizing accuracy at the highest computational cost. The paper also gives a direct extension for two-phase (candidate generation + ranking) pipelines (Section 4.3 / Appendix A.5) — the typical architecture of large-scale recommenders, including a CTR/CVR-plus-uplift-blend dating ranker.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty.** The first producer-side experiment design that is (i) provably optimal against an explicit design-inaccuracy metric, (ii) free of assumptions about how treatment effects propagate through the network, (iii) free of any requirement to know the consumer-producer graph structure in advance, and (iv) usable at arbitrary ramp sizes rather than being restricted to small treatment/control fractions.

**Prior work.** Ha-Thuc, Dutta, Mao, Wood, and Liu, "A counterfactual framework for seller-side A/B testing on marketplaces," *SIGIR* 2020 ("HaThucEtAl") — the direct precursor this paper generalizes, restricted to small (10%/10%) ramps and used as a simulation baseline. Nandy, Basu, Chatterjee, and Tu, "A/B testing in dense large-scale networks: Design and inference," *NeurIPS* 2020 ("OASIS") — a score-based network-experiment design, used as a second simulation baseline (re-implemented as a score-based counterpart of UniCoRn). Rubin, "Estimating causal effects of treatments in randomized and nonrandomized studies" (1974), "Bayesian inference for causal effects" (1978), and "Formal mode of statistical inference for causal effects" (1990) — foundational SUTVA/potential-outcomes references. Ugander, Karrer, Backstrom, and Kleinberg, "Graph cluster randomization: Network exposure to multiple universes," *KDD* 2013, and Saint-Jacques, Varshney, Simpson, and Xu, "Using ego-clusters to measure network effects at LinkedIn," arXiv:1903.08755 (2019) — the graph-cluster-randomization alternative. Pouget-Abadie, Aydin, Schudy, Brodersen, and Mirrokni, "Variance reduction in bipartite experiments through correlation clustering," *NeurIPS* 2019 — a variance-reduction alternative for bipartite experiments. Basse, Soufiani, and Lambert, "Randomization and the pernicious effects of limited budgets on auction experiments," *AISTATS* 2016, and Liu, Mao, and Kang, "Trustworthy online marketplace experimentation with budget-split design," arXiv:2012.08724 (2020) — budget-split designs for resource-constrained (e.g., advertising) marketplaces. Kohavi, Deng, Longbotham, and Xu, "Seven rules of thumb for web site experimenters," *KDD* 2014, and Kohavi and Longbotham, "Online controlled experiments and A/B testing" (2017) — standard industry A/B-testing references.

**Platform recommendation (what the paper says to actually do).** Use rank-based blending (UniCoRn) rather than score-based methods (OASIS) — the paper's simulations show a large ATE-estimation-error reduction from doing so. Set α=0 for latency-sensitive, real-time serving (zero added scoring cost); reserve α=1, or an intermediate α=0.2, for offline scoring or latency-insensitive applications, and specifically when the treatment proportion is small (α=0.2 is needed to match α=1's accuracy at a 10% treatment proportion, while α=0 already suffices at 50%). Extend the two-set (P0/P1) design to multi-phase (candidate generation + ranking) pipelines via the paper's Appendix A.5 extension when the production system has a separate candidate-generation stage.

## Project Relevance

This is the batch's second direct answer to the survey's **Q6**, and the strongest one specifically for the candidate/viewee-side half of the two-sided evaluation problem: the companion paper in this batch (Li, Johari, Zhao, and Weintraub, WWW 2022) covers the general CR-vs-LR bias trade-off, while this paper gives a concrete, production-proven algorithm for measuring the effect on the shown side without the network-clustering bias/variance trade-off that afflicts graph-cluster designs. It also speaks to **Q7** (fairness/exposure across two sides): UniCoRn's design explicitly guarantees "no cannibalization" — a randomly chosen item from the control set and from the treatment set receive the same rank distribution — a concrete mechanism for balancing exposure across a candidate-side split, directly relevant to congestion and fair-exposure concerns in a reciprocal dating market. Its stated limitation — that it "would fail to capture" a treatment's effect on a viewer's own total session time or frequency — is an important caveat for the survey's retention objective (**Q1/Q3**): a candidate-side UniCoRn deployment alone would not measure whether the viewer stayed longer, only whether candidates' exposure or engagement changed; a dating-app deployment would need a companion, standard SUTVA-compliant viewer-side A/B arm to close that gap. The paper does not address **Q1–Q5 or Q8** as ML-training questions: there is no retention/revenue training objective, no delayed label, no incrementality framing beyond ATE-of-ranking-position, and no migration-path discussion.
