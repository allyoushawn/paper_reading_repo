# Paper Analysis: Trustworthy Online Marketplace Experimentation with Budget-split Design

**Source:** `/Users/fox/Projects/paper_reading_repo/literature-survey/unified-ltv-ranking-dating/claude_opus/pdfs/2012.08724.pdf` (arXiv:2012.08724v1)
**Date analyzed:** 2026-08-17

## 1. Summary

Min Liu, Jialiang Mao, and Kang Kang (LinkedIn Corporation) address experimentation in an online ads marketplace (OAM), where buyers (advertiser campaigns, each with a finite budget B_j) compete for the attention of sellers (members). They formalize a potential-outcomes framework at the (member, campaign) pair level, Y_ij(W;B), with the identity that a campaign cannot deliver more value than its budget allows (Σ_{i∈I_j} Y*_ij(W;B) ≤ B_j). This finite-budget constraint is the source of a "cannibalization bias": if a treatment (e.g., a better matching algorithm) makes some members more attractive to a campaign, the campaign's own pacing/bidding logic reallocates its fixed budget toward those (typically treatment-group) members and away from others, so naive Bernoulli-randomized A/B tests measure treatment sales as higher not because total delivered value grew, but because it was redistributed away from the control group — a SUTVA violation that the paper proves is always non-negative in the two worked examples given (Examples 1–2), meaning naive estimation systematically **overestimates** the true treatment effect under budget constraints.

The proposed fix, the **budget-split design**, randomly splits the entire member population into two disjoint buckets, proportionally splits every campaign's budget between the two buckets (B_j^(0)=N^(0)/N·B_j, B_j^(1)=N^(1)/N·B_j), and constructs two fully independent, complete replica marketplaces (M^(0), M^(1)) — each with the same set of campaigns (at proportionally reduced budget) but disjoint members. One replica is randomly designated global-treatment, the other global-control, and the treatment effect is estimated directly by comparing outcomes between the two self-contained replicas (equation 3.4), with **no cross-arm budget competition at all**, since each replica owns its own dedicated budget slice. Theorem 1 proves this estimator is unbiased under two assumptions: limited interference within the restricted target population (Assumption 2) and a "stable system" condition (Assumption 3) — that a proportionally-restricted, K-member version of a campaign behaves similarly to the original N-member campaign, which the authors argue holds once K exceeds some burn-in threshold K₀, typically satisfiable when N is at the scale of millions. The design is deployed at two real LinkedIn marketplaces, showing large power gains over campaign-level and switchback experiments and near-elimination of cannibalization bias relative to naive member-level experiments.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design.** Two worked analytical examples (Example 1: full-budget-utilization case; Example 2: universally-beneficial-treatment case, with a super-population illustration in Supplementary Figure S6) establish the direction of cannibalization bias, followed by a formal unbiasedness proof (Theorem 1, proof in Appendix B) under two stated assumptions, followed by two real production deployments at LinkedIn (Section 5) comparing budget-split against campaign-level and switchback ("alternating-day") baselines on statistical power, and against naive member-level experiments on cannibalization-bias magnitude.

**Statistical validity.** Power curves are computed using two-sample t-tests for budget-split and campaign-level experiments, but **paired permutation tests** for switchback experiments specifically "to boost the power" of that weaker baseline — a methodological choice that, if anything, is generous to the weakest comparator rather than to the paper's own design, worth noting as a modest positive caveat. No formal variance/asymptotic theory is derived for the budget-split estimator itself; the authors explicitly list "accurately compute the variance estimators under the budget-split design" as unresolved future work (Section 6).

**Online experiments.** Yes — the paper's strongest evidence. Budget-split experimentation was deployed to two real LinkedIn online marketplaces (not individually named), where power curves (Figures 3, 4) and cannibalization-bias magnitude (Figure 5, across three distinct real treatments — bias correction, tracking improvement, training data improvement) are reported directly from production data.

**Reproducibility.** The estimator, assumptions, and Theorem 1's proof (Appendix B) are fully specified and reproducible from the paper text alone. The production power curves and bias-magnitude figures are not independently reproducible (internal LinkedIn data and infrastructure), and the two marketplaces are never named or scaled (no member/campaign counts given for the empirical deployment, unlike the numeric examples earlier in the paper).

**Overall.** A well-evidenced design paper — theory plus two live production deployments — but the honest limitations are real: Assumption 3 (stable system) is argued plausible rather than empirically verified in the reported deployments, and the paper explicitly leaves variance estimation, discrete-budget extensions, and multi-experiment throughput as open problems.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability.** Proven in production at two LinkedIn marketplaces. Section 4 gives a concrete system-architecture blueprint: the ad server's request-handling tier randomizes each member into a bucket at the member-ID level; the tracker service records bucket assignment alongside campaign ID; and the pacing/bidding module operates at a campaign-**bucket** level rather than a plain campaign level (separate budget, separate real-time spend/performance feedback, separate auction signals per bucket) — effectively making every treatment "own a standalone marketplace." The authors state this requires only incremental changes to existing modules, not an infrastructure overhaul, and that the same blueprint generalizes to other marketplaces with a well-defined buyer budget (e.g., hiring marketplaces) and, per a cited blog post (Greg Novak and Spiegel, 2020), extends with little modification to an "inventory-split" counterpart for seller-inventory-constrained (rather than buyer-budget-constrained) marketplaces.

**Problems solved.** Removes cannibalization bias without requiring a parametric model of the interference structure (unlike the paper's "analysis approach" alternatives, e.g. Johari et al. 2022, Blake and Coey 2014) and without the switchback design's carryover-effect and long-runtime problems, which the paper argues make switchback experiments impractical for slow-to-measure features and create a validity-versus-efficiency tension as the experiment period lengthens.

**Engineering cost.** Real and non-trivial: every campaign must effectively be split into two proportionally-budgeted sub-campaigns running simultaneously (a 2x increase in internal campaign count for the duration of the experiment), and Assumption 3's validity depends on member population size N being large enough (the paper suggests "at least the scale of millions" for typical OAM heterogeneity) to keep each half-sized replica marketplace behaving like the original — a real constraint for any smaller or more homogeneous candidate pool.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

**Claimed novelty.** The first marketplace-experimentation design proven unbiased for *any* marketplace where buyers have a well-defined continuous budget (finite or infinite), requiring only that single easy-to-validate assumption rather than a parametric interference model; reported "up to 30 times more powerful" than alternative designs when deployed in real marketplaces (abstract claim; see Reference Card field 13 for a caveat on this specific number); and deployable via incremental infrastructure changes rather than a platform overhaul.

**Prior work.** Basse, Soufiani, and Lambert, "Randomization and the pernicious effects of limited budgets on auction experiments," AISTATS 2016 — the closest prior design, explicitly contrasted: Basse et al. give each buyer an *indirect, artificial* budget (quota), whereas budget-split uses the buyer's own *direct, natural* budget. Ha-Thuc, Dutta, Mao, Wood, and Liu, "A counterfactual framework for seller-side A/B testing on marketplaces," SIGIR 2020 (= counterfactual interleaving, this batch's sibling problem on the seller side, cited by Nandy et al. 2021 as a related design though not directly benchmarked in this paper). Bojinov and Shephard, "Time series experiments and causal estimands," JASA 2019, and the "alternating-day" switchback baseline used directly as a comparison point in the production power study. Johari, Li, and Weintraub, "Experimental design in two-sided platforms: An analysis of bias," Management Science 2022, and Blake and Coey, "Why marketplace experimentation is harder than it seems," EC 2014 — representative "analysis approach" alternatives requiring a parametric interference model, contrasted unfavorably with the paper's assumption-light "design approach." Saveski, Pouget-Abadie, Saint-Jacques, Duan, Ghosh, Xu, and Airoldi, "Detecting network effects: Randomizing over randomized experiments," KDD 2017 — cluster-based randomization, cited as a lower-power alternative in Section 5.2. Doudchenko, Zhang, Drynkin, Airoldi, Mirrokni, and Pouget-Abadie, "Causal inference with bipartite designs," arXiv:2010.02108 (2020), and Pouget-Abadie, Aydin, Schudy, Brodersen, and Mirrokni, "Variance reduction in bipartite experiments through correlation clustering," NeurIPS 2019 — the bipartite-experiment framing this paper's OAM setup is cast within.

## Project Relevance

Speaks to **Q6**, specifically the congestion/interference problem this survey names as central to the project: the budget-split design is a general template for turning any shared, finite, allocatable resource (here, a campaign's ad budget; in the project, a candidate's attention or daily like/match capacity) into two independent, non-competing replica sub-populations for unbiased marketplace-wide A/B testing. It also bears directly on **Q7** (congestion for a shared limited resource across a two-sided/reciprocal market): the paper's finite-budget framing is structurally the same shape as the project's stated congestion constraint (B's attention and likes are a shared, limited resource across many viewers A), making this the batch's most structurally transferable design for testing changes to the candidate-facing side of a unified ranking model. It does not address Q1–Q5 or Q8: there is no ML training objective, no delayed-label handling, and no discussion of migrating from a CTR/CVR-plus-uplift blend to a unified model — it is purely an evaluation-design contribution.
