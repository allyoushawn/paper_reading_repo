## 1. Summary

**Title:** Long-term User Engagement Optimization through Model-agnostic Downstream Rewards Learning

**Authors:** Dingsu Wang, Aditya Mantha, Olafur Gudmundsson, Filip Ryzner, Liyao Lu, Matt Chun, Kelly He, Usha Amrutha Nookala, Krystal Benitez, Armando Ordorica, Haoran Guo, Dhruvil Deven Badani, David Woo, Jiacong He, Yijie Dylan Wang (Pinterest)

**Abstract (paraphrased):** Directly optimizing retention is difficult because return signals are sparse, delayed, and only partially attributable to specific recommendations. Prior sequential-modeling and RL approaches require heavy reward engineering, high computational overhead, and surface-specific implementations that generalize poorly. The paper presents a unified, model-agnostic **downstream reward (DR)** framework: it formulates the downstream-reward-learning problem and develops an offline screening framework to identify session-level behaviors that are both observable early and predictive of future retention. It proposes several model-agnostic downstream reward signals derived from observed engagement patterns across multiple sources, discusses the engineering effort to productionize these rewards by adding them to existing ranking models, and reports online A/B experiments showing consistent engagement and retention gains; the framework has been deployed across Homefeed, Related Pins, Search, and Notifications at Pinterest.

**Key contributions:** (1) an offline evaluation framework that screens candidate session-level signals against three criteria — indicative of long-term retention, observable at the session level, and incrementally predictive in a multivariate model; (2) a unified, model-agnostic downstream-reward framework (deeper-session-engagement rewards, negative rewards, use-case-adoption rewards) that plugs into different deep-learning ranking models across surfaces; (3) an efficient reward-derivation infrastructure (DRv2, Ray-based) that computes reward labels on demand from shared daily engagement sequences, replacing a slower precomputed-table approach; (4) empirical validation via long-running online A/B tests at Pinterest.

**Methodology:** The paper defines the optimal user retention objective as a discounted sum of a conditional retention reward over a policy's induced trajectory (Definition 2.1), then defines a tractable proxy — the downstream reward (DR) — as the expected surrogate reward over a predicted or observed short trajectory of downstream engagement actions (e.g., a "P2P," Pin-to-Pin, rabbit-hole trajectory) following a recommendation (Definition 2.2). Three reward families are proposed: (1) rewards for deeper session engagement (discounted cumulative reward over downstream engagement actions — closeups, saves, downloads, screenshots — within the same session trajectory); (2) negative rewards (a penalty for "shallow closeups," i.e., very brief engagements empirically associated with poor downstream outcomes and higher session-abandonment probability, with cohort-specific dwell-time thresholds); (3) use-case-adoption rewards (a reward for engaging with content outside a user's established interest clusters, encouraging exploration). Candidate reward signals are first screened offline using a Random Forest classifier (trained on hundreds of thousands of "pivot-day" user-week examples) that ranks signals by univariate correlation, feature importance, and SHAP values against future engagement transitions; a session-level Markov-chain analysis separately corroborates that deeper session actions precede longer sessions, which precede faster revisits, which precede higher retention. The screened rewards are then added to a target ranking model as an **auxiliary prediction head**, with the final ranking score a linear combination of the (unchanged) existing per-head predictions and the new DR-head predictions; head weights are tuned outside the ranker via HyperOpt to keep immediate engagement neutral while improving retention-related metrics.

**Main results:** Online A/B tests on Pinterest Homefeed (1.5% traffic, 3–4 weeks per test) show: deeper-session-engagement rewards deliver +0.36% site-wide Successful Sessions and +0.1% WAU; negative (shallow-closeup) rewards, after per-cohort threshold tuning, deliver +0.16% Successful Sessions and −0.40% unsuccessful sessions; use-case-adoption rewards deliver +0.10%/+0.18% Successful Sessions/WAU-with-2+-use-cases and +1.28% Pin downloads. Extending the deeper-session-engagement reward to other rankers yields +0.25% Search Fulfillment Rate, +0.15% Related Pins Successful Sessions, and +0.14% Notifications Successful Sessions.

## 2. Experiment Critique

**Design:** The Random Forest screening model (AUC 0.65–0.70 on held-out data) is used only to *rank candidate reward signals* by feature importance/SHAP — not to serve predictions — so its moderate discriminative power is an acceptable design choice for a screening tool, though it does mean the ranking of "which signal to try online next" carries real uncertainty. The session-level Markov-chain analysis (expected feedviews before session termination, by starting surface-action state) is explicitly labeled by the authors as "observational rather than causal."

**Statistical validity:** The pivot-day-behavior screening uses a t-test on median time-to-next-revisit across nine session-duration thresholds, reporting p < 10⁻⁴³ for all thresholds — a very large sample, high-confidence correlational result, but still correlational as the authors themselves note.

**Online experiments:** Multiple real production A/B tests at Pinterest, each 3–4 weeks, 1.5% of Homefeed traffic, reported separately for core vs. non-core users (Table 3) — a genuine strength, and the paper is candid that a first negative-reward launch (uniform threshold) hurt retention/engagement for non-core users due to segment heterogeneity, requiring a second, cohort-specific-threshold launch to fix.

**Reproducibility:** Not reproducible outside Pinterest. The paper explicitly states it does not evaluate on public datasets because "the evaluation of public dataset is limited given they do not necessarily reflect the actual online engagement patterns" and because the reward signals require Pinterest-specific action types (P2P rabbit-hole trajectories, closeups) not present in public benchmarks.

## 3. Industry Contribution

**Deployability:** High — this is the paper's central claim, and it is the most directly relevant migration-path candidate in the batch. The DR reward heads are explicitly designed to sit **on top of** an existing ranking model without replacing it: "The rewards derivation infrastructure... downstream reward framework... can be applied to different deep learning based recommendation models across surfaces." What stays unchanged: the underlying ranking/recommendation model architecture itself (whatever it is on a given surface — e.g., the existing Pinnability multi-task model on Homefeed), and the existing immediate-engagement heads' predictions and weights. What is added: (a) an offline screening pipeline (Random Forest + Markov analysis) to select which session-level signals to use as reward labels; (b) new reward-label definitions (deeper-session, negative, use-case-adoption); (c) reward-derivation infrastructure (DRv2, a Ray-based dataloader that computes DR labels on the fly from shared per-user daily engagement sequences, rather than precomputing and storing them); (d) one or more new auxiliary prediction heads on the existing ranker, whose output is linearly blended into the final score with separately tuned (HyperOpt-searched) weights.

**Problems solved:** Sidesteps the sparsity/delay problem of directly training on true long-horizon retention labels by substituting screened, near-term, session-level proxy rewards that are both denser and faster to observe, while offline-validating that those proxies actually correlate with retention before spending online-experimentation budget.

**Engineering cost:** Explicitly quantified: the DRv1→DRv2 infrastructure change reduced "idea-to-experiment time" for a new reward variant from "weeks to months" (Spark self-joins + full table recomputation) to "~3 weeks to ~2 days," while keeping incremental training cost within 5% of baseline step time. This is a meaningful, reported engineering-cost data point.

## 4. Novelty vs. Prior Work

The paper positions its downstream-reward framework as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, RL- and sequence-modeling-based retention approaches, explicitly citing Xue et al. (2023), *PreFRec: Recommender Systems with Human Preferences for Reinforcing Long-term User Engagement*, KDD 2023, and Xue et al. (2025), *AURO: Reinforcement Learning to Pace for Retention Optimization*, WWW 2025, as RL-based alternatives that require heavier reward engineering; Zou et al. (2019), *Reinforcement Learning to Optimize Long-term User Engagement*, KDD 2019, and Zhao et al. (2023), *User retention-oriented recommendation with decision transformer* (DT4IER), WWW 2023, as related sequence/RL retention work the paper claims does not transfer directly to Pinterest's visual, save/download-centric interaction patterns; and Wang et al. (2022), *Surrogate for Long-Term User Experience in Recommender Systems*, KDD 2022, as the closest prior work in spirit (surrogate/proxy rewards for long-term experience), which the paper argues identified different, non-transferable behavior concepts (e.g., "high-quality consumption," "repeated consumption" defined by time spent) rather than Pinterest-specific save/download/P2P-rabbit-hole signals. It also cites Netflix's reward design work as similarly targeted at long-term satisfaction via a bandit-feedback setting, contrasting its own framework as more general and adaptable to non-bandit recommendation settings.

## Project Relevance

Speaks most directly to **Q8** (migration paths from "CTR model + uplift blend" to a unified model): this paper *is* an "auxiliary heads first" migration pattern, fully documented with production engineering cost and multi-surface deployment evidence — one of the strongest Q8 references in the survey. It also speaks to **Q3** (label/horizon definitions, via its offline screening methodology for choosing which near-term signal to use as a delayed-outcome proxy) and **Q1** (adding a retention-adjacent objective to an existing ranker without a full model rebuild). It does **not** address Q2 in a rigorous causal sense (its credit assignment is a discounted-trajectory heuristic, not a learned or causal attribution), Q5 (no uplift/incrementality machinery — explicitly observational), Q6 online-evaluation-under-interference (no discussion of two-sided interference), or Q7 (no two-sided/reciprocal-market considerations at all — Pinterest is one-sided content discovery).
